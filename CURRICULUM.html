The premise

Three things shape this curriculum, and all three came out of your answers.

You are past the fundamentals but not at the frontier. So the course does not teach you what a proof is or re-derive Gaussian elimination. It starts at polyhedra, spends one unit longer than feels necessary on duality — because duality is the single idea that cutting planes, column generation, Lagrangian relaxation, the Hungarian algorithm, primal–dual approximation and KKT are all special cases of — and then moves fast.

From scratch, then solvers. Each unit has you build the naive thing, then run the industrial thing on the same instance and measure the gap. This is the only honest way to learn why branch-and-cut has the shape it has: you write a branch-and-bound, watch CBC beat it by 400×, and then spend the next unit finding out which of its features was worth how much. The comparison is not decoration — it is the assessment.

No calendar, so this is a dependency graph. Units say what they need, not when they happen. There are three trunks descending from a common root — the polyhedral line (LP → IP), the combinatorial line (matroids, flows, matching, DP) and the constraint line (CP, SAT) — and they are genuinely independent for a long way. You can put one down for two months and pick up another. They converge only when you get to approximation, where you need all three.

What a unit is

Every unit is the same four things. A unit is done when the checkpoint is true — not when you have read the chapter.

Theory

The named results and algorithms, with the reading that covers them. Roughly the size of one book chapter.

Build

Python, from scratch, no optimization library. Correctness checked differentially against the brute-force oracle you write in unit 00.

Then

The same instances through HiGHS / Gurobi / OR-Tools / CaDiCaL. Record the gap. The gap is the lesson.

Checkpoint

A single falsifiable claim about what you can now do. If you cannot make it true, the unit is not finished — and you know precisely what is missing.

How long. Each unit's title slide gives its own estimate, typically 1½–2 h of slides and 2–3 h of lab. Summed over all units that is about 57 h of slides and 96–99 h of labs, the capstone's 12–15 h included: roughly 155 hours in all. Take it as a scale, not a schedule.

Part 0 — the ground floor

One unit, and you will use it in all thirty-one others

The single most common way self-study in this field goes wrong is having no way to tell whether the clever thing you just wrote is correct. Fix that first.

00

Models, instances, and an oracle you trust

spine

needs nothing

Theory
What makes a problem combinatorial: a finite feasible set described implicitly by constraints. Decision vs optimization vs counting. Certificates and why "here is the answer" and "here is why nothing is better" are different objects. The nine problems you will return to all course: knapsack, set cover, assignment, bin packing, graph colouring, max-cut, TSP, job-shop scheduling, vertex cover.
Build
An instance library — generators plus readers for the formats the field actually trades in (DIMACS .cnf, TSPLIB, MIPLIB .mps) — and a brute-force enumerator. Then a differential test harness: every algorithm you write from here on is run against the enumerator on small instances, and disagreement fails loudly with the offending instance printed.
Then
Nothing yet. This unit is the measuring stick, not a thing to be measured.
Checkpoint. You can generate a random instance of any of the nine problems, solve it exactly by enumeration up to n ≈ 20, and your harness catches a deliberately broken algorithm without you looking for it.

Part I — trunk A begins · linear programming and duality

The continuous shadow that every discrete algorithm reasons about

Nothing here is combinatorial yet. It is all load-bearing anyway: integer programming is the study of what happens when you take the LP relaxation seriously, and you cannot do that without knowing what the relaxation is.

01

Polyhedra, cones, and Farkas' lemma

spine

needs 00

Theory
Polyhedra as intersections of halfspaces; the equivalence of vertex, extreme point and basic feasible solution; Minkowski–Weyl (V- and H-representations); Fourier–Motzkin elimination and its blow-up; Farkas' lemma and the idea of an infeasibility certificate; the fundamental theorem of linear programming.
Build
A Fourier–Motzkin eliminator (and watch the constraint count explode). Vertex enumeration of a small polytope by brute-force basis enumeration — which ties straight back to unit 00's enumerator. A Farkas certificate checker.
Then
scipy.spatial.ConvexHull and pycddlib to cross-check your V↔H conversions.
Checkpoint. Given an infeasible system you can produce the Farkas vector and explain, without reference to any solver, why it constitutes a proof of infeasibility.
02

Simplex, honestly

spine

needs 01

Theory
The tableau and the revised simplex; pivoting as a walk on vertices; Dantzig's rule, Bland's anti-cycling rule, degeneracy and stalling; two-phase and big-M starts; Klee–Minty and the embarrassment of a worst-case-exponential algorithm that is the workhorse of the field; why real implementations use LU factorisation with periodic refactorisation and steepest-edge pricing.
Build
A dense tableau simplex with pluggable pricing. Reproduce Beale's cycling example and watch Bland's rule fix it. Write a Klee–Minty generator and plot pivot count against dimension — the exponential is visible by n = 12.
Then
scipy.optimize.linprog (HiGHS). Compare iteration counts and find the instance family where your numerics fall apart first.
Checkpoint. Your simplex agrees with HiGHS on 1000 random LPs, you can exhibit an instance where yours cycles without Bland's rule, and you can say in one sentence what steepest-edge pricing buys over Dantzig's rule.
03

Duality — the load-bearing unit

spine

needs 02

Theory
The dual as a mechanical transform of the primal; weak and strong duality; complementary slackness; duals as shadow prices; the dual simplex and why it is the algorithm that makes branch-and-cut possible; sensitivity analysis and ranging. Spend longer here than feels justified: units 08, 10, 11, 12, 15, 24 and 29 are each this unit wearing a different hat.
Build
An automatic primal→dual transformer that handles all constraint senses and variable bounds. Extract dual values from your simplex basis and verify complementary slackness numerically. Implement the dual simplex and use it to re-solve after adding one constraint — the warm start that branch-and-cut lives on.
Then
Pull duals and reduced costs out of HiGHS and Gurobi; confirm yours match, including sign conventions (which will take you an afternoon, and should).
Checkpoint. Given a solved LP you can read off the shadow price of each constraint, predict the objective change from a small right-hand-side perturbation before re-solving, and be right.
04

Polynomial-time LP: ellipsoid and interior point

branch

needs 02 · feeds 09

Theory
Why "is LP in P?" was open until Khachiyan in 1979; the ellipsoid method's volume-shrinking argument; why it is useless in practice and indispensable in theory (see unit 09); Karmarkar and the primal–dual path-following interior point method; the central path and the log barrier; why IPMs win on large sparse LPs and lose badly on warm starts.
Build
A log-barrier method on two-variable LPs with the central path plotted — this is a picture worth having. A toy ellipsoid method for feasibility.
Then
HiGHS or Gurobi with method=barrier vs simplex on a large sparse LP; find the crossover size. Then re-solve with one constraint added and watch the barrier method lose.
Checkpoint. You can choose between simplex and barrier for a given LP and defend it, and explain why branch-and-bound still wants simplex at every node.

Part II — trunk A continues · integer programming

Where hardness enters, and the architecture that copes with it

The longest part, and the one that most repays the slow reading. Everything here is a strategy for exploiting the LP relaxation you built in Part I: tighten it, branch on it, cut it, decompose it, or dualise it.

05

Formulations, relaxations, and the integrality gap

spine

needs 03

Theory
IP and MIP; the LP relaxation; the convex hull of integer points as the ideal formulation you can never write down; formulation strength as a partial order; big-M versus indicator constraints, and how a lazily chosen M destroys solve times; extended formulations and the power of auxiliary variables — with the Fiorini–Massar–Pokutta–Tiwary–de Wolf result that no polynomial-size extended formulation of the TSP polytope exists; symmetry as a formulation defect.
Build
Three formulations of one facility-location instance family — weak big-M, tight big-M, and disaggregated. Measure the root relaxation bound of each. Keep them; units 07, 08 and 27 come back to these same three models.
Then
CBC and Gurobi through PuLP or python-mip. Tabulate relaxation gap, node count, and wall time for all three. The ordering will be stark.
Checkpoint. Shown two formulations of one problem, you can predict which solves faster from their relaxation bounds alone, and be right more often than not.
06

Total unimodularity: when integrality is free

branch

needs 03 · illuminates 14, 15

Theory
Totally unimodular matrices; the Hoffman–Kruskal theorem; interval matrices, network matrices, bipartite incidence matrices; why assignment, transportation and flow LPs have integral optima with no branching at all; balanced and perfect matrices; integral polyhedra in general. This unit explains why Part III exists — the polynomial islands are the places where the LP is already integral.
Build
A TU checker using Ghouila-Houri's criterion on small matrices. Solve an assignment problem as a pure LP and watch integrality fall out unasked. Then make the graph non-bipartite and watch half-integral vertices appear.
Then
LP-solved assignment vs scipy.optimize.linear_sum_assignment.
Checkpoint. Given a constraint matrix you can decide whether to expect integral LP optima, and when the answer is no, construct the odd cycle that breaks it.
07

Branch and bound

spine

needs 05

Theory
The search tree; bounding and pruning; branching variable selection — most-fractional, pseudocost, strong branching, reliability branching; node selection — depth-first for incumbents, best-first for the bound, and the hybrids everyone actually ships; primal heuristics (diving, RINS, feasibility pump); the gap; why the two hard jobs are finding a good solution early and proving nothing better exists.
Build
Your own branch-and-bound over your unit-02 simplex, with pluggable branching and node selection and instrumented node counts. Run the full matrix {most-fractional, pseudocost, strong} × {DFS, best-first} on the unit-05 formulations.
Then
CBC and Gurobi on the same instances. Read their logs line by line, find your factor of shame in node count, and write down which of their features you believe explains it. Units 08 and 27 then test those hypotheses.
Checkpoint. Your strong-branching variant beats your most-fractional variant on node count while losing on wall time, and you can explain that trade-off in terms of LP solves per node.
08

Cutting planes and branch-and-cut

spine

needs 07

Theory
Valid inequalities, faces and facets; Chvátal–Gomory cuts and CG rank; Gomory mixed-integer cuts read straight off the simplex tableau; the problem-specific families that do the real work — knapsack cover cuts and lifting, clique and odd-hole cuts, flow covers, MIR; the separation problem as the central question; cut management, and why solvers discard most cuts they generate; branch-and-cut as the actual architecture of every modern MIP solver.
Build
Gomory fractional cuts from your tableau, applied at the root until the tableau's numerics defeat you (they will — that is a finding, not a failure). Cover-cut separation for knapsack, with lifting. A cut pool with an aging policy. Report gap closed at the root per family.
Then
Gurobi with Cuts=0 vs default; compare your root gap closed against theirs, per cut family where the log tells you.
Checkpoint. From a fractional simplex tableau you can write down a valid Gomory cut by hand and prove it separates the current vertex while excluding no integer point.
09

Separation ⇔ optimization

branch · deep theory

needs 04 08

Theory
The Grötschel–Lovász–Schrijver equivalence: you can optimize over a polytope in polynomial time if and only if you can separate over it in polynomial time, with the ellipsoid method as the bridge. Why this makes exponentially many constraints perfectly acceptable — subtour elimination for TSP, the odd-set inequalities of the matching polytope — and why lazy constraints are the engineering face of a theorem. This is the most beautiful result in the course.
Build
A subtour-elimination separation oracle: connected components for integral points, min-cut (your unit-14 max-flow, if you have it) for fractional ones. Solve the TSP relaxation by lazy constraint generation and watch a few hundred constraints stand in for 2n.
Then
Gurobi lazy-constraint callbacks on TSPLIB instances of a few hundred cities. Then Concorde, to see what a decade of this idea looks like.
Checkpoint. You can state the theorem precisely, and you have solved a TSP over an exponential constraint family while only ever writing down a few hundred of its constraints.
10

Column generation, Dantzig–Wolfe, branch-and-price

spine

needs 08 16

Theory
The restricted master problem; reduced costs and the pricing subproblem; Dantzig–Wolfe decomposition and block-angular structure; why the pricer is usually itself NP-hard and that is fine; the Lagrangian bound you get for free; dual degeneracy and stabilisation; branch-and-price, and why naive variable branching destroys the pricing problem.
Build
Cutting stock by column generation with a knapsack pricer (your unit-16 DP, reused). Then vehicle routing, where pricing becomes a resource-constrained shortest path — the step where this stops being a toy.
Then
The compact MIP formulation of the same cutting-stock instance in Gurobi. Compare bound quality and model size; the point at which the compact model stops fitting in memory is the argument for the whole technique.
Checkpoint. You can explain why the master's dual values are exactly the objective coefficients of the pricing problem, in the vocabulary of unit 03, without hand-waving.
11

Lagrangian relaxation and subgradient methods

spine

needs 03 16

Theory
Dualising the hard constraints; the Lagrangian dual and its concavity; the integrality property — and why it tells you in advance when Lagrangian relaxation will give you nothing the LP did not; subgradients and step-size rules; the Held–Karp bound for TSP via 1-trees; Lagrangian heuristics and variable fixing by reduced cost.
Build
The Held–Karp 1-tree bound with subgradient ascent, on TSPLIB instances. A Lagrangian relaxation of generalised assignment, with the bound tracked against iteration count so you can see the notorious zig-zag.
Then
Compare your Held–Karp bound to Gurobi's root LP bound on the same instances. They should be close, and knowing why they are close is the unit.
Checkpoint. Before writing any code, you can predict whether dualising a given constraint set will beat the LP relaxation, using the integrality property.
12

Benders decomposition

branch

needs 10

Theory
Splitting a MIP into a master over the hard variables and a subproblem over the rest; optimality and feasibility cuts derived from the subproblem's dual; Benders as row generation and therefore the formal dual of Dantzig–Wolfe's column generation; two-stage stochastic programming with recourse as its natural home; modern variants — Benders inside the branch-and-bound tree, cut strengthening, Pareto-optimal cuts.
Build
Benders on a two-stage stochastic facility location, with the scenario subproblems solved independently.
Then
The monolithic deterministic-equivalent model in Gurobi. Find the scenario count at which Benders overtakes it — then report it as a crossover plot, not an anecdote.
Checkpoint. You can draw Dantzig–Wolfe and Benders on one page as duals of each other — rows vs columns, master vs subproblem — and point at where unit 03 sits in both.

Part III — trunk B · the polynomial islands

The problems that are genuinely easy, and the structural reasons why

Independent of Parts I–II: you can start this trunk the day after unit 00. But read unit 06 alongside it — total unimodularity is the polyhedral explanation for everything that happens here.

13

Matroids and the exact reach of greedy

spine

needs 00

Theory
Independence systems and matroids; the exchange axiom; the Rado–Edmonds theorem — greedy is optimal on a matroid and only on a matroid, which is the cleanest "when does this heuristic actually work" answer in the field; graphic, linear, uniform, partition and transversal matroids; matroid intersection in polynomial time and three-matroid intersection in NP-hard; submodularity, the 1 − 1/e greedy bound for monotone submodular maximisation, and the matroid polytope.
Build
A matroid interface with independence oracles; instantiate the graphic matroid and watch Kruskal's algorithm fall out of generic greedy. Matroid intersection. Greedy submodular maximisation on a coverage function, with the 1 − 1/e bound checked empirically against exact optima from unit 00.
Then
networkx MST for the graphic case; an exact MIP for small submodular instances.
Checkpoint. Shown a greedy algorithm someone proposes, you can either name the matroid underneath it or construct the instance where it fails.
14

Network flows

spine

needs 13

Theory
Max-flow min-cut; augmenting paths; Ford–Fulkerson's failure to terminate on irrational capacities; Edmonds–Karp, Dinic, push–relabel and their complexities; min-cost flow by successive shortest paths with potentials, cycle cancelling, and network simplex; flow decomposition. The real skill is reduction to flow: bipartite matching, project selection / maximum closure, image segmentation, baseball elimination, and scheduling with deadlines.
Build
Dinic and push–relabel; min-cost flow by SSP with Johnson potentials; the project-selection reduction end to end.
Then
OR-Tools SimpleMaxFlow / SimpleMinCostFlow and networkx. Benchmark on layered vs random vs bipartite graphs and find the family where push–relabel beats Dinic.
Checkpoint. Handed a problem you have not seen, you can decide whether it is a flow in disguise and write the reduction, including the capacities that encode the objective.
15

Matching

spine

needs 14 · feeds 18, 23

Theory
König's and Hall's theorems on bipartite graphs; Hopcroft–Karp; the Hungarian algorithm as primal–dual on the assignment LP — the first place unit 03 pays off outside LP; Tutte's theorem; Edmonds' blossom algorithm and the paper that first argued "polynomial" is the right definition of tractable; the matching polytope's odd-set inequalities (which is unit 09's other example); Gale–Shapley stable matching as a different beast entirely.
Build
Hopcroft–Karp. The Hungarian algorithm from scratch, printing its dual potentials. Blossom as the stretch goal — it is hard, and worth one difficult week. Gale–Shapley, for contrast.
Then
scipy.optimize.linear_sum_assignment, networkx.max_weight_matching.
Checkpoint. You can verify your Hungarian algorithm's answer is optimal by checking complementary slackness against its dual potentials, without re-running the algorithm.
16

Dynamic programming over combinatorial structure

spine

needs 15 · feeds 10, 11

Theory
DP as shortest path in a DAG of states; knapsack's pseudo-polynomial algorithm and precisely why it does not contradict NP-hardness; bitmask DP; Held–Karp exact TSP in O(n²2ⁿ); DP on trees; treewidth and tree decompositions; Courcelle's theorem as the general statement of why all of these work; the vocabulary of parameterised complexity — FPT, XP, kernelisation — as the honest way to say "hard but not hopeless".
Build
Knapsack DP (this becomes unit 10's pricer). Held–Karp exact TSP. A tree-decomposition DP for vertex cover, with runtime plotted against treewidth on random graphs — the exponential in the parameter, made visible.
Then
Held–Karp against your unit-07 branch-and-bound and against Gurobi on n = 15…22 — three completely different algorithms, one crossover plot.
Checkpoint. You can compute a decomposition's width and predict the DP's runtime from it to within an order of magnitude.

Part IV — trunk C · constraint programming and SAT

The other tradition: prune the domain rather than relax and bound

A genuinely different intellectual lineage, with different vocabulary and different instincts — and the one that currently wins scheduling competitions outright. Independent of Parts I–II except where marked.

17

Constraint satisfaction and propagation

spine

needs 00

Theory
The CSP as a formalism — variables, domains, constraints, often no objective at all; consistency levels (node, arc, path, k-consistency) and what each costs; AC-3 and AC-4; bounds vs domain consistency; propagation to a fixpoint; backtracking search interleaved with propagation as the CP loop. The philosophical difference from Part II stated plainly: MIP relaxes and bounds, CP prunes domains, and the two see completely different instances as easy.
Build
A small CP engine: domains with trailing, a propagation queue, AC-3, backtracking search. Solve Sudoku, n-queens, map colouring, magic series — and instrument propagations and nodes, because those two numbers are the whole diagnostic vocabulary of Part IV.
Then
OR-Tools CP-SAT on the same models.
Checkpoint. You can trace your propagator's fixpoint by hand on a 4×4 Sudoku, and explain why arc consistency does not imply satisfiability.
18

Global constraints and their filtering algorithms

spine

needs 17 15

Theory
Why one constraint over n variables beats n(n−1)/2 binary ones; Régin's filtering for alldifferent, via Hall sets and maximum matching in the value graph — which is unit 15 doing load-bearing work in a different tradition; cumulative with timetable and edge-finding filters, and disjunctive scheduling; element, table, circuit, gcc; the trade-off between a propagator's strength and its cost, which is the only design question in CP.
Build
Régin's alldifferent on top of your Hopcroft–Karp. A cumulative timetable propagator. Plug both into your unit-17 engine and measure the node-count collapse on n-queens and job-shop.
Then
CP-SAT's AddAllDifferent and AddCumulative on the same models.
Checkpoint. On one instance, your decomposed model and your global-constraint model give the same answer and differ by orders of magnitude in node count — and you can name the Hall set responsible.
19

Search, restarts, and large neighbourhood search

spine

needs 18 · feeds 21, 26

Theory
Variable ordering (first-fail, dom/wdeg, impact-based) and value ordering; symmetry breaking during search; randomisation and restart policies, Luby in particular; nogood recording; heavy-tailed runtime distributions and why they are the actual justification for restarts; large neighbourhood search — destroy and repair, with the propagator as the repair engine — as the bridge from exact search to heuristics.
Build
dom/wdeg and impact-based heuristics in your engine; Luby restarts; run 100 seeds on one hard instance and plot the runtime distribution until you can see the heavy tail. Then an LNS layer on job-shop.
Then
CP-SAT with its LNS workers disabled and enabled; OR-Tools' routing solver on a CVRP.
Checkpoint. You can show the heavy-tailed runtime histogram from your own solver and demonstrate that restarts move its mean — with the distributions, not with one lucky run.
20

SAT and the CDCL engine

spine

needs 17

Theory
CNF, resolution, DPLL; unit propagation; conflict analysis via the implication graph and the first-UIP cut; clause learning and non-chronological backjumping; VSIDS activity heuristics; two-watched-literal propagation; clause deletion and LBD; phase saving. Then the honest framing: CDCL is resolution with a very good heuristic, and proof complexity tells you exactly what it can never do quickly. Encodings: Tseitin, at-most-one (pairwise, sequential, commander, bitwise), cardinality networks.
Build
A CDCL solver — watched literals, 1UIP learning, VSIDS, Luby restarts, LBD-based clause deletion. Then encoders for graph colouring, n-queens, and the pigeonhole principle, which will humiliate it. That humiliation is the unit's point.
Then
MiniSat, CaDiCaL and Kissat on your CNFs. DIMACS in and out, so instances travel both ways.
Checkpoint. Your solver emits a DRAT proof that an external checker accepts, and you can explain pigeonhole's hardness in terms of resolution lower bounds rather than "it just takes a long time".
21

Lazy clause generation, or what CP-SAT actually is

branch

needs 19 20

Theory
The hybrid that ate the field: CP propagators that explain their inferences as clauses, fed to a CDCL engine that learns from them; the order encoding of integers that lets a SAT solver reason about bounds; SMT and DPLL(T) as the same idea arriving from the other direction; the linear relaxation CP-SAT runs alongside, which is Part II smuggled into Part IV.
Build
Make one unit-18 propagator explanation-producing and feed its clauses to your unit-20 CDCL. Measure what learning from propagation buys on job-shop.
Then
Read CP-SAT's log on a job-shop instance and identify, by name, every component you have now built yourself.
Checkpoint. For a problem you have not modelled before you can say which of MIP / CP / SAT / CP-SAT you would reach for first, and defend it with something other than taste.

Part V — where the trunks converge

When exact is hopeless: how far from optimal must you settle?

This is the part that needs all three trunks — LP for rounding, matching and MST for Christofides, CP for the neighbourhood search. It is also the part with the most beautiful theory in the whole subject.

22

Hardness, and the shape of what is possible

spine

needs 11

Theory
NP-completeness through the canonical reduction chain (3SAT → vertex cover → set cover → …); strong vs weak NP-hardness, and why knapsack sits on the weak side; the approximation hierarchy — FPTAS ⊂ PTAS ⊂ APX ⊂ log-approximable ⊂ the rest; the PCP theorem's role in making hardness of approximation possible; Set Cover's (1−o(1))·ln n threshold; Vertex Cover's 2−ε and Max-Cut's 0.878 under the Unique Games Conjecture. The practical payoff: knowing when to stop looking for a better algorithm.
Build
Mechanised reductions — a program that maps a 3SAT instance to a vertex cover instance and maps solutions back, verified against your unit-00 oracle in both directions. Reductions become much less abstract once yours has a bug.
Then
Solve both sides of each reduction with CP-SAT and confirm optimality is preserved, not just feasibility.
Checkpoint. For each of unit 00's nine problems you can state the best known approximation ratio and the best known hardness bound, and say where the gap is still open.
23

Combinatorial approximation algorithms

spine

needs 22 16 15

Theory
Greedy set cover's Hn bound and its tightness; 2-approximation for vertex cover via maximal matching; metric TSP — double-tree 2-approximation, Christofides at 3/2, and the 2021 Karlin–Klein–Oveis Gharan improvement that finally broke it; k-center greedy and its 2-tightness; local search for k-median; scheduling — list scheduling's 2−1/m, LPT's 4/3, and the FPTAS for makespan on a fixed number of machines.
Build
Greedy set cover with the Hn bound checked empirically. Christofides — which needs your MST from unit 13 and your minimum-weight perfect matching from unit 15, and is the moment trunk B pays off. LPT. k-center greedy.
Then
Measure actual ratios against CP-SAT-proved optima on instances small enough to solve exactly. The distance between worst-case guarantee and typical performance is the finding.
Checkpoint. Your Christofides never exceeds 1.5× optimum on instances you can solve exactly, and you can point at the line of code corresponding to each step of the proof.
24

LP rounding and the primal–dual method

spine

needs 23 03

Theory
Deterministic rounding (vertex cover's ½-threshold, which is a two-line proof and a genuinely surprising one); randomized rounding with the Chernoff and union-bound argument giving set cover's O(log n); the integrality gap as the hard limit on any LP-based analysis; the primal–dual schema — raise duals, buy primals, pay for what you bought — for set cover, Steiner forest and facility location; iterative rounding and Jain's 2-approximation for Steiner network as the elegant endpoint.
Build
Randomized rounding for set cover, with the failure probability measured empirically against the bound. Primal–dual set cover. Primal–dual facility location.
Then
Three numbers on every instance — the LP bound from HiGHS, the integer optimum from Gurobi, and your rounded solution — plotted together. The visible gap between the first two is the integrality gap you just read about.
Checkpoint. You can exhibit an instance realising the integrality gap of a given LP, thereby proving no rounding of that LP can beat that ratio.
25

Semidefinite relaxations

branch

needs 24 21

Theory
Optimising over the PSD cone; vector programs as relaxations that LPs cannot express; Goemans–Williamson Max-Cut and random-hyperplane rounding at 0.878…; the Lovász theta function sandwiched between clique and chromatic number; the Lasserre / Sum-of-Squares hierarchy as a tower of ever-tighter relaxations and the lower bounds against it; why SDPs are convex but expensive, which is why they stay a theoretical tool.
Build
Goemans–Williamson with CVXPY plus your own hyperplane rounding, over many random hyperplanes. The theta function on small graphs. Compare the SDP bound, the LP bound, and the truth on the same instances.
Then
Exact Max-Cut with Gurobi for n ≤ 40 to measure your realised ratio.
Checkpoint. You can show empirically that your GW cuts average well above 0.878 of optimum, and explain why the guarantee is nonetheless tight.
26

Metaheuristics, benchmarked honestly

spine

needs 24 19

Theory
Local search and neighbourhood design; 2-opt, 3-opt, Or-opt and Lin–Kernighan for TSP; simulated annealing and its cooling schedules; tabu search; GRASP; iterated and adaptive large neighbourhood search; genetic algorithms, and a frank account of why they are usually the wrong answer. Then the part most courses omit: this literature has a replication problem, "our metaheuristic beat theirs" is very often unfalsifiable, and knowing what an honest comparison requires is more valuable than knowing a tenth metaphor.
Build
2-opt and Or-opt with don't-look bits and neighbour lists for TSP. Simulated annealing with a tuned schedule. ALNS for CVRP with destroy/repair operators and adaptive weights.
Then
LKH and OR-Tools routing on the same instances under the same time budget — the constraint most published comparisons quietly drop.
Checkpoint. You can produce a time-to-target plot comparing three of your own heuristics over 30 seeds, and correctly decline to declare a winner where the distributions overlap.

Part VI — making it real

The two units that make every earlier claim defensible

Short, unglamorous, and the difference between having done a course and being able to use any of it. Unit 28 in particular retroactively upgrades every "compare" step you have run so far.

27

Modelling for a real solver

spine

needs 26 08

Theory
What presolve actually does, and the modelling habits that defeat it; symmetry and symmetry breaking (lex-leader constraints, orbital branching) — the single biggest lever on many models; indicator constraints instead of big-M; bound tightening; warm starts and MIP starts; solution pools; and reading a solver log fluently — the gap, node count, cut counts per family, heuristic hits, presolve reductions. MIPLIB 2017 as the field's shared reality check.
Build
Take your worst-performing model from units 05 and 07 and make it 10× faster without changing what it means. One intervention at a time, each measured separately.
Then
Gurobi and HiGHS logs on MIPLIB instances — including a few that neither solves, so the experience of a genuinely open instance is not theoretical.
Checkpoint. A before/after table of your own model with one row per intervention and the time each bought, containing no row you cannot explain.

Convexity, gradients, and what continuity changes

coda

needs 03

Theory
Convex sets and functions; gradients and subgradients; the KKT conditions as the generalisation of LP duality you already know — complementary slackness is the KKT complementarity condition, and seeing that is most of the unit; first-order methods (gradient descent, momentum, Nesterov) and their rates; stochastic gradients; constrained methods — projected gradient, and Frank–Wolfe, which puts a linear optimization oracle inside a continuous loop and is therefore the cleanest formal handshake between the two halves of this course.
Build
GD, momentum and Nesterov on standard test functions with convergence plotted. Projected gradient onto a simplex. Then Frank–Wolfe over a polytope, using your unit-02 simplex as the linear oracle.
Then
scipy.optimize and CVXPY for the same problems.
Checkpoint. You can derive the KKT conditions for a linear program and show they reduce exactly to complementary slackness from unit 03.

Automatic differentiation

coda

needs 29 28

Theory
The three things constantly confused: symbolic, numerical and automatic differentiation. Dual numbers and forward mode. The Wengert tape and reverse mode. The one fact that explains modern ML's existence: reverse mode computes a full gradient in O(1) forward passes regardless of parameter count, while forward mode costs one pass per parameter. Checkpointing and the memory/compute trade. Jacobian-vector and vector-Jacobian products; forward-over-reverse for Hessian-vector products. And where autodiff quietly lies: non-smooth points, control flow, in-place mutation, and argmax.
Build
Forward-mode AD with dual numbers. Then a reverse-mode tape with a small op set — enough to train a two-layer MLP with your own backprop. Gradient-check every op against finite differences.
Then
JAX or PyTorch: grad, jacrev, jacfwd. Confirm your gradients agree to 1e-6, and time yours against theirs.
Checkpoint. Your reverse-mode engine trains an MLP on a real dataset, and you can compute the tape's memory cost in bytes before you run it.

Where discrete meets differentiable

coda

needs 30 07 15

Theory
Differentiating through discrete choices — straight-through estimators, Gumbel-Softmax and concrete relaxations, REINFORCE versus reparameterisation. Differentiable optimization layers: differentiating through an argmin via the implicit function theorem applied to the KKT conditions (OptNet, cvxpylayers), differentiable sorting and top-k, and Sinkhorn as a differentiable relaxation of the assignment problem — your unit-15 Hungarian algorithm, softened. Then learning to optimize: learned branching and cut-selection policies inside MIP solvers, neural combinatorial optimization (pointer networks, attention models, POMO), and the honest current state — still behind LKH and CP-SAT on classical problems, genuinely promising as guidance inside exact solvers rather than as a replacement for them.
Build
Sinkhorn against your Hungarian algorithm on the same assignment instances — accuracy, speed, and the gradient the Hungarian cannot give you. Then a learned branching rule for your unit-07 branch-and-bound, trained by imitating strong branching, evaluated on node count over held-out instances.
Then
cvxpylayers for the argmin layer; optionally a pretrained attention TSP model against LKH, which is a bracing comparison.
Checkpoint. Your imitation-learned branching rule beats most-fractional branching on node count on held-out instances — or you can say precisely why it did not, in terms of unit 28's methodology rather than anecdote.

Capstone — one problem, four traditions

Everything converges on a single instance family, which you attack four ways and then referee.

The brief

Pick one problem — capacitated vehicle routing with time windows, or job-shop scheduling. Then solve it four times: (a) a MIP with your own cover and subtour cuts on a commercial solver; (b) branch-and-price with a resource-constrained shortest-path pricer; (c) CP-SAT with global constraints and a search strategy you chose deliberately; (d) ALNS with operators you designed.

Then run all four through your unit-28 harness on a shared instance set under a shared time budget, and write the report that says which wins where. The deliverable is not a winner — it is a defensible account of which instance characteristics favour which method, with the evidence attached and the cases where the answer is "they are indistinguishable" reported as such.

If thirty-two is too many

There is a defensible short path. It drops every branch node and all of trunk C's depth, and it still leaves you able to read the literature.

Reading and tools

Chosen so that most units have exactly one chapter to read, not four books to triangulate between.

Primary texts

  • Bertsimas & Tsitsiklis, Introduction to Linear Optimization — Part I. The best treatment of duality for someone who wants to actually use it.
  • Wolsey, Integer Programming (2nd ed.) — Part II, readable end to end.
  • Conforti, Cornuéjols & Zambelli, Integer Programming — Part II when you want the modern, deeper account.
  • Korte & Vygen, Combinatorial Optimization — Part III's reference, and the book to own.
