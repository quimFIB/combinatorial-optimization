02

Simplex, honestly

Walking from vertex to better vertex: why it works, when it loops forever, when it takes exponentially long, and why it is still the workhorse.

needs 01~1½ h slides + 3 h lablab: uv run co lab 02

Unit 01 ended with the fundamental theorem: a bounded, feasible LP over a pointed polyhedron is optimized at a vertex, and vertices are basic feasible solutions. Lab 01 enumerated all $\binom{m}{n}$ bases to find them. This unit replaces enumeration by local search on the graph of vertices. Because the objective is linear and the feasible region convex, local search here is exact: a vertex with no improving neighbour is optimal.

"Honestly" in the title means the unit does not stop at the version that works on textbook examples. It covers the three ways the naive method fails: cycling (degeneracy), exponential running time (Klee–Minty), and floating point. Each of them is reproduced in the lab.

Convention for the whole unit: $\max\{c^\top x : Ax \le b,\ x \ge 0\}$, $A \in \mathbb{Q}^{m\times n}$, with slacks $s = b - Ax \ge 0$.

section 1

From vertices to bases

An algebraic handle on a geometric walk.

02 · bases

Bases and basic solutions

Add slacks: $\;Ax + s = b,\; x, s \ge 0$. Write all $n+m$ variables as one vector with matrix $[A \mid I]$, which has rank $m$.

definition

A basis $B$ is a set of $m$ columns of $[A\mid I]$ that are linearly independent. Setting the other ($N$, nonbasic) variables to 0 gives the basic solution $x_B = A_B^{-1} b$.

It is feasible when $x_B \ge 0$.

Basic feasible solutions are exactly the vertices of unit 01. Different bases can give the same vertex, which is degeneracy.

the starting basis

If $b \ge 0$, the slacks form a feasible basis: $x = 0,\ s = b$. The origin is a vertex.

If some $b_i < 0$ it is not feasible. That is what phase 1 (section 3) is for.

Why BFS = vertex in this form: in $\{(x,s) : Ax + s = b,\ x,s \ge 0\}$ a point is a vertex when the columns of its positive variables are linearly independent. That set can be extended to a basis of $m$ independent columns. This is unit 01's "$n$ tight constraints of rank $n$" rewritten for equality form: the tight constraints are the nonbasic variables' $\ge 0$ bounds.

A vertex with fewer than $m$ positive basic variables is degenerate. It extends to a basis in several ways, one per choice of which zero-valued variables to call basic. Degeneracy is not rare: combinatorial LPs such as assignment, matching and set covering are massively degenerate, and it is the reason cycling is a practical concern and not just a curiosity.

02 · bases

Reduced costs: the optimality certificate

Solve the constraints for the basic variables and substitute into the objective:

$$x_B = A_B^{-1} b - A_B^{-1} A_N\, x_N, \qquad c^\top x = \underbrace{c_B^\top A_B^{-1} b}_{\text{current value}} + \underbrace{(c_N^\top - c_B^\top A_B^{-1} A_N)}_{\bar c_N^\top}\, x_N$$
optimality criterion

If the BFS is feasible and every reduced cost $\bar c_j \le 0$, it is optimal.

proof

Every feasible point has $x_N \ge 0$, so its value is the current value plus $\bar c_N^\top x_N$, which is at most the current value. $\square$

If some $\bar c_j > 0$, increasing $x_j$ from 0 improves the objective at rate $\bar c_j$. That $j$ may enter the basis.

This is the "dictionary" view (Chvátal's term): each basis rewrites the same LP as "basic variables = constants − combinations of nonbasic ones" and "objective = constant + reduced costs times nonbasic ones". A pivot moves to an equivalent rewriting. The optimality test reads the sign pattern of the objective row.

The quantity $y^\top = c_B^\top A_B^{-1}$ is the vector of simplex multipliers. Reduced costs are $\bar c_j = c_j - y^\top a_j$, and at an optimal basis $y$ is an optimal dual solution. Unit 03 is about exactly this. The criterion "all $\bar c_j \le 0$" says $A^\top y \ge c$ and $y \ge 0$ (reading the slack columns), which is dual feasibility.

Sign convention used by the lab's tableau: the objective row stores $-\bar c$, so optimality is "no negative entries in the objective row". That is the convention of most textbooks that start from the $z - c^\top x = 0$ row.

The criterion is sufficient but not necessary for optimality of the point. At a degenerate optimal vertex some bases can show a positive reduced cost even though the vertex is optimal, because the improving direction is blocked at step length 0. That is why simplex sometimes makes degenerate pivots at an optimal vertex before it can prove optimality.

02 · bases

The ratio test: how far to move

Increase entering $x_j$ to $t \ge 0$ with the other nonbasic variables held at 0. The basic variables change linearly:

$$x_B(t) = \bar b - t\, d, \qquad \bar b = A_B^{-1}b,\ \ d = A_B^{-1} a_j$$

Feasibility needs $\bar b_i - t d_i \ge 0$ for every row, which binds only where $d_i > 0$:

$$t^* = \min_{i:\,d_i > 0} \frac{\bar b_i}{d_i}$$

The row attaining the minimum leaves: its basic variable hits 0.

no $d_i > 0$

Then $x_j$ can grow forever, and the LP is unbounded. The ray $x_B = \bar b - t d$, $x_j = t$ is a certificate: feasible for all $t$, with objective growing at rate $\bar c_j > 0$.

degenerate step

If the minimizing $\bar b_i = 0$, then $t^* = 0$. The basis changes but the point does not move.

Geometrically, the nonbasic variables other than $x_j$ stay at their bound 0, so $n-1$ of the $n$ tight constraints at the current vertex stay tight. The solutions of $n-1$ independent equations form a line, and the move is along the edge of the polyhedron in that line. The ratio test stops at the first new constraint that becomes tight, which is the vertex at the other end of the edge. A pivot is therefore an edge step, and the simplex path is a walk on the graph of the polyhedron.

Why only $d_i > 0$: rows with $d_i \le 0$ get larger or stay as $t$ grows, so they never become infeasible. Pivoting on a negative $d_i$ would make the right-hand side of that row negative, which is an infeasible basis. Lab step 2's "think about" asks exactly this.

02 · bases

A walk on the example polygon

$\max\ 2x+y$ over unit 01's polygon. Dantzig's rule: enter $x$ (coefficient 2), then $y$.

  1. Origin. Slack basis. $\bar c = (2, 1)$. Enter $x$. Ratio test: $x-y\le 1$ binds first, at $x=1$.
  2. $(1, 0)$. Now $x = 1 + y - s_2$ and $z = 2 + 3y - 2s_2$. Enter $y$. $x + y \le 4$ binds at $y = 1.5$.
  3. $(2.5, 1.5)$. Both reduced costs $\le 0$: optimal, $z = 6.5$.

Two pivots, and the vertex $(0,4)$ was never visited. Simplex visits only an improving path, not the whole vertex set.

Check step 2 yourself. From $x - y + s_2 = 1$ at the basis $\{s_1, x\}$: $x = 1 + y - s_2$. Substitute into $2x + y$ to get $2 + 3y - 2s_2$. The reduced cost of $y$ is 3 and of $s_2$ is $-2$. Then from $x + y + s_1 = 4$: $s_1 = 3 - 2y + s_2 \ge 0$ gives $y \le 1.5$.

At the optimum the reduced costs of the two slacks are $-(\tfrac32, \tfrac12)$: increasing either slack (loosening that constraint the other way) costs objective. Their negatives $y = (\tfrac32, \tfrac12)$ are the shadow prices. Loosen $x+y\le 4$ by one unit and the optimum rises by $\tfrac32$. That is unit 03's first computation, done here for free.

The tableau is the dictionary written as a matrix. Row $i$ says "basic variable $i$ + (nonbasic combination) = rhs", and the bottom row says "$z - \bar c^\top x_N = $ current value". A pivot is one step of Gauss–Jordan elimination that makes the entering column a unit vector. That is all the algebra there is.

Everything in the tableau is $A_B^{-1}$ applied to the original data: the body is $A_B^{-1}[A\mid I]$ and the rhs is $A_B^{-1}b$. In particular the slack columns of the body are $A_B^{-1}$. The revised simplex method (section 3) stores only a factorization of $A_B$ and computes the one column and one row a pivot needs.

section 2

How the naive method fails

Twice: by never stopping, and by taking exponentially long to stop.

02 · failure

Degeneracy and cycling

A degenerate pivot has $t^* = 0$: the basis changes and the point stays. The objective does not increase, so the usual termination argument (strict improvement, finitely many bases) fails.

Beale, 1955

$\max\ \tfrac34 x_1 - 20x_2 + \tfrac12 x_3 - 6x_4$
$\tfrac14 x_1 - 8x_2 - x_3 + 9x_4 \le 0$
$\tfrac12 x_1 - 12x_2 - \tfrac12 x_3 + 3x_4 \le 0$
$x_3 \le 1$

With the largest-coefficient rule and ties broken by lowest row, the basis returns to the slacks after 6 degenerate pivots, and repeats forever.

optimum

$\tfrac54$ at $x = (1, 0, 1, 0)$. Bland's rule reaches it in 6 pivots.

Both counts come from the lab's reference solution. Your step 3 test reproduces them.

Finiteness argument without degeneracy: each pivot strictly increases the objective, so no basis repeats, and there are at most $\binom{n+m}{m}$ bases. With degeneracy the objective can stay flat, and a sequence of distinct bases at the same vertex can close into a loop.

Cycling was considered a theoretical curiosity for decades: early codes rarely cycled because floating-point noise broke the ties. It is not purely theoretical. Highly degenerate combinatorial LPs stall (long runs of degenerate pivots) in practice, and exact-arithmetic solvers, which have no noise to break ties, do cycle without a rule.

The lab detects cycling by remembering bases, which costs memory proportional to the number of pivots. A production code never does this. It prevents cycling instead, by one of the rules on the next slide.

02 · failure

Bland's rule

theorem (Bland, 1977)

If the entering variable is always the lowest-index improving one, and ties in the ratio test go to the lowest-index basic variable, the simplex method never cycles.

proof idea

Suppose it cycles, and let $x_t$ be the largest-index variable that enters and leaves during the cycle. Compare the dictionary where $x_t$ leaves (entering $x_s$, $s < t$) with the one where $x_t$ re-enters. Reduced costs from the second, applied to the move of the first, give an identity whose terms are all $\le 0$ by the index choices, except one that must be $> 0$. Contradiction.

Other cures: lexicographic ratio test (Dantzig, Orden, Wolfe 1955); perturbing $b$ symbolically or numerically.

The price: Bland's rule ignores how much a column improves, so it is usually slow. Production codes use good pricing plus perturbation, and fall back to anti-cycling measures only when they detect stalling.

The full proof is one page in Chvátal ch. 3 or Bertsimas & Tsitsiklis §3.4 (they use the lexicographic rule and leave Bland as an exercise with hints). The key identity: in the later dictionary the objective is $z = z_0 + \sum_j \bar c^{*}_j x_j$. The earlier move increases $x_s$ by $t$ and changes basic variables linearly. Since both dictionaries describe the same function, the coefficients of $t$ must agree, and the sign constraints from "lowest index" choices at both pivots make them disagree.

Perturbation in practice: add tiny random amounts to the right-hand side (primal simplex) or to the costs (dual simplex) so that ties essentially never happen, solve, then remove the perturbation and clean up with a few more pivots. Real dual simplex codes, HiGHS among them, do this, which is part of why they barely ever cycle.

Lab check: in then, Bland's rule takes 465 pivots on the $n=12$ Klee–Minty cube, against Dantzig's 4095. Bland is not a speed rule; it just happens to do better on an instance built to defeat Dantzig.

Klee–Minty: exponentially many pivots

A squashed $n$-cube (Klee & Minty, 1972). In Chvátal's form:

$$\max \sum_{j=1}^{n} 10^{n-j} x_j \quad\text{s.t.}\quad 2\sum_{jFrom the origin, Dantzig's rule visits all $2^n$ vertices: exactly $2^n - 1$ pivots.

The cube is deformed so that each coordinate's "high" and "low" faces are nearly parallel, and the largest-coefficient choice always starts the long way round.

Bland's counts on this family satisfy $p_n = p_{n-1} + p_{n-2} + 1$, a Fibonacci recurrence, so they grow like $\varphi^n \approx 1.618^n$. That is exponential, just slower. For essentially every deterministic pivoting rule that has been proposed (Dantzig, Bland, steepest edge, greatest improvement, shadow vertex), someone has built a Klee–Minty-style family that forces exponentially many pivots. For randomized rules the best known upper bounds are subexponential (Kalai 1992; Matoušek, Sharir & Welzl), and Friedmann, Hansen & Zwick (2011) proved subexponential lower bounds for some of them.

Why HiGHS needs so few: pricing that measures improvement relative to edge length (next section), and a different starting basis ("crash"), neither of which falls for a construction aimed at Dantzig's rule. Don't read "9 iterations" as "HiGHS is polynomial". Read it as "the lower bound is for one rule".

A related open question: the polynomial Hirsch conjecture, whether the diameter of the vertex graph of every polytope is polynomial in $m$ and $n$. The original Hirsch conjecture (diameter $\le m - n$) was disproved by Santos (2012). If the polynomial version is false, no pivoting rule at all can be polynomial.

02 · failure

So why is simplex the workhorse?

in practice

On real LPs the number of iterations is typically a small multiple of the number of constraints. Worst cases are artificial.

in theory, eventually

Average case (Borgwardt 1982): polynomial expected pivots for the shadow-vertex rule on a random model.

Smoothed analysis (Spielman & Teng 2004): add small Gaussian noise to any LP, and shadow-vertex simplex takes polynomial expected time.

And a structural reason, which is the one that matters for this course: simplex warm-starts. Change the LP slightly and the old basis is nearly optimal. Branch-and-bound (unit 07) and cutting planes (unit 08) re-solve thousands of slightly changed LPs.

Smoothed analysis explains why worst-case constructions are fragile. The Klee–Minty cube relies on precise coefficient ratios, and perturbing them destroys the long path. Spielman and Teng received the Gödel Prize (2008) for it. Later work (Dadush & Huiberts, among others) has tightened the bounds considerably.

The warm-start point is developed in unit 03 (the dual simplex) and unit 04 (why interior-point methods lose it). For a single large, cold LP, interior-point methods are often faster. For a sequence of related LPs, simplex wins, and integer programming is a sequence of related LPs.

section 3

Starting, and what real codes do

Two phases, the revised method, better pricing, and tolerances.

02 · practice

Getting started: two phases

If some $b_i < 0$, the slack basis is infeasible. Phase 1: for each such row, negate it and add an artificial $a_i \ge 0$ with coefficient $+1$, then solve

$$\max\ -\textstyle\sum_i a_i$$

starting from the basis of slacks and artificials, which is feasible by construction.

  • Optimum $< 0$: the original LP is infeasible. The phase-1 duals give a Farkas certificate, as in unit 01.
  • Optimum $= 0$: pivot remaining artificials out of the basis, drop them, restore the true objective. Phase 2 continues from that feasible basis.
big-M, and why not

Alternative: one phase with objective $c^\top x - M\sum a_i$ for a huge $M$. It is simple to state and numerically poor, because $M$ mixes magnitudes in the reduced costs. The same disease comes back in unit 05 as big-M constraints.

redundant rows

An artificial stuck in the basis at 0 whose row has no nonzero real entry means that row is a combination of the others. Delete it. The lab's step 4 test includes one.

The phase-1 objective row must be "priced out" before it is a valid dictionary. The artificials are basic, so their reduced costs must be zero: subtract each artificial's row from the objective row. Forgetting this is the most common phase-1 bug, and hint rung 2 of step 4 is about it.

Connection to unit 01: phase 1 is an LP whose optimum is 0 exactly when the original system is feasible. When the optimum is negative, the optimal dual solution of phase 1 is a Farkas vector for the original system. So LP software proves infeasibility with the same object you built by Fourier–Motzkin, obtained much more cheaply.

Real codes start differently. A "crash" procedure picks a starting basis with as many structural variables as possible, and the dual simplex method starts from a dual feasible basis instead, which is often easier to find.

02 · practice

The revised simplex method

The tableau stores $A_B^{-1}[A \mid I]$, which is dense, $m \times (n+m)$, and is updated in full at every pivot. A pivot actually needs only:

  1. $y^\top = c_B^\top A_B^{-1}$: one solve with $A_B^\top$ (BTRAN);
  2. reduced costs $\bar c_j = c_j - y^\top a_j$ for the candidates (pricing);
  3. $d = A_B^{-1} a_j$ for the chosen column: one solve (FTRAN);
  4. the ratio test, then an update of the factorization of $A_B$.
in a real solver
  • $A_B$ is kept as sparse LU factors (Markowitz ordering), not an inverse.
  • Each pivot updates the factors (Forrest–Tomlin), and periodically they are refactorized from scratch to control fill-in and error growth.
  • Real LPs are very sparse, so solves cost far less than $m^2$.

For a sparse LP with $m = 10^5$ rows, a dense tableau would have $10^{10}$ entries. The revised method never forms it. Each iteration costs two triangular solves with the LU factors plus pricing, and pricing can itself be partial (look at only some columns) when $n$ is huge.

Refactorization is a numerical safeguard as much as a speed one. Updates accumulate rounding error, and a fresh LU with threshold pivoting resets it. Solvers also monitor the primal and dual residuals ($\|A_B x_B - b\|$, etc.) and refactorize early when they drift.

The lab builds the dense tableau on purpose, because it makes every quantity visible. The comparison with HiGHS in then is between the textbook object and the engineered one.

02 · practice

Pricing: which column enters

Dantzig: the largest $\bar c_j$. It measures improvement per unit of $x_j$, so it depends on the units $x_j$ is measured in.

Steepest edge (Goldfarb & Reid 1977): the largest improvement per unit of distance along the edge,

$$\max_j \frac{\bar c_j}{\|\eta_j\|}, \qquad \eta_j = \begin{pmatrix} -A_B^{-1} a_j \\ e_j \end{pmatrix}$$

where $\eta_j$ is the edge direction in the space of all variables.

what it buys

Typically far fewer pivots, and much less sensitivity to how variables are scaled.

what it costs

The norms $\|\eta_j\|$ must be kept current, which takes an extra solve per pivot with clever update formulas. Devex (Harris 1973) approximates them cheaply.

This is the one-sentence answer the checkpoint asks for.

Why scaling fools Dantzig: substitute $x_j = 1000\,x'_j$. The column and the cost of $x'_j$ are multiplied by 1000, so $\bar c'_j = 1000\,\bar c_j$ and Dantzig now strongly prefers $x'_j$, although the geometry is unchanged. Steepest edge divides by the edge length, which scales with the column, and so it is far less affected.

Empirically, dual steepest edge in the dual simplex is the default in most commercial and open codes for good reason: on benchmark sets it reduces iteration counts substantially compared with Dantzig pricing, enough to more than pay for the extra work per iteration. Forrest & Goldfarb (1992), "Steepest-edge simplex algorithms for linear programming", is the classic study.

02 · practice

Tolerances, and what they hide

Floating-point simplex needs tolerances: a reduced cost counts as negative only below $-10^{-7}$, a pivot element as usable only above some threshold, a constraint as satisfied within $10^{-7}$ or so.

The lab's then solves $Hx = H\mathbf{1}$ with $H$ the Hilbert matrix. The only solution is $x = \mathbf 1$, objective $n$:

the lesson

At $n = 8$ both report objective $\approx 8.000$, so the value looks right. The point is wrong by more than 1 in some coordinate.

A wrong $x$ at the right value satisfies every constraint to within tolerance. From the solver's side nothing failed.

The Hilbert matrix is the standard ill-conditioned example: its condition number grows like $e^{3.5n}$. Once the condition number times machine epsilon ($\approx 10^{-16}$) reaches the feasibility tolerance ($\approx 10^{-7}$), points far from the true solution satisfy the constraints "within tolerance". Here that happens at $n \approx 7$–$8$.

Real LPs are rarely this badly conditioned, but badly scaled models (big-M constraints with $M = 10^6$ next to coefficients of $10^{-3}$) create the same effect. Solvers scale the model internally, and HiGHS and Gurobi both report warnings about coefficient ranges. Unit 27 comes back to modelling habits that avoid this.

Exact rational LP solvers exist (QSopt_ex, SoPlex in exact mode), and they are what exact verification of MIP results relies on. They typically solve in floating point first and then repair and verify the basis in rational arithmetic.
