Benders decomposition

Fix the hard variables, let the easy ones' duals write an inequality about the choice, and repeat. Row generation — the mirror image of unit 10.

needs 10~1¾ h slides + 2½ h lablab: uv run co lab 12

Jacques Benders published the method in 1962 for mixed-integer programs: integer variables in a master problem, continuous variables in an LP subproblem. Its natural home turned out to be stochastic programming. A first-stage decision is followed by many scenarios, each a separate LP once the first stage is fixed.

The unit has one conceptual goal, the duality with Dantzig–Wolfe, and one practical lesson from the lab. Whether Benders beats the monolithic model depends on how many iterations it needs, and that depends on the instance, not only on its size.

How to read this deck

One tiny stochastic facility problem (two facilities, one customer, two demand scenarios) runs through section 1: its value function for every first-stage choice, the cuts read off the duals, and the three iterations of the loop. Every number is from the lab's reference solution.

Splitting

A hard decision, then an easy one.

12 · splitting

The value function of the easy part

$$\begin{aligned} & \min_{y \in Y}\ f^\top y + Q(y) \\ & Q(y) = \min_{x \ge 0} \{\, c^\top x : Wx \ge h - Ty \,\} \end{aligned}$$

$y$: the complicating variables (integer, few). For fixed $y$ the inner problem, the recourse, is an LP, often separable into blocks. Its value is the value function $Q(y)$.

by LP duality (unit 03) $$\begin{aligned} Q(y) &= \max_{\pi \in \Pi}\ \pi^\top (h - Ty) \\ \Pi &= \{\pi \ge 0 : W^\top \pi \le c\} \end{aligned}$$

The dual polyhedron $\Pi$ doesn't depend on $y$. So $Q$ is the maximum of finitely many affine functions (one per vertex of $\Pi$): convex and piecewise linear. If $Q(y) = \infty$, an extreme ray of $\Pi$ certifies it.

The lab's facility location, for scenario $s$:

$$\begin{aligned} Q_s(y) = \min\ & \textstyle\sum c_{ij} x_{ij} + p \sum z_j \\ & \textstyle\sum_i x_{ij} + z_j \ge d_{sj} && (v_j) \\ & \textstyle\sum_j x_{ij} \le u_i y_i && (\alpha_i) \end{aligned}$$ $$Q_s(y) = \max_{v, \alpha}\ \textstyle\sum_j d_{sj} v_j - \sum_i u_i \alpha_i\, y_i$$

Dual feasibility: $v_j - \alpha_i \le c_{ij}$, $0 \le v_j \le p$, $\alpha \ge 0$. None of it involves $y$.

Compare with unit 11, where the Lagrangian function was a minimum of affine functions (concave) and was maximised. Here the value function is a maximum of affine functions (convex) and is minimised. Benders builds $Q$ from below with its affine pieces, one per cut, just as Kelley's cutting-plane method builds a convex function from below.

The whole method rests on "$\Pi$ doesn't depend on $y$". A dual solution computed at one $y$ is still dual feasible at every other $y$, so its objective is a valid lower bound on $Q$ everywhere. That's the optimality cut two slides on.

12 · splitting

Optimality and feasibility cuts

optimality cut

Solve at $\bar y$ and take an optimal dual $\bar\pi$. For every $y$:

$$\theta \ge \bar\pi^\top (h - Ty)$$

It's tight at $\bar y$, and valid elsewhere by weak duality.

feasibility cut

If the LP at $\bar y$ is infeasible, a ray $\bar r$ of $\Pi$ has $\bar r^\top (h - T\bar y) > 0$. Every feasible $y$ needs

$$\bar r^\top (h - Ty) \le 0$$

In practice: solve the phase-one LP $\min \sum z$, whose duals are bounded ($v \le 1$). A positive value gives the ray.

Lab step 2's tests, on every $y \in \{0, 1\}^F$:

  • each optimality cut equals $Q_s(\bar y)$ at $\bar y$ and stays below $Q_s(y)$, computed independently by scipy, at every $y$;
  • each feasibility cut excludes its $\bar y$ and keeps every $y$ at which the scenario is feasible.
complete recourse

A penalty for unmet demand makes every $y$ feasible, so feasibility cuts disappear. That's common modelling practice. Without it, $y = 0$ is the master's first answer and every scenario returns a feasibility cut.

For the facility problem the simplest feasibility cut is visible by hand. Take $v = 1$ for every customer and $\alpha = 1$ for every facility: the cut is $\sum_j d_{sj} \le \sum_i u_i y_i$, total capacity at least total demand. The phase-one duals give that cut or a sharper one restricted to a subset of customers.

Why the phase-one LP instead of a ray: LP solvers report dual rays inconsistently, and some only after a re-solve with presolve off (unit 03's HiGHS lesson). The phase-one LP is always feasible and bounded, so its duals are an ordinary optimal solution.

12 · splitting · example

Cuts from duals, with numbers

scenario 2 at "open both"

Demand 8: A serves 5, B serves 3. The duals price what one more unit would change:

  • $v = 3$: one more unit of demand would come from B, at cost 3.
  • $\alpha_A = 2$: one more unit of A's capacity would replace a B unit, saving $3 - 1 = 2$.
  • $\alpha_B = 0$: B has spare capacity.

Cut $\theta_2 \ge d\,v - 5\alpha_A y_A - 5\alpha_B y_B = 24 - 10\,y_A$.

Check it: at "both" it gives 14 $= Q_2$, tight. At "A only", 14 $\le 35$; at "B only", 24 $\le 45$; at "none", 24 $\le 80$. Valid everywhere, as promised.

scenario 1 at "open none"

Everything unmet: $v = 10$, and each unit of capacity would save $10 - 1 = 9$ at A, $10 - 3 = 7$ at B.

Cut $\theta_1 \ge 40 - 45\,y_A - 35\,y_B$: exact at "none" (40), but weak elsewhere: $-5$ at "A only", $5$ at "B only", $-40$ at "both", where the true values are 4, 12, 4.

without the penalty: a feasibility cut

Drop the unmet-demand option. At "A only", scenario 2 needs 8 but has capacity 5: infeasible. The phase-one LP gives $v = 1$, $\alpha_A = \alpha_B = 1$:

$8 - 5y_A - 5y_B \le 0$: "open at least 8 units of capacity". It removes "A only", "B only" and "none" at once.

Dual feasibility of the two dual solutions, against $v - \alpha_i \le c_i$ and $0 \le v \le 10$: at "both", $3 - 2 = 1 \le 1$ and $3 - 0 \le 3$; at "none", $10 - 9 = 1 \le 1$ and $10 - 7 = 3 \le 3$. Both are tight, which is why they are optimal. And since the feasible duals don't depend on $y$, the same numbers give valid cuts at every $y$.

The feasibility cut. With no penalty, the phase-one LP minimises unmet demand $z$. Its optimum at "A only" is $z = 3$; the duals $v = 1$ (one more unit of demand, one more unmet) and $\alpha = 1$ (one more unit of capacity, one fewer unmet) give $8 \cdot 1 - 5 \cdot 1 \cdot y_A - 5 \cdot 1 \cdot y_B \le 0$. The reference feasibility_cut returns const 8 and coefficients $(-5, -5)$; at "both" it returns nothing.

All four cuts come from the reference second_stage and optimality_cut.

The loop, and why it ends

The master problem over $y$ and $\theta$ with the cuts so far:

$$\min f^\top y + \theta \quad \text{s.t. cuts},\ y \in Y$$
  1. Solve the master: $\text{LB} = $ its value, giving $\bar y$.
  2. Solve the subproblem(s) at $\bar y$: add a feasibility or optimality cut.
  3. If feasible, $\text{UB} = \min(\text{UB},\ f^\top \bar y + Q(\bar y))$.
  4. Stop when $\text{UB} - \text{LB} \le \varepsilon$ (the Benders bounds meet).
on the example (one cut per scenario)
itmaster picksLBUBcuts added
0none060$\theta_1 \ge 40 - 45y_A - 35y_B$, $\ \theta_2 \ge 80 - 45y_A - 35y_B$
1A, B1625$\theta_1 \ge 4$, $\theta_2 \ge 24 - 10y_A$

It always ends: there are only finitely many dual vertices, hence finitely many cuts (finite convergence).

The master is a MIP that gains cuts every iteration and is re-solved each time. That re-solve is the method's cost centre, and section 4 is mostly about avoiding it.

explore interactively

The example, iteration by iteration. Iteration 0: the master has no cuts and $\theta \ge 0$, so it opens nothing: LB 0, and the true cost of "none" is 60. Iteration 1: with the two cuts from "none", opening both makes both cuts' right-hand sides at most zero ($-40$ and $0$), so the master pays only 16 for opening; "A only" would pay $10 + \tfrac12 \cdot 0 + \tfrac12 \cdot 35 = 27.5$. LB 16, true cost 25. Iteration 2: now "both" costs $16 + \tfrac12 \cdot 4 + \tfrac12 \cdot 14 = 25$, "A only" costs $10 + 2 + 17.5 = 29.5$, "B only" $6 + 2.5 + 22.5 = 31$. LB 25 = UB: optimal. The single-cut version takes the same three iterations here.

Why it ends, in full: $\Pi$ has finitely many vertices and extreme rays, so there are finitely many distinct cuts. A cut regenerated at an unchanged $\bar y$ means the master's $\theta$ already equals $Q(\bar y)$, and then LB = UB.

The lower bounds are monotone because the master only gains constraints. The upper bounds are not monotone in raw form, so the lab records the best seen, as in unit 10's Farley bound. The tests check both sequences and that the final gap is closed.

One subtlety the tests catch: the master's θ must start bounded below. With nonnegative costs, $\theta \ge 0$ is valid, and the lab uses it. Without any lower bound the first master is unbounded.

the duality

For an LP, Benders on the primal is Dantzig–Wolfe on its dual. The transpose of the second matrix has the shape of the first: linking variables become linking constraints, and generated rows become generated columns.

where unit 03 sits

DW: reduced costs $c - \pi^\top A_j$ decide which column enters.
Benders: dual solutions $\pi$ of the subproblem are the cut coefficients.
In both, the same LP duality moves information between master and subproblem, in opposite directions.

That is the curriculum's checkpoint. A check that you've got it: write the LP $\min\{c^\top x + d^\top y : Ay \ge b,\ Tx + Wy \ge h\}$. Apply Benders with $y$ as master variables. Now dualise the whole LP and apply Dantzig–Wolfe with the dual constraints corresponding to $x$ as the block. The restricted master of one is the dual of the relaxed master of the other, iteration by iteration.

For integer $y$ the symmetry breaks. Benders keeps integrality in the master, which is exactly why the method was invented. Dantzig–Wolfe keeps it in the pricing problem, which is what gives unit 10 its strong bound.

Stochastic programming

Decide now, adapt later, pay the expectation.

12 · stochastic programming

Two-stage recourse and the L-shaped method

$$\min_{y \in Y}\ f^\top y + \sum_{s=1}^S p_s\, Q_s(y)$$

A two-stage stochastic program. The deterministic equivalent (extensive form) copies the second-stage variables once per scenario: size $\times S$, one MIP. Given $y$, the scenarios decouple into $S$ independent LPs.

  • L-shaped method (Van Slyke & Wets 1969): Benders with one aggregated cut $\theta \ge \sum_s p_s(\cdot)$.
  • Multi-cut (Birge & Louveaux 1988): one $\theta_s$ and one cut per scenario. It passes more information, so fewer iterations, but the master is bigger.
  • The scenario LPs are embarrassingly parallel.
where scenarios come from

Sample average approximation (Kleywegt, Shapiro & Homem-de-Mello 2002): draw $S$ samples, solve, and repeat with fresh samples to estimate the optimality gap statistically. More scenarios means a better estimate and a bigger model, which is exactly the regime where decomposition should win.

The expected value of perfect information and the value of the stochastic solution (EVPI, VSS) measure whether solving the stochastic model was worth it. Birge & Louveaux ch. 4.

The lab's instances draw scenario demands as the base demand times a log-normal factor (σ = 0.35) and add a penalty of 50 per unit unmet. They are small enough that the extensive form is always solvable, which is what lets the then step measure a crossover instead of asserting one.

  • Tight capacities (1.6× demand): Benders wins from $S = 5$. A handful of cuts settles which facilities must open.
  • Loose capacities (3×): many near-equivalent facility sets, 20–60 iterations. The extensive form wins until single-cut overtakes it between $S = 50$ and $100$.

The crossover depends on iterations × cost per iteration, and the instance family controls the first factor. Report it as a plot per family, never as one number.

The numbers are noisy: tight, S = 100, multi-cut took 8.5 s where S = 200 took 1.9 s. Each master is a fresh MIP solve whose time varies with the cut set. The printed log-scale plot in then shows the spread better than the table.

The extensive-form times grow roughly linearly in S here because HiGHS's presolve and root LP handle the block structure well. On harder second stages (integer recourse, thousands of scenarios) the extensive form stops fitting, and decomposition is the only option.

section 4

Modern Benders

Stop re-solving the master.

12 · modern

One tree, and better cuts

branch-and-Benders-cut

Solve the master once, with a solver callback. Whenever branch-and-bound finds an integer (or even fractional) $\bar y$, solve the subproblems and add violated cuts as lazy constraints. No re-solving, and one search tree. Fischetti, Ljubić & Sinnl (2017) solved facility location instances with thousands of customers this way.

LP first (McDaniel & Devine 1977)

Generate cuts at the master's LP relaxation first. They're cheap, and valid for integer $y$. Lab then, loose, S = 25: integer iterations 21 → 13, but no wall-time gain in this re-solving implementation.

Pareto-optimal cuts (Magnanti & Wong 1981)

When the subproblem's dual is degenerate, many cuts are tight at $\bar y$. Choose the one highest at a core point $y^0$ in the relative interior of $\mathrm{conv}(Y)$, which needs one extra LP. Lab: on this capacitated family, the same iteration count and slower. Degeneracy is rare here, and the classic gains are on uncapacitated location.

beyond LP subproblems

Combinatorial Benders (Codato & Fischetti 2006): no-good cuts from infeasible subsets. Logic-based Benders (Hooker & Ottosson 2003): any subproblem, CP or SAT included, as long as it returns an inference about the master's choice. It returns in unit 21.

The honest summary of this unit's measurements is that the textbook accelerations did little on this instance family, and the structural choice (multi- vs single-cut) mattered more. That's typical. Benders performance depends on the family, and the published speed-ups are measured on families chosen to show them. Lab step 4's degenerate hand instance is where Magnanti–Wong visibly does something: a cut worth 15 at the core point instead of 10.

Other stabilisation for Benders mirrors unit 10: in-out separation (Ben-Ameur & Neto 2007) separates at a point between the master solution and a core point. Partial decomposition (Crainic et al.) keeps a few representative scenarios in the master.
