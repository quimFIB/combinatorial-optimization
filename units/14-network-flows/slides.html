Network flows

Max-flow min-cut, three algorithms and why each exists, costs and potentials, and the skill that matters most: recognizing a flow in disguise.

needs 13~2 h slides + 3 h lablab: uv run co lab 14

Flows are the workhorse of trunk B. Matching (unit 15) is a special case, subtour separation (unit 09) used a cut, and a surprising range of problems reduce to "find a maximum flow" or "find a minimum cut". This unit's checkpoint is about exactly that recognition skill.

Unit 06 already told you the flow LP has integral vertices (its constraint matrix is TU). The algorithms here are fast combinatorial ways to find those vertices, and the max-flow min-cut theorem is LP duality specialized to networks.

14 · max flow

Flows, cuts, and weak duality

Lab step 1's textbook network. Max flow 5. The cut $S = \{s, a, b\}$ has capacity $3 + 2 = 5$, which proves it.

definitions

A flow on a network respects capacities ($0 \le f \le c$) and conservation at every node except $s$ and $t$. Its value is the net flow out of $s$.

An $s$–$t$ cut is a set $S \ni s$, $t \notin S$. Its capacity is the total capacity of arcs leaving $S$.

weak duality

Every flow's value is at most every cut's capacity.

Why: value = (flow out of $S$) − (flow into $S$) ≤ capacity out of $S$.

On the figure: the cut $\{s\}$ has capacity 5, $\{s, a\}$ has $2 + 1 + 3 = 6$, $\{s, a, b\}$ has 5. Any flow is at most 5.

The flow value across any cut is the same: sum the conservation equations over the nodes of $S$, and everything internal cancels. That is the one-line proof of weak duality, and it is unit 00's certificate pattern once more. A cut is a short proof that no flow is larger.

For this network: arcs s→a (3), s→b (2), a→b (1), a→t (3), b→t (2). The flow sends 3 along s→a→t and 2 along s→b→t, so the a→b arc is unused. The cut {s, a, b} crosses a→t and b→t, capacity 5.

14 · max flow

Max-flow min-cut

Ford & Fulkerson 1956

The maximum flow value equals the minimum cut capacity. With integer capacities there is an integral maximum flow.

why, via the residual graph

The residual graph has $u \to v$ with capacity $c - f$ (room to send more) and $v \to u$ with capacity $f$ (room to undo). If $t$ is reachable from $s$, an augmenting path improves the flow. If not, the reachable set $S$ has every leaving arc saturated and every entering arc empty, so the flow equals $S$'s capacity. The next slide does it on the network.

The proof is the algorithm, and it produces the certificate:

  1. While an augmenting path exists, push its bottleneck (the Ford–Fulkerson method).
  2. At the end, the reachable set is a minimum cut. That is lab step 1's min_cut, and the test checks its capacity equals your flow.

It is LP duality (unit 03) for the flow LP, whose dual is the cut LP, with integrality from total unimodularity (unit 06).

explore interactively

Integrality follows from the algorithm: starting from zero flow with integer capacities, every bottleneck is an integer, so every intermediate flow is integral. The TU argument (unit 06) gives the same conclusion without an algorithm. Both are asked for in the checkpoint.

The name "residual graph" hides one important idea: backward arcs let an algorithm undo earlier decisions. Without them, greedy path-pushing gets stuck at non-maximum flows. With them, local improvement is exact, like simplex on a polytope.

The backward arcs are not used on this small network. They matter when an early path was a poor choice: had step 1 used $s \to a \to b \to t$ (bottleneck 1), step 2 could send one unit along $s \to b \to a \to t$, cancelling that use of $a \to b$. 14 · max flow

Choosing paths: Ford–Fulkerson to Edmonds–Karp to Dinic

any augmenting path

With bad path choices, integer capacities can take time proportional to the flow value, so it is not polynomial. With irrational capacities it may never terminate, and may even converge to a non-maximum value (Zwick's golden-ratio example).

Edmonds–Karp (1972)

Shortest augmenting paths by BFS: $O(VE^2)$. The idea: distances from $s$ never shrink, so each arc can be the bottleneck only $O(V)$ times.

full argument in the notes (N)

Dinic (1970)

Group all shortest paths of the same length into one phase: BFS levels, then a blocking flow in the level graph by DFS with current-edge pointers. $O(V)$ phases, $O(VE)$ each, so $O(V^2 E)$ overall.

On unit-capacity networks, $O(E\sqrt V)$. On bipartite matching networks this is Hopcroft–Karp (unit 15).

In lab then, on a 40×40 layered network, Edmonds–Karp took 0.24 s and Dinic 0.057 s.

Full proof of the Edmonds–Karp bound

The Edmonds–Karp argument in one paragraph. Let $d(v)$ be the BFS distance from $s$ in the residual graph. Augmenting along a shortest path never decreases any $d(v)$. When an arc $u \to v$ is a bottleneck it disappears, and it can only come back after flow is pushed on $v \to u$, which needs $d(u) = d(v) + 1$ at that later time. So $d(u)$ has grown by 2. Distances are at most $V$, so each arc is a bottleneck $O(V)$ times, there are $O(VE)$ augmentations, and each costs $O(E)$.

Dinic's current-edge pointer: within a phase, once an edge leads to a dead end or is saturated, it stays useless for the rest of the phase, because levels don't change until the next BFS. Skipping it permanently is what makes the phase $O(VE)$.

14 · max flow

Push–relabel, and the heuristics that make it fast

Push–relabel, Goldberg & Tarjan (1988). No augmenting paths. Maintain a preflow (nodes may have excess) and heights:

  • push excess from $u$ along a residual arc to $v$ with $h(u) = h(v) + 1$;
  • relabel $u$ to $1 + \min h(v)$ over residual neighbours when it can't push.

$O(V^2 E)$ generic, $O(V^3)$ FIFO, $O(V^2\sqrt E)$ with the highest-label rule.

Production push–relabel adds the gap heuristic (if no node has height $k$, lift everything above $k$ at once) and periodic global relabelling (exact heights by a backward BFS from $t$). Without them it climbs deep layered networks one height at a time.

Why heights certify optimality at the end. Valid heights satisfy $h(u) \le h(v) + 1$ on every residual arc, with $h(s) = n$ and $h(t) = 0$. A residual path from $s$ to $t$ would need at least $n$ steps each decreasing height by at most 1, but a simple path has at most $n - 1$ arcs. So no augmenting path exists, and once no excess remains the preflow is a maximum flow. That is lab step 3's "think about".

OR-Tools' SimpleMaxFlow is a push–relabel implementation with these heuristics, written in C++. It is 5–20× faster than the lab's best on every family here, which is the usual factor for a good compiled implementation of the same algorithm.

Dinic and push–relabel, on the example

Dinic: one phase is enough

BFS levels on the network: $s$ 0, $a$ 1, $b$ 1, $t$ 2. The level graph keeps only arcs going one level up: $s \to a$, $s \to b$, $a \to t$, $b \to t$. Arc $a \to b$ joins two level-1 nodes and is dropped.

Blocking flow: DFS sends 3 on $s \to a \to t$, then 2 on $s \to b \to t$. Now every $s$–$t$ path in the level graph has a saturated arc. The next BFS can't reach $t$: done, value 5, in one phase.

On a deep layered network, phases take turns: each one raises the $s$–$t$ distance by at least 1.

explore interactively

The push–relabel run is a logged copy of the reference push_relabel. The source starts at height $n = 4$ and floods both of its arcs. Node $a$ holds 3 but sits at height 0, level with everything, so it relabels to 1 and pushes downhill: first along $a \to b$ (1 unit, the arc order in the lab's adjacency list), then 2 along $a \to t$. Node $b$ now holds 3 but $b \to t$ has capacity 2. After pushing 2, the residual arcs out of $b$ go back to $a$ (height 1, the unit $a$ sent) and to $s$ (height 4), so $b$ relabels to 2 and pushes that unit back, and $a$ sends it on to $t$ through the capacity it still has.

The undo is the whole point of residual arcs, now inside a different algorithm: $a$'s early push to $b$ was a mistake, and heights rising above $a$ are what let $b$ return it. The final flow $(3, 2, 0, 3, 2)$ is the same as Edmonds–Karp's.

Dinic matches the reference dinic: one BFS, a blocking flow of value 5, and a second BFS that fails.

14 · max flow

The theoretical frontier moved recently

  • 1956 Ford–Fulkerson: augmenting paths.
  • 1970–72 Dinic, Edmonds–Karp: polynomial.
  • 1988 Goldberg–Tarjan: push–relabel.
  • 1998 Goldberg–Rao: $\tilde O(\min(E^{1/2}, V^{2/3})\, E \log U)$.
  • 2013 Orlin: $O(VE)$, strongly polynomial.
  • 2022 Chen, Kyng, Liu, Peng, Probst Gutenberg & Sachdeva: almost-linear time, $E^{1+o(1)}$, for max flow and min-cost flow.
why the 2022 result matters here

It solves the min-cost flow LP with an interior-point method (unit 04) whose linear systems are solved by specialized graph data structures. Continuous optimization produced the fastest known combinatorial flow algorithm.

Nobody runs it in practice yet: its constants are enormous. Push–relabel and network simplex remain what solvers ship.

The 2022 paper won the FOCS best paper award. The method is an interior-point method on the min-cost flow LP, run for $m^{1+o(1)}$ iterations, each iteration needing only approximate changes to the solution. A dynamic data structure maintains those changes over a shrinking hierarchy of approximating trees. It is a striking instance of this course's premise that the discrete and the continuous are closely linked.

14 · costs

Min-cost flow

min-cost flow

Send $d$ units from $s$ to $t$ minimizing $\sum_e \text{cost}_e f_e$, subject to capacities.

optimality

A feasible flow is minimum-cost iff its residual graph has no negative-cost cycle.

Algorithms: cycle canceling (Klein 1967: push around negative cycles); network simplex (unit 02, specialized to trees); successive shortest paths (the lab).

successive shortest paths with potentials
  1. Bellman–Ford once for potentials $\pi$.
  2. Repeat: Dijkstra on reduced costs $c_\pi(u,v) = c(u,v) + \pi(u) - \pi(v) \ge 0$; update $\pi \mathrel{+}= \text{dist}$; augment along the shortest path.

Reverse arcs carry cost $-c$, but reduced costs stay nonnegative, so Dijkstra stays valid. The potentials are exactly the dual variables of the min-cost flow LP.

Strongly polynomial algorithms exist (Tardos 1985; Orlin 1993).

Why reduced costs stay nonnegative. After Dijkstra, $\text{dist}(v) \le \text{dist}(u) + c_\pi(u,v)$ for every residual arc, so the new reduced cost $c_\pi(u,v) + \text{dist}(u) - \text{dist}(v) \ge 0$. Arcs on the shortest path have new reduced cost exactly 0, so their reverse arcs, created by augmenting, also have reduced cost 0.

A detail from building the lab: a lazy-deletion Dijkstra that re-inserts nodes when their distance improves still returns correct answers without potentials, as long as there is no negative cycle. It becomes a label-correcting search, and its running time can blow up. The mutation check found this: removing the potentials passed every test and ran out of memory on a random search. So potentials are what make it efficient, not only correct.

Complementary slackness (unit 03) for flows: an arc with positive reduced cost carries no flow, an arc with negative reduced cost is saturated, and arcs strictly in between have reduced cost 0. The potentials at the end of successive shortest paths satisfy exactly this, which certifies optimality.

Send 3 units from $s$ to $t$ as cheaply as possible. The routes: $s\,a\,b\,t$ costs 3 per unit (room 1), $s\,b\,t$ costs 5 (room 2), $s\,a\,t$ costs 6 (room 1).

two rounds

Start. Bellman–Ford from $s$: $\pi = (0, 1, 2, 3)$ for $(s, a, b, t)$. Reduced costs $c + \pi(u) - \pi(v)$: $sa$ 0, $ab$ 0, $bt$ 0, $sb$ 2, $at$ 3. All $\ge 0$.

Round 1. Dijkstra finds $s\,a\,b\,t$ at reduced length 0, i.e. true cost 3. Send its bottleneck, 1 unit ($a \to b$ fills). Cost so far 3.

Round 2. Distances $(0, 0, 2, 2)$, new $\pi = (0, 1, 4, 5)$. Now $sb$ and $bt$ have reduced cost 0, $at$ has 1: path $s\,b\,t$, true cost 5, send 2. Total $3 + 10 = 13$.

Each round takes the cheapest remaining route, exactly as the table of routes suggests, and Dijkstra never meets a negative arc.

Round 2 in detail. After round 1 the residual graph has backward arcs $a \to s$, $b \to a$, $t \to b$ with negative costs $-1, -1, -1$. With the potentials $(0, 1, 2, 3)$ their reduced costs are $-1 + 1 - 0 = 0$, $-1 + 2 - 1 = 0$, $-1 + 3 - 2 = 0$: not negative, so Dijkstra is safe. From $s$: $a$ at 0 (via $sa$, still 1 unit of room), $b$ at 2 (via $sb$), $t$ at 2 (via $b \to t$), or $0 + 3 = 3$ via $a \to t$. So $t$ is reached at 2 through $b$.

Checking the optimum. Three units must leave $s$; the cheap route has room for 1, and the next cheapest, $s\,b\,t$ at 5, has room for 2. $3 \cdot 1 + 5 \cdot 2 = 13$. The reference min_cost_flow returns cost 13 with flow $(1, 2, 1, 0, 3)$ on $(sa, sb, ab, at, bt)$.

Project selection: a maximum closure is a minimum cut

Two projects both need tool $q$. Min cut: cut $q \to t$ (6), with source side $\{s, p_1, p_2, q\}$. Best profit $= 13 - 6 = 7$.

Project selection: choose projects, each with a profit (possibly negative), closed under "requires".

  • $s \to p$ with capacity profit, for positive profits;
  • $p \to t$ with capacity $-$profit, for negative ones;
  • $p \to q$ with capacity $\infty$ if $p$ requires $q$.

A finite cut can't cut an $\infty$ arc, so its source side is a closed set. Its capacity is (profit forgone) + (costs paid), so

$$\text{best profit} = \textstyle\sum \text{positive profits} - \text{min cut}.$$

Picard (1976): maximum-weight closure. Lab step 5.

Check the figure's alternatives: take nothing (profit 0, cut 13); take only $q$ (not useful); take $p_1$ and $q$ (8 − 6 = 2); take all three (13 − 6 = 7). The minimum cut capacity 6 corresponds to "take all three", and $13 - 6 = 7$ matches.

Many real problems are closures in disguise: open-pit mining (a block can be mined only if the blocks above it are), selecting features with prerequisite features, choosing which dependencies of a software build to include.

14 · reductions

More flows in disguise

bipartite matching

$s$ → left (capacity 1), left → right along edges (1), right → $t$ (1). Max flow = max matching, and the min cut gives König's vertex cover. Unit 15 does it faster.

baseball elimination (Schwartz 1966)

Can team $x$ still finish first? Give $x$ all its remaining wins. For each remaining game between other teams, a node with capacity = games left, feeding into the two teams; each team → $t$ with capacity (wins $x$ can reach − wins so far). $x$ is still alive iff the max flow saturates every game arc.

image segmentation

Pixels as nodes; $s$/$t$ arcs weighted by how foreground/background each pixel looks; neighbour arcs weighted by similarity. The minimum cut is the best labelling (Greig, Porteous & Seheult 1989; graph cuts in vision).

scheduling with deadlines

Jobs → time slots within their windows, with capacities for processing time and machine count. A flow saturating all jobs is a feasible schedule.

The recognition questions to ask of any new problem:

  • Is there a quantity that moves and is conserved?
  • Are the decisions "cut or not cut" with pairwise costs? If so, try a minimum cut.
  • Are the constraints "at most $c$ from this group", with integer answers wanted? Then flow integrality (unit 06) gives integrality for free.

Baseball elimination is the checkpoint exercise. It is worth doing before reading Kleinberg & Tardos §7.12, because the reduction's elegance is in how the capacities encode "how many more wins can each team afford".
