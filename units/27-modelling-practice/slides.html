Modelling for a real solver

What presolve does and what defeats it, symmetry, big-M against indicators, starts, reading the log, and the discovery that most single-run speed-ups are noise.

needs 26, 08~1½ h slides + 2½ h lablab: uv run co lab 27

Parts I–V built algorithms. Part VI is about using the industrial ones well. The same problem can be written in ways that differ by orders of magnitude in solve time, and a solver's log is the instrument that tells you which parts of the solve are struggling.

The unit's measured finding comes out of the lab and is worth stating first: on ten instances, only two of six interventions had effects larger than SCIP's run-to-run variation. That's not a failure of the other four. It's the reason unit 28 exists.

The running example: five items, bins of 5

Sizes $3, 3, 2, 2, 2$ (total 12), capacity 5. Any bin holds a 3 and a 2, or two 2s, or one item.

Here the heuristic and the bounds already agree: 3 is proved without any search.

three models, three LP bounds
modelLP
A: capacity constraints, separate $x \le y$1.0
B: capacity linked, $\text{load} \le 5\,y$2.4
F: arc flow2.5

A's LP spreads every item as ⅕ over five bins, each bin open only ⅕. B's LP is the area bound $12/5$. Arc flow's 2.5 rounds up to the optimum: the round-up property.

The LP values are SCIP on the reference assignment_model(sizes, 5, 5, linked=..., vtype="C") and arcflow_model(sizes, 5, vtype="C"); the integer versions all return 3. FFD and lower_bound_l2 are the reference functions.

Why model A's LP is 1. Its capacity constraints don't mention $y$, and $x_{ib} \le y_b$ only forces $y_b$ to be at least the largest fraction of any item in bin $b$. Spreading each item as $\tfrac15$ over five bins satisfies every capacity constraint and needs $y_b = \tfrac15$: total 1. Whatever the instance, that trick gives exactly 1.

Why arc flow's LP is 2.5. It can't split an item between bins, only split whole bin patterns: two units of flow along "3 then 2" and half a unit along "2, one unit of waste, 2" put exactly 2 units on size-3 arcs and 3 on size-2 arcs, at a cost of 2.5 bins.

27 · presolve

What presolve does, and what defeats it

reductionexample
bound tightening$x + 10y \le 12$, $y \in \{0,1\}$ ⇒ $x \le 12$
coefficient tightening$x \le 1000y$ with $x \le 50$ ⇒ $x \le 50y$
dual fixinga variable that only hurts the objective and only helps constraints → its bound
aggregation$x = y + z$ substituted away
probingfix a binary both ways and learn implications
clique detectionpairwise conflicts merged into one constraint

Achterberg et al., "Presolve reductions in mixed integer programming", INFORMS JoC 32 (2020).

modelling habits that defeat it
  • Big-M larger than it needs to be. Presolve can tighten some; it can't know your data's true range.
  • Unbounded continuous variables feeding big-M constraints: nothing to tighten from.
  • Redundant formulations that hide structure, like one aggregated constraint in place of many disaggregated ones (unit 05).
  • Numerically wide coefficients (1e-6 next to 1e6): tolerances start to bite, and cuts get rejected.
indicator constraints instead of big-M

$y = 1 \Rightarrow a^\top x \le b$, stated as such. The solver chooses between big-M reformulation (with the tightest M it can derive), branching on the implication, or SOS1. It's often better, sometimes worse. Measure.

Unit 05 met the aggregated and disaggregated facility-location models, and big-M. The lab here meets the same thing in bin packing: capacity constraints linked to y (strong) against separate x ≤ y constraints (weak). The weak model's LP bound is exactly 1 bin, whatever the instance.

Why "sometimes worse" for indicators: branching on an implication gives no LP bound at all until the binary is fixed, while a well-chosen big-M does contribute to the relaxation. The best choice depends on whether M can be made small.

27 · presolve · example

Two reductions, on a two-constraint model

$$\begin{aligned} & x + 10\,y \le 12 \\ & x \le 1000\,y \\ & 0 \le x \le 50,\quad y \in \{0, 1\} \end{aligned}$$
step 1: bound tightening

Constraint 1 with $y \ge 0$ gives $x \le 12 - 10y \le 12$. The bound $x \le 50$ becomes $x \le 12$.

step 2: coefficient tightening

Constraint 2 only matters when $y = 0$, where it says $x \le 0$. When $y = 1$ it allows 1000, but $x$ can't exceed 12 anyway. So 1000 can shrink to 12: $x \le 12\,y$, the same integer solutions.

what it changes for the LP

The point $x = 11$, $y = 0.011$ satisfies the original constraints: $11 + 0.11 \le 12$ and $11 \le 11$. After step 2 it needs $11 \le 0.132$: cut off.

Same integer points, a much tighter relaxation. That is the whole value of presolve, and why the tightening in step 2 needed step 1's bound first.

If $x$ had no upper bound (the "unbounded continuous variable" habit), constraint 1 still supplies $x \le 12$. Without it as well, nothing bounds $x$, and the 1000 has to stay.

The example is built by hand to isolate the two reductions; there is no solver run to quote. A real presolve would also note that constraint 1 makes $y = 1$ force $x \le 2$, and could derive the stronger pair $x \le 12 - 10y$ as the variable bound. Probing does exactly that: fix $y$ to 0 and to 1, propagate, and record what $x$ can be in each case.

The same pattern is lab step 1's two bin-packing models: "load ≤ 5" plus "x ≤ y" against "load ≤ 5y". The second is the coefficient-tightened version of the first, and its LP bound on the running example is 2.4 instead of 1.

section 2

Symmetry

The single biggest lever on many models.

27 · symmetry

Interchangeable objects cost $k!$

In the assignment model (symmetry), any permutation of the bins maps a packing to another packing of the same cost. With 17 bins, every solution has up to 17! ≈ 3.6 × 10¹⁴ copies, and branch and bound can prove infeasibility of a subtree only by visiting its copies too.

three ways to stop paying
  • Symmetry-breaking constraints (lex-leader, Crawford et al. 1996): keep one representative per orbit. Lab: $y_b \ge y_{b+1}$, item $i$ only in bins $0..i$.
  • Orbitopes (Kaibel & Pfetsch 2008): the convex hull of lexicographically sorted 0/1 matrices, with a propagation algorithm.
  • Orbital branching and fixing (Ostrowski et al. 2011): detect the symmetry group, branch on orbits, fix variables in the same orbit.
the interaction

Modern solvers detect symmetry themselves (SCIP: misc/usesymmetry). Explicit constraints can duplicate that work, or conflict with the solver's choice of representative.

explore interactively

A better model removes the symmetry at the source. The arc-flow and pattern formulations don't have bin identities at all: a solution is a multiset of patterns. That's why section 3's reformulation was the one intervention with a large, stable effect.

Why the lab's symmetry breaking is valid: renumber the bins of any packing by their smallest item. Then used bins come first, and the bin holding item i has number at most i. Every orbit keeps at least one member, so the optimum is unchanged, and the tests check exactly that.

The 12 packings. Either both 3s get a 2 and one 2 is alone (3 choices of the lone 2, times 2 ways to pair the others: 6), or one 3 gets a 2, the other 3 is alone, and the two remaining 2s share (2 choices of 3, times 3 choices of its partner: 6).

Tell the solver what you know.

27 · what you know

Bounds, starts, and a different model

  • Upper bound → smaller model (the bounded model). First-fit decreasing packs into at most $\frac{11}{9}\mathrm{OPT} + \frac69$ bins, so no more bins than that are needed.
  • Lower bound → fixings. Martello–Toth L2 ≥ ⌈Σ/C⌉. With symmetry broken, bins $0..L_2-1$ must be used.
  • MIP start. A feasible solution before the first node: an incumbent for pruning, a seed for improvement heuristics. Solution pools keep several.
  • Reformulation. Arc flow (Valério de Carvalho 1999): one path per bin through capacities 0..C, pseudo-polynomial size. Its LP equals Gilmore–Gomory's pattern LP (unit 10).

explore interactively

Pseudo-polynomial is the catch. The arc-flow model has O(C × distinct sizes) arcs, so C = 100 is tiny and C = 10⁹ is impossible. Column generation (unit 10) keeps the same LP with an implicit column set, which is why branch-and-price is the standard exact method for large-capacity cutting stock.

The round-up property: for almost all practical bin-packing instances the optimum equals the pattern LP rounded up, so the LP bound is enough to prove optimality once a good packing is found. That's what "median nodes 1" measures.

27 · what you know · example

The arc-flow graph of the example

A bin is a path from 0 to 5: its item arcs are its contents, loss arcs (along the line) are empty space. Thick: two bins "3 then 2". Dashed: one bin "waste 1, then 2, then 2".

reading the optimal flow

2 units along $0 \xrightarrow{3} 3 \xrightarrow{2} 5$ and 1 unit along $0 \to 1 \xrightarrow{2} 3 \xrightarrow{2} 5$. Flow out of node 0 is 3: three bins.

Size-3 arcs carry 2 (two items of size 3), size-2 arcs carry $1 + 3 = 4 \ge 3$: the extra unit is a 2-slot left empty.

The graph has 3 + 4 item arcs and 5 loss arcs for capacity 5. At capacity 1000 it has thousands: the pseudo-polynomial size of the previous slide.

No bin labels appear anywhere, so there is no symmetry to break.

explore interactively

The flow is the one SCIP returns for the reference arcflow_model(sizes, 5): f_0_3 = 2, f_3_2 = 3, f_1_2 = 1, loss_0 = 1, $z = 3$. Flow conservation at node 3: in $2 + 1 = 3$, out 3. The integer-flow decomposition into three paths is not unique; the caption shows one.

The LP relaxation of the same model gives 2.5 (first slide of section 1). Since the optimum must be an integer, $\lceil 2.5 \rceil = 3$ is a proof of optimality as soon as FFD's 3-bin packing is known, which is why arc flow needed 1 node on the lab's instances.

diagnosis by pattern
  • Dual bound stuck at the first LP value, many nodes: a weak relaxation. Reformulate or add structure.
  • Incumbent late or never, bound fine: give a start, or check heuristics.
  • Huge node count, gap closing slowly: symmetry, or branching on the wrong variables.
  • Time in presolve or root LP: model size or numerics, before any search.

Lab step 6 parses the statistics report into a dict and checks it against SCIP's API. Its traps: "nodes" appears twice, and "cut pool" isn't a separator.

An example while building the lab: 30 items, linked assignment model, 10 s. The report said first LP value 13.34, final dual bound 13.34, primal bound 15, gap 12.44%, after about 1 350 nodes. That's the "stuck bound" pattern: the LP can't see that bins are discrete, and no cut SCIP found moved it. The arc-flow model's median of 1 node in then is the same diagnosis from the other side.

The log formats change between versions. Parsing by column name rather than position is what keeps a parser working after an upgrade.

what survives

Linking (A → B) and reformulation (→ F). The rest are within noise on ten instances. Two runs of the whole script also disagreed on solved counts (A: 5 then 6; B: 9 then 8), because runs near the time limit flip.

MIP solvers are chaotic in the technical sense (performance variability): a tiny change (a seed, constraint order, a different but equivalent model) changes branching decisions early, and the tree grows differently from there. Lodi & Tramontani (2013) document this across solvers.

MIPLIB 2017 (Gleixner et al. 2021): 1 065 instances, a 240-instance benchmark set, and many still open. The shared reality check. then section 2 runs any MIPLIB files you download.

This slide is the bridge to unit 28. The practical rules: report several seeds (or several permutations of the model), use a shifted geometric mean over many instances rather than totals over a few, and treat any single-instance speed-up under about 2× as unestablished until repeated.

Solver developers use exactly this discipline internally. A new feature is accepted on a large test set with many seeds, and a few percent improvement on the shifted geometric mean is a real result there. On ten instances and one seed it's nothing.
