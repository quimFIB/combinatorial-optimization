03

Duality — the load-bearing unit

Every LP has a shadow LP that bounds it, prices its constraints, certifies its optimum, and re-solves it cheaply after a change.

needs 02~2 h slides + 2½ h lablab: uv run co lab 03

The curriculum calls this unit load-bearing, and asks you to spend longer on it than feels justified. The reason is on the second-to-last slide: cutting planes, column generation, Lagrangian relaxation, Benders, the Hungarian algorithm, primal–dual approximation and KKT are each duality in a different setting. If this unit is solid, those units are applications. If it is shaky, they are seven separate things to memorize.

There are four views of the dual in this unit, and each gets a section:

  • a bound (weak and strong duality);
  • a certificate (complementary slackness);
  • a set of prices (sensitivity);
  • an algorithm (the dual simplex).

03 · overview

Four faces of one idea

1 · a bound

Nonnegative combinations of constraints bound the objective. The best such bound is an LP, and it is exact.

2 · a certificate

A pair $(x, y)$ with equal objectives proves both optimal. Complementary slackness is the pointwise version.

3 · a set of prices

$y_i$ is what one more unit of $b_i$ is worth, exactly, within a computable range.

4 · an algorithm

The dual simplex keeps optimality and restores feasibility. It is the method used after adding a cut or a bound.

The lab follows the same order. Step 1 writes the dual, step 2 reads it from a tableau, step 3 uses it as a certificate, step 4 runs the dual simplex, and step 5 uses it for prices.

section 1

The dual as a bound

Adding up constraints, done as well as possible.

03 · bound

Where the dual comes from

$\max\ 3x + 2y\;$ s.t. $\;x + y \le 4,\ \ 2x + y \le 6,\ \ x, y \ge 0$.

Multiply the rows by $u, v \ge 0$ and add:

$$(u + 2v)\,x + (u + v)\,y \;\le\; 4u + 6v$$

If $u + 2v \ge 3$ and $u + v \ge 2$, the left side is at least $3x + 2y$ for every $x, y \ge 0$. So $4u + 6v$ is an upper bound on the optimum.

The best such bound is itself an LP:

the dual $$\begin{aligned}\min\ & 4u + 6v\\ \text{s.t. } & u + 2v \ge 3\\ & u + v \ge 2\\ & u, v \ge 0\end{aligned}$$

$u = v = 1$ gives the bound $10$, and $x = y = 2$ attains $10$. Both are optimal, and neither needed a solver to check.

This is unit 00's certificate idea in its original setting. There, a matching was a lower bound on vertex cover because we combined constraints (one per matched edge). Here the combination has real-valued weights, and the best weights form an LP.

It is also unit 01's Farkas argument turned into optimization. Farkas says a nonnegative combination of rows can prove $0 \le -1$. Duality says a nonnegative combination can prove "objective $\le$ bound", and the best provable bound is the optimum.

Keep this LP. It is SMALL in the lab and the tableau example in unit 02, and its dual $(1, 1)$ is the first thing lab step 2 checks.

The rules, once

primal (max $c^\top x$)dual (min $b^\top y$)
row $i$: $a_i x \le b_i$$y_i \ge 0$
row $i$: $a_i x \ge b_i$$y_i \le 0$
row $i$: $a_i x = b_i$$y_i$ free
$x_j \ge 0$dual row $j$: $(A^\top y)_j \ge c_j$
$x_j \le 0$dual row $j$: $(A^\top y)_j \le c_j$
$x_j$ freedual row $j$: $(A^\top y)_j = c_j$
how to rebuild it

Multipliers must keep inequalities pointing the right way: $\le$ rows get $y \ge 0$. The combination must dominate the objective on the variable's allowed sign: $x_j \ge 0$ needs coefficient $\ge c_j$.

involution

For a min primal, flip every sign. Then $\text{dual}(\text{dual}(P)) = P$ exactly, and lab step 1 tests this on 40 random LPs.

Don't memorize the table; rebuild it from the bounding argument each time until it becomes automatic. For a $\ge$ row, you need $y_i \le 0$: multiplying $a_i x \ge b_i$ by a nonpositive number gives $y_i a_i x \le y_i b_i$, which points the same way as the $\le$ rows and can be added to them. For a free $x_j$, the combined coefficient must equal $c_j$ exactly. If it were larger, a very negative $x_j$ would make the combined left side smaller than the objective and break the bound.

Most textbooks give the rules for a min primal (Bertsimas & Tsitsiklis table 4.1). They are the same table read backwards, which is exactly why the dual of the dual is the primal.

03 · bound

Weak and strong duality

weak duality

For $\max\{c^\top x : Ax \le b,\, x \ge 0\}$ and its dual: if $x$ and $y$ are feasible, $c^\top x \le b^\top y$.

proof

$c^\top x \le (A^\top y)^\top x = y^\top (Ax) \le y^\top b$, using $x \ge 0$ and then $y \ge 0$. $\square$

strong duality

If the primal has an optimal solution, so does the dual, and the optimal values are equal.

proof, from farkas (unit 01)

For $\varepsilon > 0$, the system $Ax \le b,\ -c^\top x \le -(z^* + \varepsilon),\ x \ge 0$ has no solution. Its Farkas vector $(y, t) \ge 0$ must have $t > 0$, because $Ax \le b$ is feasible. Scaled to $t = 1$, $y$ is dual feasible with $b^\top y < z^* + \varepsilon$.

Filling in the strong duality proof. Farkas for $\{x \ge 0 : Ax \le b,\ -c^\top x \le -(z^*+\varepsilon)\}$ gives $(y, t) \ge 0$ with $y^\top A - t c^\top \ge 0$ and $y^\top b - t (z^* + \varepsilon) < 0$ (the form with $x \ge 0$; see the notes of unit 01's Farkas slide). If $t = 0$, then $y^\top A \ge 0$ and $y^\top b < 0$, which is a certificate that $Ax \le b$, $x \ge 0$ is infeasible, a contradiction. So $t > 0$. Divide by $t$: $A^\top y \ge c$, $y \ge 0$, and $b^\top y < z^* + \varepsilon$. By weak duality $b^\top y \ge z^*$. The dual optimum therefore lies in $[z^*, z^* + \varepsilon)$ for every $\varepsilon$, and since an LP attains its optimum (unit 01, FTLP) it equals $z^*$.

A second proof is constructive: the simplex method terminates (with Bland's rule) at a basis with all reduced costs $\le 0$, and $y^\top = c_B^\top A_B^{-1}$ is then dual feasible with $b^\top y = c^\top x$. That is section 2's reading of the tableau.

Four possible outcomes, not nine

Unbounded primal ⇒ infeasible dual: any dual solution would bound it.

Both infeasible happens, for example with $\max\ x_1$ subject to $x_1 - x_2 \le -1,\ -x_1 + x_2 \le -1$.

In the lab's random general LPs, HiGHS found 246 optimal pairs and 154 unbounded/infeasible pairs out of 400.

The table follows from weak duality (the ✗ in "optimal / unbounded" and "unbounded / unbounded") and strong duality (the ✗ in "optimal / infeasible").

Check the doubly infeasible example: adding the two primal rows gives $0 \le -2$, so the primal is infeasible. The dual is $\min -y_1 - y_2$ subject to $y_1 - y_2 \ge 1$, $-y_1 + y_2 \ge 0$, $y \ge 0$; adding its rows gives $0 \ge 1$, so it is infeasible too. (The second dual row is $\ge 0$ because $x_2$ has cost 0.)

This is why solvers report "infeasible or unbounded" from presolve. Sometimes presolve can see that the dual is infeasible, which leaves both possibilities open for the primal. Unit 02's then met this: HiGHS labelled some unbounded LPs infeasible until presolve was turned off.

section 2

The dual as a certificate

Where the multipliers must be zero, and where they can be read off.

03 · certificate

Complementary slackness

theorem

Feasible $x$ and $y$ are both optimal if and only if

$$y_i\,(b_i - a_i x) = 0 \ \text{ for every row } i, \qquad x_j\,\big((A^\top y)_j - c_j\big) = 0 \ \text{ for every variable } j.$$
proof

$b^\top y - c^\top x = \underbrace{y^\top (b - Ax)}_{\ge 0} + \underbrace{x^\top (A^\top y - c)}_{\ge 0}$. The gap is 0 exactly when every product is 0, and a zero gap means both are optimal by weak duality. $\square$

In words: a constraint with slack has price 0. A variable that is used has zero reduced cost.

Checking takes two matrix–vector products. That is lab step 3.

Each product in the sum is a nonnegative number times a nonnegative number, which is why a zero total forces each one to zero. That identity is the proof of weak duality written as an equation instead of an inequality.

Complementary slackness is often the fastest way to find a dual solution when you already have a primal optimum. The slack rows force some $y_i = 0$, the positive variables force some dual rows to hold with equality, and what remains is a square linear system. Lab step 3's rejection tests include a pair that is primal and dual feasible and fails only this condition: $x = (0,0)$, $y = (1,1)$ on the textbook LP.

It generalizes directly to KKT conditions (unit 29). There, complementarity says an inactive constraint has zero multiplier, which is the same statement with gradients in place of rows.

03 · certificate

Reading the dual off the final tableau

At an optimal basis, $y^\top = c_B^\top A_B^{-1}$, and the reduced cost of every column is $\bar c_j = c_j - y^\top a_j$.

A slack $s_i$ has cost 0 and column $e_i$, so $\bar c_{s_i} = -y_i$. The objective row, which stores $-\bar c$, holds $y_i$ in slack column $i$.

so

The optimal dual solution is the slack columns of the objective row. Every simplex solve returns a primal solution and a dual certificate at once.

Unit 02's final tableau:

y012−12
x10−112
z001110

$y = (1, 1)$, and $b^\top y = 4 + 6 = 10 = c^\top x$.

Dual feasibility is visible in the same row. Structural columns hold $-\bar c_j = (A^\top y)_j - c_j \ge 0$, which is exactly $A^\top y \ge c$, and slack columns hold $y_i \ge 0$. "No negative entries in the objective row" is the optimality test of unit 02 and it is also the dual feasibility test. The primal simplex method keeps primal feasibility and works toward dual feasibility, and the dual simplex method (section 4) does the opposite.

Phase 1 may have negated some rows or deleted redundant ones. That doesn't matter here: reduced costs are properties of columns, and row operations don't change them. Lab hint rung 2 of step 2 says this.

section 3

The dual as prices

What a unit of each resource is worth, and for how long.

03 · prices

Shadow prices

Optimum of the textbook LP as the right-hand side of $x + y \le b_1$ varies. Shaded: the range where the current basis stays optimal.

theorem

If the optimal basis is nondegenerate, $\;\dfrac{\partial z^*}{\partial b_i} = y_i$.

More generally $z^*(b)$ is concave and piecewise linear, and $y$ is a supergradient.

Here $y_1 = 1$ on $b_1 \in [3, 6]$. Below 3, the constraint $2x+y \le 6$ stops binding and the price jumps to 3. Above 6, $x+y \le b_1$ stops binding and the price drops to 0.

The curve was computed with the exact simplex at 17 values of $b_1$: slopes 3, 1, 0, with breakpoints at $b_1 = 3$ (value 9) and $b_1 = 6$ (value 12).

Why concave: $z^*(b)$ is the minimum over dual feasible $y$ of $b^\top y$. The dual feasible set does not depend on $b$, so $z^*$ is a minimum of linear functions of $b$, which is concave. The pieces correspond to the dual vertices, one per optimal basis. This picture comes back in unit 11: the Lagrangian dual function is also a minimum of linear functions, and subgradient methods climb it.

The economic reading: if row $i$ is a resource (machine hours, budget), $y_i$ is the most you should pay for one more unit, and a row with slack is worth nothing at the margin. This is also where the name "shadow price" comes from.

03 · prices

Ranging: how long the price holds

Change $b_i$ to $b_i + \delta$. Reduced costs don't involve $b$, so the basis stays optimal for as long as it stays feasible:

$$x_B(\delta) = A_B^{-1}(b + \delta e_i) = \bar b + \delta\, d$$

where $d$ is slack column $i$ of the final tableau.

Feasible while $\bar b_r + \delta d_r \ge 0$ for all rows:

$$\max_{d_r > 0} \frac{-\bar b_r}{d_r} \;\le\; \delta \;\le\; \min_{d_r < 0} \frac{-\bar b_r}{d_r}$$
example

Slack column 1 is $d = (2, -1)$ with $\bar b = (2, 2)$: $\delta \in [-1, 2]$, so $b_1 \in [3, 6]$. Inside, $z^* = 10 + 1\cdot\delta$.

HiGHS's ranging report gives $[3, 6]$ with objective 9 and 12 at the ends. The lab's then checks 103 binding rows, and all agree.

Outside the range the basis changes and the slope changes. A linear prediction there is wrong, which is why lab step 5 must refuse it.

Degeneracy caveat: at a degenerate optimum, several optimal bases exist and each has its own range, and the left and right derivatives of $z^*$ differ. Solvers report the range for the basis they stopped at. Treat ranging output as "this basis's range", not "the" range.

The same analysis works for the objective coefficients: changing $c_j$ keeps the basis feasible and changes reduced costs, so the basis stays optimal while the objective row stays nonnegative. Solvers report both kinds (HiGHS's getRanging has col_cost_up/col_cost_dn next to the row bounds used in the lab).

The non-binding rows the lab's then skips: when slack $s_i$ is basic with value $v$, $d = e_r$, so the range is $\delta \ge -v$ with no upper end. You can loosen a non-binding constraint forever, and tighten it until it just binds. HiGHS's report uses a different convention for those rows, so the comparison is restricted to binding ones.

Sign conventions: an afternoon, as promised

The same LP, $\max 3x + 2y$, $x + y \le 4$, $2x + y \le 6$:

source$y_1$$y_2$
your tableau11
HiGHS, sense=max11
HiGHS, written as $\min -c^\top x$−1−1
SciPy linprog marginals−1−1
one rule covers all four

A reported dual is $\partial(\text{objective})/\partial(\text{rhs})$ in the solver's own objective sense.

Maximizing, loosening a $\le$ row helps, so the dual is $\ge 0$. Minimizing $-c^\top x$, the same loosening lowers the objective, so it is $\le 0$.

Every solver documents its convention somewhere, and every modeller has at some point shipped a sign error from forgetting it. Before trusting duals from a solver, solve a two-variable LP whose duals you know, like this one, and look. That is what the curriculum means by "which will take you an afternoon, and should".

Two more places where conventions differ: duals of $\ge$ rows (some solvers report them with the opposite sign to $\le$ rows, some don't), and reduced costs of variables at their upper bound in bounded-variable simplex (which have the opposite sign to those at their lower bound). HiGHS, Gurobi and CPLEX each document these; they don't all agree.

Gurobi, for reference, reports Pi for constraint duals and RC for reduced costs. It isn't installed here, and no lab depends on it.

section 4

The dual as an algorithm

Keep optimality, restore feasibility.

03 · dual simplex

The dual simplex method

State: a basis whose objective row is nonnegative (dual feasible) but with some negative right-hand sides (primal infeasible).

  1. Leaving row: any row $r$ with $\bar b_r < 0$. The most negative is the usual choice.
  2. Entering column: among $j$ with $T_{rj} < 0$, minimize $\;\dfrac{T_{mj}}{-T_{rj}}$, the dual ratio test.
  3. Pivot. If no $T_{rj} < 0$, row $r$ reads "nonnegative combination = negative": the LP is infeasible.
why it works

It is exactly the primal simplex method applied to the dual LP, run on the same tableau.

The dual ratio test keeps every objective-row entry nonnegative after the pivot, and the objective value decreases monotonically toward the optimum from above.

Row $r$ in the infeasible case is a Farkas certificate: unit 01 again.

Why the ratio test has that form. Pivoting on $T_{rs}$ subtracts $\frac{T_{ms}}{T_{rs}} \times$ row $r$ from the objective row. For column $j$ the new entry is $T_{mj} - T_{ms}\frac{T_{rj}}{T_{rs}}$. With $T_{rs} < 0$ and $T_{rj} < 0$, this stays $\ge 0$ exactly when $\frac{T_{mj}}{-T_{rj}} \ge \frac{T_{ms}}{-T_{rs}}$, which is what choosing the minimum ratio guarantees. Columns with $T_{rj} \ge 0$ only get larger.

Infeasibility: row $r$ says $\sum_j T_{rj} x_j = \bar b_r < 0$ with every coefficient $\ge 0$ and every variable $\ge 0$. The left side cannot be negative. The row is a combination of the original rows, so its multipliers are a Farkas certificate. Lab step 4's third test builds this case on purpose, with the cut $x + y \ge 5$ against $x + y \le 4$.

In modern solvers the dual simplex is the default LP algorithm for most problems, not just for re-solves. Dual steepest-edge pricing is cheap to maintain, and on many real LPs the dual is easier to get feasible.

03 · dual simplex

Why branch-and-cut lives on it

After an optimal solve, add a cut $a^\top x \le \beta$ (unit 08) or tighten a bound $x_j \le \lfloor x_j^* \rfloor$ (unit 07):

  • the new row, written in the current basis, leaves the objective row unchanged, so the basis is still dual feasible;
  • only the new row's rhs can be negative, so the tableau is still almost primal feasible.

That is the dual simplex's starting state, and it is typically a few pivots away from optimal.

Lab then, 163 random LPs with one added cut:

warmcold
your dual simplex (pivots)0.983.34
HiGHS (iterations)0.963.47

On 4×5 LPs. The ratio widens with size: a cold solve's pivots grow with the LP, while a warm re-solve's pivots grow with the size of the change.

A branch-and-bound tree with 10 000 nodes solves 10 000 LPs. Each node's LP is its parent's plus one bound change. Warm-starting each from the parent's basis with the dual simplex, instead of solving cold, is the difference between a usable solver and an unusable one. This is also why interior-point methods, which cannot warm-start well (unit 04), are rarely used inside branch-and-bound except at the root.

A subtlety for unit 07: a bound change is not an added row in real codes. Bounded-variable simplex handles bounds implicitly, and a changed bound makes one basic variable infeasible. The effect on the dual simplex is the same.

When one of those units feels hard, come back to the slide here that it generalizes.

The pattern to look for, in every one of them: there is a relaxed problem whose solution gives a bound, the bound is a function of some multipliers, and the algorithm is a method for choosing good multipliers. Weak duality guarantees the bound is valid, and complementary slackness tells you when it is tight.
