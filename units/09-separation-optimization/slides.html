09

Separation ⇔ optimization

You can optimize over a polytope in polynomial time exactly when you can find a violated inequality in polynomial time. Exponentially many constraints are fine.

needs 04, 08~2 h slides + 2½ h lablab: uv run co lab 09

The curriculum calls this the most beautiful result in the course, and the branch unit it would fight to keep. It turns the ellipsoid method (unit 04), which is useless for solving LPs, into the reason we can trust formulations with exponentially many constraints. It also explains why lazy constraints, which every solver supports, are sound engineering and not a hack.

The lab makes it concrete on the oldest example: Dantzig, Fulkerson and Johnson's 1954 subtour formulation of the TSP.

How to read this deck

The theorem is given as two ideas, each with a picture (the second one is a square and its polar, a diamond); the ellipsoid details are in the notes. The subtour section runs on six cities, where the separation oracle is carried out by hand: components on an integer point, a minimum cut on a fractional one, and Stoer–Wagner on four vertices. Every cut value was computed with the lab's reference solution.

section 1

The theorem

Two oracles for a polytope, and the fact that they are equally powerful.

09 · theorem

Two ways to be given a polytope

optimization oracle

Given $c$: return $\arg\max\{c^\top x : x \in P\}$, or report $P = \emptyset$.

"Solve the problem for any objective."

separation oracle

Given $y$: either certify $y \in P$, or return an inequality $a^\top x \le \beta$ valid for $P$ with $a^\top y > \beta$.

"Find a violated constraint, if there is one."

A polytope with exponentially many facets can have a short separation oracle, one that searches its facets implicitly. For subtour constraints it is a minimum cut, and for matching's odd-set constraints a minimum odd cut.

For the theorem, $P$ must be "well-described": its facets have bounded encoding size $\varphi$, and the running time may be polynomial in $\varphi$ and $n$.

The encoding bound is what lets the ellipsoid method start and stop. From $\varphi$ you get an initial ball radius $R = 2^{O(n\varphi)}$ and a minimum volume below which a full-dimensional $P$ can't hide (unit 04's $R$ and $r$). Lower-dimensional polytopes need extra work, since they contain no ball. GLS handle this by finding the affine hull first, which is one of the technical parts of their book.

"Strong" versions of the oracles, the exact ones above, are what the theorem is about. There are also "weak" versions with an $\varepsilon$ tolerance, which are the natural notion for general convex bodies. The equivalences between all of them are GLS chapter 4.

09 · theorem

Grötschel, Lovász & Schrijver (1981)

theorem

For a family of well-described polyhedra, the optimization problem is solvable in polynomial time if and only if the separation problem is.

separation ⇒ optimization, the idea

The ellipsoid method (unit 04) only ever asks "is this centre inside, and if not, which inequality fails?". A separation oracle answers exactly that, so the ellipsoid method runs, and with the objective added as a sliding objective it optimizes.

optimization ⇒ separation, the idea

Flip the picture with the polar $P^\circ$: facets of $P$ become vertices of $P^\circ$, and vertices of $P$ become facets. "Find a violated facet of $P$" becomes "optimize over $P^\circ$", and the next slide shows it on a square.

details in the notes (N)

Details

Separation ⇒ optimization. Run the ellipsoid method on $P \cap \{c^\top x \ge \gamma\}$. Its cuts come from the separation oracle, or from the objective constraint $c^\top x \ge \gamma$ when the centre is feasible (a "sliding objective"). The volume argument bounds the number of iterations, and rounding at the end gives an exact optimal vertex.

Optimization ⇒ separation. With $0$ in the interior of $P$, the polar is $P^\circ = \{a : a^\top x \le 1\ \forall x \in P\}$. Separating $y$ from $P$ means finding $a \in P^\circ$ with $a^\top y > 1$, which is optimization over $P^\circ$. A separation oracle for $P^\circ$ is "maximize $a^\top x$ over $P$", which is exactly the optimization oracle we have. Apply the first direction to $P^\circ$.

The first direction is the one the lab's intuition uses. The ellipsoid method never needs the constraint list, only an answer to "is this centre feasible, and if not, which inequality does it violate?" (unit 04, slide "Useless in practice, indispensable in theory").

The second direction is the elegant one. The polar of a polytope has a vertex for each facet of $P$ and a facet for each vertex of $P$. An optimization oracle for $P$ answers "which vertex of $P$ maximizes this direction", which is exactly "which facet of $P^\circ$ is most violated by this point". So optimizing over $P$ is separating over $P^\circ$. Running ellipsoid over $P^\circ$ with that oracle finds a most-violated facet of $P$.

Practical meaning of the second direction: if a problem is NP-hard to optimize over, like the TSP, then separating over its full convex hull is NP-hard too. That is why TSP codes separate only chosen families (subtours, combs, …), and never "all facets".

09 · theorem · example

A square and its polar

Left: $P = [-1,1]^2$ and $y = (1.5, 0.5)$. Right: $P^\circ = \{a : |a_1| + |a_2| \le 1\}$. The facet $x_1 \le 1$ of $P$ is the vertex $(1, 0)$ of $P^\circ$.

$P$ (square)$P^\circ$ (diamond)
4 facets $\pm x_1 \le 1$, $\pm x_2 \le 1$4 vertices $(\pm1, 0)$, $(0, \pm1)$
4 vertices $(\pm1, \pm1)$4 facets $\pm a_1 \pm a_2 \le 1$
separating the point by optimizing over the diamond

Is $y = (1.5, 0.5)$ in $P$? Maximize $a^\top y = 1.5a_1 + 0.5a_2$ over $P^\circ$. The best vertex is $a = (1, 0)$, value $1.5$.

$1.5 > 1$, so $a^\top x \le 1$, i.e. $x_1 \le 1$, is valid for $P$ and violated by $y$. A cut, found by an optimization.

If the maximum had been $\le 1$, every facet of $P$ would hold at $y$, so $y \in P$.

explore interactively

Why the polar of the square is the diamond. $a$ is in $P^\circ$ when $a^\top x \le 1$ for every $x$ in the square. The worst $x$ is a vertex, $(\pm1, \pm1)$, giving $|a_1| + |a_2| \le 1$. So each vertex of $P$ contributes one facet of $P^\circ$.

Why a vertex of $P^\circ$ is a facet of $P$. The vertex $(1, 0)$ stands for the inequality $1 \cdot x_1 + 0 \cdot x_2 \le 1$, and that is a facet of the square. Every valid inequality of the form $a^\top x \le 1$ is a point of $P^\circ$, and the strongest ones are its vertices.

For the square the polar is written out, so this is a toy. The theorem's power is when $P$ has exponentially many facets: $P^\circ$ then has exponentially many vertices, and "optimize over $P^\circ$" is done by the ellipsoid method with the optimization oracle for $P$ as its separation oracle.

What it bought

resultvia
Maximum-weight matching as an LP in polynomial timeodd-set inequalities (unit 06) separated by a minimum odd cut (Padberg & Rao 1982)
The first polynomial algorithm for submodular function minimizationseparation over the base polytope by a greedy algorithm (unit 13)

NP-hardness of separation for hard polytopesthe other direction, contrapositive

Some of these (stable sets in perfect graphs, submodular minimization, for a long time) had no other known polynomial algorithm. The ellipsoid method was the only route.

Combinatorial polynomial algorithms for submodular function minimization came much later: Iwata, Fleischer & Fujishige, and Schrijver, both in 2000. For maximum-weight stable set in perfect graphs, no combinatorial polynomial algorithm is known to this day. The only polynomial algorithm goes through the ellipsoid method or other convex optimization over the theta body.

For matching, Edmonds' blossom algorithm (1965) was already polynomial. GLS gave a second proof, "because the LP over its polytope is polynomially separable", that doesn't need the algorithm at all.

section 2

Subtours

The constraint family that started it all, and its minimum-cut oracle.

09 · subtours

The subtour elimination LP

Solid edges at 1, dashed at ½. Every degree is 2 and the graph is connected, yet the cut around $\{3,4,5\}$ weighs 1: a subtour constraint is violated.

Edge variables $x_e$ on the complete graph, the DFJ formulation (1954), with $\delta(S)$ the edges leaving $S$:

$$\begin{aligned}\min\ & \textstyle\sum_e c_e x_e \\ \text{s.t. } & \textstyle\sum_{e \in \delta(v)} x_e = 2 \quad \forall v \\ & \textstyle\sum_{e \in \delta(S)} x_e \ge 2 \quad \forall\, \emptyset \ne S \subsetneq V \\ & 0 \le x_e \le 1\end{aligned}$$

The degree constraints alone allow subtours: several disjoint cycles. The subtour constraints say every vertex set must be entered and left. There are $2^{n-1}-1$ of them.

With integrality, the degree constraints plus all subtour constraints describe exactly the tours. The LP relaxation is the subtour polytope, which contains the TSP polytope and is strictly bigger, so its bound is a lower bound on the optimal tour.

DFJ solved a 49-city instance (48 US state capitals plus Washington) by hand in 1954, adding subtour constraints and a few other cuts as needed. It is the first cutting-plane computation and the origin of the whole approach.

Counting: a set and its complement give the same constraint, so there are $(2^n - 2)/2 = 2^{n-1} - 1$ distinct constraints. The lab's tests count 127 for $n = 8$.

09 · subtours

Separation is a minimum cut

claim

$x^*$ violates some subtour constraint iff the global minimum cut of the support graph with edge weights $x^*$ has value below 2.

The constraint for $S$ is exactly "the cut $\delta(S)$ has weight $\ge 2$", so the most violated one is the lightest cut. It is a restatement, not a trick.

Integer points are easier: a 0/1 solution of the degree constraints is a union of cycles, and each cycle's vertex set has cut weight 0. The connected components check suffices (lab step 5).

Stoer–Wagner (1997)
  1. Build a maximum-adjacency order: repeatedly add the vertex most strongly attached to those already added.
  2. The last vertex's attachment, the cut of the phase, is a minimum cut separating the last two vertices, $s$ and $t$.
  3. Record it, merge $s$ and $t$, and repeat until one vertex is left.

$n - 1$ phases, $O(nm + n^2 \log n)$ in total. No flows needed.

why merging is safe: notes (N)

explore interactively

Why merging is safe: the global minimum cut either separates $s$ and $t$, and then the phase's cut, a minimum $s$–$t$ cut, is at least as good; or it doesn't, and then it survives the merge. So the best phase cut is a global minimum. The lab's hint rung 3 sketches it. The key lemma, that the cut of the phase is a minimum $s$–$t$ cut, is a two-paragraph induction in the paper.

Before Stoer–Wagner, global minimum cut was done with $n - 1$ maximum-flow computations (a Gomory–Hu tree), which is unit 14's tool. Concorde uses flow-based separation with heuristics that find most violated sets without any flow at all: connected components, and shrinking edges with $x_e = 1$.

09 · subtours · example

The oracle, on six cities

an integer point: components are enough

$x = 1$ on the edges of triangles $\{0,1,2\}$ and $\{3,4,5\}$, 0 elsewhere. Every degree is 2.

Support graph components: $\{0,1,2\}$ and $\{3,4,5\}$. Two components, so both constraints $x(\delta(\{0,1,2\})) \ge 2$ and $x(\delta(\{3,4,5\})) \ge 2$ are violated, with left side 0.

a fractional point: connected, still violated

In each triangle two edges at 1 and one at $\tfrac12$: $01 = 12 = 1$, $02 = \tfrac12$, and the same on $\{3,4,5\}$. Two bridges at $\tfrac12$: $03$ and $25$.

Degrees: vertex 0 has $1 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 2$, vertex 1 has $1 + 1 = 2$, and so on. All degree constraints hold, and the support graph is connected.

Minimum cut: $\{3,4,5\}$, weight $\tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1 < 2$. The constraint for $S = \{3,4,5\}$ is violated.

Stoer–Wagner on four vertices

Weights $01 = 3$, $23 = 3$, $02 = 12 = 13 = 1$.

Why the fractional point needs a real min cut: components see one piece, and every degree is right. Only a cut computation finds the thin connection.

explore interactively

The reference oracle. separate_subtours on the integer point returns both components; on the fractional point components returns one piece and min_cut returns $(1.0, (3, 4, 5))$. The cut edges are exactly the two bridges. The constraint for $\{0,1,2\}$ is the same one, since a set and its complement share a cut.

The Stoer–Wagner phases on the four-vertex graph. Phase 1 starts at 0. Vertex 1 is attached by 3, vertex 2 by 1, vertex 3 by 0: add 1. Now 2 is attached by $1 + 1 = 2$ and 3 by 1: add 2. Then 3 is attached by $1 + 3 = 4$: add 3, cut of the phase 4, merge 2 and 3. Phase 2: 1 is attached by 3, $\{2,3\}$ by $1$; add 1; then $\{2,3\}$ is attached by $1 + 1 + 1 = 3$: cut of the phase 3. Phase 3: one merge left, cut 4. The best is 3, matching the reference min_cut, which returns $(3.0, (2, 3))$.

Why the cut of a phase is a minimum cut between its last two vertices is the lemma in the paper. The consequence used above is simpler: any global minimum cut either separates that pair, and then the phase found something at least as good, or keeps them together, and then it survives their merge.

Lab then, random Euclidean instances. The exact tour at $n = 200$ took 10 MIP solves and 46 s with HiGHS as the integer solver.

Why so few: most subtour constraints are satisfied by a wide margin at any reasonable LP point. Only cuts around clusters of nearby cities come close to binding, and there are few such clusters. The LP only needs constraints that bind at its optimum, which number at most the dimension, and in practice far fewer are ever violated along the way.

The naive "re-solve the whole MIP after adding cuts" loop in step 5 is wasteful. Each MIP solve starts from scratch. A branch-and-cut implementation adds the cuts inside one tree as they are found, which is the next slide.

section 3

In practice

Lazy constraints, and how far the idea goes.

09 · practice

Lazy constraints and callbacks

Every MIP solver can take constraints it doesn't see up front:

  • Lazy constraints: checked on every candidate integer solution; if one is violated, the solution is rejected and the constraint is added. Required for correctness.
  • User cuts: separated at fractional LP points to tighten the bound. Optional; only for speed.

Gurobi: a callback with cbLazy and cbCut. SCIP: a constraint handler with check, enforce and separate methods. That is the same distinction.

the classic bug

Adding subtour constraints only as user cuts means the solver can accept an integer solution made of subtours, if no fractional node happened to trigger separation. Lazy constraints need enforcement on integer solutions, which is what lab step 5 does, clumsily.

The GLS theorem is why this is sound: as long as the separation routine is exact on integer solutions (enforcement) and good on fractional ones (bound), the solver solves the problem over the implicit exponential family. The lab's step 4 shows the LP version: "no violated constraint found" is a proof that the full LP is solved, because minimum cut is an exact oracle.

SCIP's Python interface (PySCIPOpt, installed) supports writing constraint handlers. A TSP handler in that style is the natural extension of this lab if you want to see branch-and-cut with lazy subtour constraints instead of repeated MIP solves.

09 · practice

How good is the bound, and how far has this gone?

integrality gap of the subtour LP

For metric TSP the optimal tour is at most $\tfrac32$ times the subtour LP bound (Wolsey 1980; Shmoys & Williamson 1990), and instances with gap close to $\tfrac43$ exist. The 4/3 conjecture says that is the truth; it is open.

On random Euclidean instances the gap is tiny, under 1% in the lab.

Unit 11 computes the same bound a completely different way (the Held–Karp bound from 1-trees), and gets the same number.

Concorde

Concorde: Applegate, Bixby, Chvátal & Cook. Branch-and-cut with subtour constraints, comb inequalities, and "local cuts" found by solving small projected subproblems.

It solved an 85 900-city instance from a chip-layout application (pla85900) to proven optimality in 2006, with about 136 CPU-years of computation.

The equality of the subtour LP bound and the Held–Karp bound is a theorem (Held & Karp 1970): the Lagrangian dual of the degree constraints over 1-trees has the same optimum as the subtour LP. Unit 11 uses it as its checkpoint, "they should be close, and knowing why they are close is the unit".

Comb inequalities (Chvátal 1973; Grötschel & Padberg 1979) are a family of TSP facets with no known polynomial exact separation in general. Concorde separates them heuristically, plus exactly for special cases. This is the second GLS direction in action: the full TSP polytope is NP-hard to separate over, so practice uses families.
