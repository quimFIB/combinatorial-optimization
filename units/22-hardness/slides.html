Hardness, and the shape of what is possible

Reductions as programs, the numbers that make a problem hard, and the hierarchy of how close to optimal a polynomial algorithm can promise to get.

needs 11 (and 00, 16, 20)~1 h slides + 2 h lablab: uv run co lab 22

Parts I–IV built exact methods, and the best of them (CP-SAT, a MIP solver with cuts) prove optimality on remarkably large instances. They still take exponential time in the worst case, and Part V takes that seriously. If you must answer in polynomial time, what can you promise?

This unit is the map, and units 23–25 are the territory. The theory here says, for each problem, how good a guarantee is possible. It tells you when a better algorithm is worth looking for and when the search is over unless P = NP.

Reductions

A program between problems, with bugs.

22 · reductions

Karp's chain

polynomial (Karp) reduction $A \le_p B$

A polynomial-time map $f$ from instances of $A$ to instances of $B$ with $x \in A \iff f(x) \in B$. For optimisation, add a polynomial map $g$ taking solutions of $f(x)$ back to solutions of $x$.

If $B$ is in P, so is $A$. Read the other way: if $A$ is NP-hard, so is $B$. Every NP-hardness proof runs from a known hard problem to the new one.

Cook (1971) and Levin (1973): SAT is NP-complete. Karp (1972): 21 more, by reductions.

Shaded: the links the lab mechanises (with CNF → 3-CNF). Every leaf is one of unit 00's nine problems.

The direction is the whole point, and it's the most common error in student proofs. To show your new problem $B$ is hard, you must turn every instance of a known hard problem into an instance of $B$. The reverse only shows that $B$ is no harder than the known problem.

Many of the links in the tree go through intermediate problems in the textbooks (Garey & Johnson ch. 3). Hamiltonian cycle from 3-SAT uses a gadget per variable (a zig-zag path) and one per clause, and TSP follows by giving edges of the graph weight 1 and non-edges weight 2. Subset sum reduces to partition by adding two padding numbers, and bin packing with two bins of capacity half the total is exactly partition. Keep that last one in mind: it drives bin packing's 3/2 in section 3.

3-SAT → vertex cover, and back

Filled: the cover of size $k = n + 2m = 7$ built from $x_1 = x_2 = 1$, $x_3 = 0$.

  • Every cover takes ≥ 1 vertex per variable edge and ≥ 2 per triangle, so ≥ $k$.
  • Satisfiable ⇒ cover of size $k$: the true literals, plus each triangle minus one corner whose literal is true. That corner's link is covered from above.
  • Cover of size $k$ ⇒ satisfiable: exactly one literal per variable (read it as true) and two corners per triangle. The third corner's link must be covered by its literal, which is therefore true.

The reduction needs exactly three literals per clause, which is why lab step 2 (clause splitting) exists. With a clause of width 2 the gadget is an edge, not a triangle, and $k$ changes per clause. That's doable, but it's the kind of special case where a reduction grows its first bug.

The backward map in the lab takes any cover of size ≤ $k$, not only minimum ones. The lower bound argument shows it's then exactly $k$ with the structure above. Tests hand it a minimum cover from HiGHS, which has no reason to look like the one you would build.

The instance grows from $n$ variables and $m$ clauses to $2n + 3m$ vertices and $n + 6m$ edges: linear. Size matters when you transfer running-time lower bounds (the exponential time hypothesis) rather than just NP-hardness.

22 · reductions

What a reduction preserves, and what it doesn't

three strengths
  1. Decision: yes-instances to yes-instances. Enough for NP-hardness.
  2. Optimum: $\mathrm{OPT}(f(x))$ determined by $\mathrm{OPT}(x)$, with solutions mapped back. Vertex cover → set cover is this: the same 0/1 vector, the same cost.
  3. Gap: near-optimal solutions of $f(x)$ map back to near-optimal solutions of $x$ (L-reductions, gap-preserving reductions). Needed to transfer hardness of approximation.
measured: the gadget is decision-only

An assignment leaving $u$ clauses unsatisfied gives a cover of $k + u$. So $\text{min cover} - k \le \text{fewest unsatisfied}$. Equality fails: covering both $x_v$ and $\lnot x_v$ costs 1 and can rescue several clause gadgets.

300 small random 3-CNFs, MaxSAT by RC2 against the minimum cover by CP-SAT: the excess was strictly smaller on 19.

So a 1.01-approximate cover tells you nothing useful about a near-satisfying assignment. Approximation-hardness results need reductions built for it.

Why the distinction matters. Every NP-complete problem is equivalent to every other for exact solution, yet their approximability ranges from FPTAS (knapsack) to nothing at all (general TSP). Decision reductions can't see that range. The theory of approximation-preserving reductions (Papadimitriou & Yannakakis 1991, L-reductions and MAX SNP; later AP-reductions) exists to sort problems by it.

The 19-of-300 figure isn't in then.py; it was a check made while writing this deck (random formulas with 3–8 variables and n to 8n clauses). An earlier draft of the lab README claimed equality, and brute force said otherwise. It's the lesson of this unit in miniature.

Weak and strong NP-hardness

Unit 16's knapsack DP runs in $O(nC)$. Polynomial in the value of $C$, exponential in its length $\log C$: pseudo-polynomial.

definitions

Weakly NP-hard: NP-hard, but solvable in time polynomial in $n$ and the largest number.
Strongly NP-hard: still NP-hard when every number is at most a polynomial in $n$ (written in unary).

weakstrong
knapsack, subset sum, partitionbin packing, 3-partition
makespan on 2 machinesmakespan on $m$ machines, $m$ in the input
TSP, set cover, vertex cover, colouring (no numbers at all)
theorem (Garey & Johnson 1978)

If a problem is strongly NP-hard, has integer objective values, and $\mathrm{OPT} \le p(n, \text{max number})$ for a polynomial $p$, then it has no FPTAS unless P = NP.

proof

Run the FPTAS with $\varepsilon = 1/(p + 1)$. Its error is below $\varepsilon\,\mathrm{OPT} < 1$, so it's exact, in time $\mathrm{poly}(n, p)$. On the unary-sized instances where the problem is still NP-hard, that's polynomial.

An FPTAS is a pseudo-polynomial exact algorithm in disguise, and vice versa (next slide). Weak hardness is the precondition.

Bin packing is the instructive case. It contains partition, which is only weakly hard, so why is bin packing strongly hard? Because 3-partition (split $3m$ numbers into $m$ triples of equal sum) is strongly NP-hard, and it's bin packing with $m$ bins where every bin must be exactly full. The number of bins in the input is what makes the difference: partition is the special case with two bins.

Problems with no numbers (vertex cover, colouring) are trivially strongly NP-hard, since their only numbers are bounded by $n$.

22 · numbers

The knapsack FPTAS (Ibarra & Kim 1975)

  1. Drop items that don't fit alone. Let $v_{\max}$ be the largest remaining value.
  2. $K = \varepsilon\, v_{\max} / n$; round $v_i' = \lfloor v_i / K \rfloor$.
  3. Exact DP by value on $v'$: $W[p]$ = least weight reaching value exactly $p$. At most $n \cdot n/\varepsilon$ values, so $O(n^3/\varepsilon)$ time.
why $\ge (1 - \varepsilon)\,\mathrm{OPT}$ $$\begin{aligned} \textstyle\sum_{S} v_i &\ge K \textstyle\sum_{S} v_i' \ge K \textstyle\sum_{O} v_i' \\ &\ge \textstyle\sum_{O} v_i - nK = \mathrm{OPT} - \varepsilon\, v_{\max} \\ &\ge (1 - \varepsilon)\,\mathrm{OPT}\end{aligned}$$

The last step needs $\mathrm{OPT} \ge v_{\max}$: step 1's filter. Both it and the $/n$ have a lab test that breaks without them.

Lab then: 100 items, values to $10^6$, weights to $10^4$.

Exact weight DP (unit 16): 0.05 s. CP-SAT: 0.23 s. The guarantee is loose by orders of magnitude, and at these sizes the exact method is faster. An FPTAS earns its place when values and weights are huge.

Why the actual loss is zero: with 100 items of similar size, the optimum contains about 50 of them, and rounding error is spread across many items whose ties are broken nearly at random. The $nK$ worst case needs every item in the optimum to lose almost $K$ and the rounded optimum to pick a different, lighter set. That's rare on random data.

This is the pattern for the rest of Part V: worst-case guarantees are real theorems, and typical performance is much better. Unit 23's then measures the same distance for greedy set cover, Christofides and LPT.

Every inclusion is strict unless P = NP.

the gap technique

If it's NP-hard to tell $\mathrm{OPT} \le a$ from $\mathrm{OPT} \ge b$, no algorithm beats ratio $b/a$ (unless P = NP).

  • Bin packing, 3/2. Partition asks whether 2 bins suffice. Otherwise 3 are needed. A ratio below 3/2 would decide partition.
  • General TSP, nothing. Hamiltonian cycle: edges weigh 1, non-edges $\rho n + 1$. A tour costs $n$ if a cycle exists, and $> \rho n$ otherwise.
  • Colouring, 4/3. Deciding 3-colourability separates 3 from ≥ 4.

Bin packing's 3/2 is an absolute ratio: it comes from instances with OPT = 2. It says nothing about large instances, and indeed bin packing has an asymptotic PTAS (Fernandez de la Vega & Lueker 1981) and an algorithm using OPT + O(log OPT) bins (Hoberg & Rothvoss 2017). Whether OPT + 1 is achievable in polynomial time is open. "Bin packing is hard to approximate" is true only in the narrow sense.

Metric TSP is where the triangle inequality changes everything: the reduction from Hamiltonian cycle needs non-edges much longer than twice an edge, which the metric forbids. Unit 23 shows the triangle inequality is exactly what the double-tree and Christofides proofs use.

Our TSP.random instances are Euclidean distances rounded to integers. Rounding can break the triangle inequality by one unit, which matters only for proofs, not for measurements.

The PCP theorem: where approximation hardness comes from

PCP theorem (Arora–Safra; Arora–Lund–Motwani–Sudan–Szegedy 1998)

$\mathrm{NP} = \mathrm{PCP}(O(\log n), O(1))$: every NP statement has a proof that a verifier can check by reading a constant number of bits chosen with $O(\log n)$ random bits, always accepting a correct proof and rejecting a false claim with probability ≥ ½.

equivalently: a gap

There is an $\varepsilon > 0$ such that telling a satisfiable 3-CNF from one where every assignment violates ≥ $\varepsilon m$ clauses is NP-hard.

Dinur (2007) gave a combinatorial proof by gap amplification.

Before PCP, the gap technique needed a lucky NP-hard question with a built-in gap (2 vs 3 bins). Afterwards, every NP problem has one, via Max-3SAT, and gap-preserving reductions carry it onward.

Håstad (2001): optimal inapproximability

A verifier reading 3 bits and checking their parity. Consequence: for every $\varepsilon > 0$, Max-3SAT is NP-hard to approximate within $7/8 + \varepsilon$.

A uniformly random assignment satisfies $7/8$ of the clauses in expectation. The dumbest algorithm is optimal.

The equivalence between the two statements is short. Given a PCP verifier for 3-SAT, write each of its polynomially many random choices as a constant-size formula over the proof bits, convert to 3-CNF, and a false claim leaves a constant fraction of those formulas violated. Conversely, a gap 3-SAT reduction gives a verifier: pick a random clause and read its three variables.

The historical route went through interactive proofs (Babai, Fortnow, Lund; Feige, Goldwasser, Lovász, Safra, Szegedy 1991, who first noticed the connection to clique approximation). It's one of the rare cases where a result about proof systems has direct algorithmic consequences.

Three thresholds, and the Unique Games Conjecture

set cover

Greedy: $H_n \le \ln n + 1$ (Johnson 1974; Lovász 1975; Chvátal 1979).

NP-hard within $(1 - \varepsilon)\ln n$ (Dinur & Steurer 2014; Feige 1998 under a weaker assumption).

Closed: greedy is optimal up to lower-order terms.

vertex cover

Maximal matching or LP rounding: 2. Nothing known beats $2 - o(1)$.

NP-hard: 1.3606 (Dinur & Safra 2005), then $\sqrt 2 - \varepsilon$ (Khot, Minzer & Safra 2018).

UGC-hard: $2 - \varepsilon$ (Khot & Regev 2008).

Max-Cut

Goemans–Williamson SDP: $\alpha_{GW} \approx 0.87856$ (unit 25).

NP-hard above $16/17 \approx 0.941$ (Håstad 2001; Trevisan et al. 2000).

UGC-hard above $\alpha_{GW}$ (Khot, Kindler, Mossel & O'Donnell 2007).

Unique Games Conjecture (Khot 2002)

For every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is a $k$ such that, for constraint problems on a graph whose edge constraints are permutations of $k$ labels, it's NP-hard to tell instances where $1 - \varepsilon$ of the constraints can be satisfied from those where at most $\varepsilon$ can. Open. If true, the simple algorithms for vertex cover and Max-Cut, and SDP rounding for every CSP (Raghavendra 2008), are optimal.

The pattern to notice: the algorithms matching these thresholds are simple (greedy, a matching, one SDP and a random hyperplane). Decades of attempts to beat them failed, and the hardness results explain why. That's the practical payoff the curriculum promises: once a threshold is known, a better worst-case algorithm isn't worth hunting for. Effort goes into instance-specific methods instead (Parts II–IV) or into typical-case performance (unit 26).

The 2018 result proved the 2-to-2 Games Conjecture, a weaker cousin of UGC, which gives $\sqrt 2$ for vertex cover unconditionally. It's considered evidence for UGC, but the full conjecture remains open.

The table has ten rows for nine problems because TSP splits: our generator makes (rounded) Euclidean instances, which have a PTAS, while the general metric case is the famous open problem. Karlin, Klein and Oveis Gharan's improvement on Christofides is tiny in value and enormous in significance: it broke a 45-year barrier.

For job shop the known super-constant hardness results need assumptions stronger than P ≠ NP and apply to variants (Mastrolilli & Svensson 2011). Treat the row as "somewhere between 5/4 and polylog, and nobody knows where".

Colouring's "lower-order" gap is between $n^{1-\varepsilon}$ for every $\varepsilon$ and a ratio of $n$ divided by polylog factors: essentially nothing is achievable, and that's known.

FFD's bound is Dósa's tight version (2007). The asymptotic results are why bin packing is easy in practice (unit 27): the additive loss is at most a few bins.
