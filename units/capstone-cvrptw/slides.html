One problem, four traditions

Vehicle routing with time windows, solved by branch-and-cut, branch-and-price, CP-SAT and ALNS, then refereed on Solomon's instances with unit 28's method.

needs 07, 08, 10, 18–19, 26, 28~1¼ h slides + 12–15 h lablab: uv run co lab capstone

The capstone is the one place in the course where the four traditions meet on a single problem: polyhedral (a MIP with your cuts), decomposition (column generation inside a tree), constraint programming (a circuit constraint and propagation), and local search (ALNS). Each has been built on its own in earlier units. Here they have to be compared fairly, which is harder than building any of them.

The brief in the curriculum asks for "a defensible account of which instance characteristics favour which method". So the deliverable is the report in NOTES.md, and the code is the means to it. A method that wins nowhere is still a finding, if the evidence is attached.

How to read this deck

A four-customer instance, small enough to schedule by hand, carries the mechanics: the start-time recurrence, the constant-time insertion test ALNS relies on, and the primal gap the referee reports. Its optimum was computed by brute force with the lab's reference schedule and confirmed by solve_cpsat. Distances are in tenths, as everywhere in the capstone.

capstone · the problem

VRPTW

vehicle routing with time windows

VRPTW: a depot 0 and customers $1..n$. Customer $i$ has demand $q_i$, a window $[a_i, b_i]$ in which service must start, and a service time $s_i$. Identical vehicles of capacity $Q$ leave the depot no earlier than $a_0$ and return by $b_0$. Travel time equals distance $d_{ij}$. Arriving early means waiting.

Find routes that serve every customer once, within capacity and windows, minimising total distance. The fleet is unlimited.

Along a route, service starts satisfy $$t_j = \max\bigl(a_j,\; t_i + s_i + d_{ij}\bigr) \le b_j.$$ Waiting is free, so this start-time recurrence is the entire time-window model.

getting around

Solomon's instances are forty years old and still the standard, because they vary exactly the two things that change which method works: how customers are spread (clustered, random, mixed) and how long the routes can be (series 1 against series 2). Series 2's horizons are several times longer, so routes of 30 or more customers are feasible, which matters enormously for pricing in (b).

Gehring and Homberger's instances extend the same design to 200–1000 customers. They're out of reach for the exact methods here, and the capstone doesn't use them.

capstone · conventions

Two objectives, and why the numbers are in tenths

the heuristic tables

Minimise vehicles first, then distance (a hierarchical objective), with double-precision Euclidean distances. SINTEF's best-known table uses this: R101 at 100 customers is 19 vehicles, 1650.80.

the exact literature, and this lab

Minimise distance only (the distance-only objective), with $d_{ij} = \lfloor 10 \cdot \mathrm{euclid}(i, j) \rfloor / 10$, truncated to one decimal (Kallehauge et al. 2005; Baldacci, Mingozzi & Roberti 2011). Everything is stored as integers in tenths.

  • Integers keep CP-SAT exact, make "equal cost" mean equal, and let a bound be rounded up: a node whose LP is 617.03 can't hold a solution under 617.1.
  • Truncation can break the triangle inequality by up to 0.1. So set partitioning uses $= 1$, not $\ge 1$, where dropping a customer from a route could lengthen it.
  • The two objectives' results aren't comparable. A distance-only optimum may use more vehicles and less distance than the hierarchical best-known.

Check on convention: all four methods give R101.25 = 617.1, C101.25 = 191.3, RC101.25 = 461.1 and R201.25 = 463.3. As far as can be checked from memory, these match the optima reported in the exact literature.

Mixing these conventions is the commonest way to produce a meaningless comparison table in VRPTW papers. A heuristic that reports "1 % above best known" against the hierarchical table while minimising distance only can look worse than it is, or better, depending on the instance.

The last line is deliberately hedged. The values were not looked up during the build: Solomon's own page, which lists the small instances' optima, was unreachable. They're the values remembered from the exact-methods literature, and they agree to the tenth on four instances across three series. That is strong evidence the convention is right, but it isn't a citation.

capstone · overview

The four traditions, and the referee

(a) polyhedral · step 2

Arc variables on SCIP, big-M time propagation, and your rounded capacity cuts through a constraint handler. Units 05, 08, 09, 27.

(b) decomposition · step 3

Set partitioning over routes, priced by an elementary shortest path with capacity and time windows, branching on arcs. Units 07, 10.

(c) constraint programming · step 4

CP-SAT's multiple-circuit constraint with enforced time and load, plus a hint and a chosen strategy. Units 17–21.

(d) local search · step 5

ALNS with constant-time feasible insertion, Shaw removal and regret repair. Units 19, 26.

the referee · steps 1 and 6

A checker that trusts no method, primal gaps against the best any method found, paired tests with Holm's correction, and the verdict "indistinguishable" when the data say so. Units 00, 28.

Step 1 comes first for the same reason unit 00 did: before any method runs, you need a way to tell whether its answer is right. Here that means schedules, a violation checker and routes from arcs, which all four methods reuse.

A practical note on scale: the capstone is several labs' worth of work. Steps 2–5 are each roughly one earlier lab. The tests are fast (under ten seconds against the reference) because they check every method against brute force on instances of up to about ten customers.

section 1

Four models of one problem

What each tradition writes down, and what it leaves to the solver.

capstone · (a) branch-and-cut

A compact model whose capacity lives in your cuts

The arc MIP: $x_{ij} \in \{0,1\}$ for arcs that some route could use, and start times $t_i \in [a_i, b_i]$:

$$\begin{aligned} \min\ & \textstyle\sum d_{ij} x_{ij} \\ & x(\delta^+(i)) = x(\delta^-(i)) = 1 \quad i \ne 0 \\ & t_j \ge t_i + s_i + d_{ij} - M_{ij}(1 - x_{ij}) \end{aligned}$$

with $M_{ij} = \max(0,\ b_i + s_i + d_{ij} - a_j)$, the smallest $M$ that's valid.

The time constraints already rule out subtours: around a cycle, each start would exceed the previous one by $s_i + d_{ij} > 0$. They don't limit load.

rounded capacity inequalities

For every customer set $S$: $\;x(\delta^-(S)) \ge \lceil q(S) / Q \rceil$.

Separation by connected components of the support graph. On an integer point the components are the routes, so an overloaded route always yields a violated cut. That makes the handler exact as a lazy constraint. On a fractional point it's a heuristic: exact separation is NP-hard (CVRPSEP is the standard code).

colib.vrptw.lazy_cut_handler supplies SCIP's plumbing; you supply separate(values).

The curriculum's brief says "cover and subtour cuts". For VRPTW the rounded capacity inequality is the natural family: with $\lceil q(S)/Q \rceil$ replaced by 1 it's the subtour elimination constraint of unit 09, and it plays the role that covers played for knapsack constraints in unit 08. The lab uses it for both purposes. There are no knapsack constraints here for classical covers to act on.

Big-M time windows give famously weak LP bounds. That's the price of a compact model, and it's what section 3's tables show: the MIP proves small tight-window instances fast and stalls as horizons widen. Stronger compact models exist (time-indexed or path-based), but they're larger, and at that point the extended formulation in (b) is the better tool.

The "commercial solver" of the brief becomes SCIP, as throughout the course. That costs speed, not the lesson.

capstone · (b) branch-and-price

Routes as columns, found by labelling

$$\min \sum_{r \in \Omega} c_r \lambda_r \quad \text{s.t.} \sum_{r \ni i} \lambda_r = 1\ \ \forall i,\quad \lambda \ge 0$$

Set partitioning over routes: $\Omega$ is every feasible route, too many to write down. Unit 10's loop: solve over a few, read duals $\pi$, and find a route with $c_r - \sum_{i \in r} \pi_i < 0$.

a label at a vertex

(reduced cost, load, start time, unreachable set, route). Extend along allowed arcs. $L$ dominates $L'$ when it's no worse in cost, load and time, and its unreachable set is a subset of $L'$'s.

  • Feillet et al. (2004): count customers you can't reach any more as visited. Dominance then fires between labels whose routes differ.
  • Branch on arc flows $\sum_r [ij \in r] \lambda_r$. Forbidding an arc keeps pricing the same problem on a smaller graph; forcing $ij$ forbids its alternatives.
  • Integral arc flows mean integral $\lambda$: each customer has one arc in and one out with flow 1, so every column in the support follows them.
  • Bound $\lceil LP \rceil$ because distances are integers. A restricted master IP at the root gives the first incumbent.

Pricing is the whole game. On series 1 routes are short, windows prune extensions early, and labelling is fast. On series 2 a route can hold dozens of customers, labels rarely dominate each other, and exact pricing in pure Python stops keeping up. Section 3 measures exactly where.

What state-of-the-art codes add (Pessoa et al.'s VRPSolver, Baldacci et al.): ng-route relaxation, bidirectional labelling with a midpoint, completion bounds, subset-row cuts in the master, and strong branching. On hard instances each can be worth an order of magnitude or more. With them, exact codes solve Solomon's 100-customer instances, which pure-Python labelling can't approach.

The heuristic pricing pass (keep eight labels per vertex) finds negative columns cheaply during most of the column-generation run. Only the final "prove there's none" pass must be exact. That is why the stub asks for a label limit.

capstone · (c) CP-SAT

A circuit constraint, enforced implications, and a strategy

m.add_multiple_circuit([(i, j, x[i, j]) for i, j in arcs])
for (i, j), b in x.items():
    if i and j:
        m.add(t[j] >= t[i] + s[i] + d[i][j]).only_enforce_if(b)
        m.add(u[j] >= u[i] + q[j]).only_enforce_if(b)
m.minimize(sum(d[i][j] * b for (i, j), b in x.items()))

The multiple-circuit constraint: vertex 0 may carry many circuits; every other vertex is on exactly one. That replaces the degree constraints and subtour elimination at once. No big-M: the enforced implication is propagated directly, and lazy clause generation (unit 21) learns from its failures.

search choices you make
  • A hint: a complete solution (from ALNS) that CP-SAT tries first.
  • A decision strategy: branch on short arcs first, value 1. With one worker it's only followed under FIXED_SEARCH.
  • Workers: CP-SAT is a portfolio. One worker is a fair comparison with the single-threaded methods; eight is how it's meant to be run.

The brief asks for a strategy chosen deliberately. Deliberate includes measuring whether it helps and dropping it if it doesn't.

On 25-customer instances in the prototype runs, default one-worker CP-SAT proved RC101.25 in 0.2 s, but the nearest-arc fixed strategy took 2.2 s and 84 000 branches. A plausible reading is that the fixed order overrides CP-SAT's learned heuristics without supplying better information. That's one instance, and the full comparison belongs in NOTES.md with the harness behind it.

The same model with eight workers solved most small instances an order of magnitude faster in branches. Whether that's fair depends on the question. If the question is which algorithm, one thread each. If the question is what to deploy, give each method the machine.

capstone · (d) ALNS

Feasible insertion in constant time

Keep, per route, the forward starts $t_k$ and the latest starts $\ell_k$:

$$\begin{aligned} \ell_k &= \min\bigl(b_k,\; \ell_{k+1} - s_k - d_{k,k+1}\bigr), \\ \ell_{\text{depot}} &= b_0. \end{aligned}$$

Inserting $c$ between $p$ and $q$ is feasible iff $$\begin{aligned} t_c &= \max(a_c,\ t_p + s_p + d_{pc}) \le b_c, \\ &\max(a_q,\ t_c + s_c + d_{cq}) \le \ell_q, \end{aligned}$$ plus the load. Waiting absorbs any push up to $\ell_q$, so nothing after $q$ needs rechecking: a constant-time feasible insertion.

  • Shaw removal: take out customers related in space, time and demand, so repair has room to rearrange them.
  • Regret-k repair: insert first the customer with most to lose, $\sum_{h=2}^{k} (\text{cost}_h - \text{cost}_1)$, over its best position per route.
  • A new route is always an option. Without it, repair can never increase the number of routes, and minimising distance sometimes needs more of them. The first prototype lacked it and got stuck 13 % above optimal on R201.25.
  • Annealing acceptance, and three destroy × three repair operators with adaptive weights (unit 26).

The constant-time test is due to Savelsbergh (1992), and every serious VRPTW heuristic uses a form of it. The lab's test checks it against a full reschedule at every position of 40 routes on six instances.

The missing new-route option is worth dwelling on, because it's a design bug no test for "valid output" would catch. Every solution was feasible. It was simply confined to a region of the search space. It surfaced only by comparing with the exact methods' optima, which is what the capstone's structure is for.

Hybrid genetic search (Vidal 2022) is the heuristic state of the art for CVRP and its time-window variants. ALNS here is the unit-26 design carried over, not a competitor to HGS.

section 2

Refereeing

A result is a claim. The referee checks it.

capstone · referee

What the harness holds fixed, and what it measures

trustevery solution re-checked, every cost recomputed
runawayskilled 30 s past the limit or beyond 2.5 GB, and recorded

The referee also runs a violation checker on every answer. Six runs side by side on eight cores, so absolute seconds include some contention. Every method pays it.

primal gap (Berthold 2013)

$\gamma(v, v^*) = |v - v^*| / \max(|v|, |v^*|)$, 1 with no solution. $v^*$ is the best value any method found.

On the example ($v^* = 200$): inserting 4 at the end of $0 \to 1 \to 2 \to 3 \to 0$ gives $v = 220$, $\gamma = 20/220 = 0.091$. Serving 4 on its own route gives $140 + 160 = 300$, $\gamma = 100/300 = 0.333$. No solution: 1.

two questions, two metrics

Who finds good solutions? Primal gap at the limit, all four methods. Who proves optimality fastest? PAR1 seconds, the three exact methods. ALNS never proves anything, so it has no place in the second.

Paired over instances, Wilcoxon signed-rank, Holm over all pairs at once.

The two-metric split is the main methodological decision. Folding "found the optimum" and "proved it" into one score either penalises ALNS for not proving (unfair), or rewards the exact methods' proofs at nothing (uninformative).

The limitation that matters most: each (method, instance) runs once, with seed 0. Unit 27 found that SCIP's seed alone moves run times by large factors, and CP-SAT's single-worker search and ALNS are random too. So the verdicts are about this instance set, not about seed-to-seed variation. The honest extension is five seeds, averaged per instance before pairing, which head_to_head already supports. It needs five times the 11 hours of CPU.

A second limitation: a primal gap at a fixed limit throws away when each method got there. A primal integral (the area under the gap-versus-time curve) would keep it, but needs each method to log its incumbents over time.

Integrity: no invalid solution, no crash, no "optimal" refuted (no refuted claim), no two different proven optima.

  • B&P is all or nothing. Its gaps are runs with no solution: 3, 26 and 49 of 56. Ten runs overran the limit inside one uninterruptible pricing call.
  • ALNS's gaps flatter it, because the "best found" is often its own value. Against proven optima: 47 of 51 at n = 25, 20 of 21 at 50, 9 of 11 at 100. The worst miss is 7.7%, on C205.100.

The two readings of the gap column show why a single summary number misleads. Mean primal gap punishes a method that sometimes returns nothing (B&P) far more than one that is always a little off (the MIP), and it rewards whichever method defines the reference.

The SINTEF sanity check: at n = 100 the best distance found is a median 0.996 of SINTEF's hierarchical best-known distance. The objectives differ, so that says only that the solutions are of the right quality, not that they compete with the table.

The window / horizon column is the mean window width divided by the depot's horizon. It's a crude feature. Series 2's horizons are several times longer, so a similar ratio hides much longer absolute windows, and route length is what actually drives labelling cost. A better report would regress the winner on route length in the best solution, which the stored runs allow.

"Indistinguishable" at n = 25 is mostly ties: on 33 to 45 of 56 instances both methods reach the same value. The tests aren't weak there. The methods genuinely agree.

the report's rule

Every sentence of NOTES.md ties a method to an instance characteristic, points at a table, and names the test behind it. Where the test says indistinguishable, the sentence says so.

The C104.25 example is the most useful line on this slide. The same code on the same instance proved optimality in 39.5 s during the prototype run, with one other job running. In the benchmark it ran with five others and hit the limit with nothing. Near-limit results are fragile, and the only defence is replication.
