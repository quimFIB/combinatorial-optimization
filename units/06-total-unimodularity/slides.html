06

Total unimodularity: when integrality is free

The structural reason assignment, transportation and flow problems need no branching, and the smallest structure that ends it.

needs 03illuminates 14, 15~1 h slides + 2 h lab

Unit 05 said the ideal formulation of an integer program is the convex hull of its solutions, and that it is usually out of reach. This unit is about the important exception: problems whose natural formulation already is the convex hull. For them the LP relaxation solves the integer problem, which explains why trunk B (flows, matching, assignment) is polynomial.

It is a branch unit and the express route skips it. If you take trunk B, read it alongside units 14 and 15: it is the polyhedral explanation for everything that happens there.

section 1

The theorem

An algebraic condition on $A$ that makes every vertex integral.

06 · theorem

Totally unimodular matrices

definition

$A$ is totally unimodular (TU) if every square submatrix has determinant in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$.

In particular every entry is $-1$, $0$ or $1$.

Hoffman & Kruskal, 1956

For integral $A$: $\{x \ge 0 : Ax \le b\}$ has only integral vertices for every integral $b$ if and only if $A$ is TU.

proof of "if"

A vertex solves $B x_B = b'$ for a nonsingular square submatrix $B$ of $[A \mid I]$ and integral $b'$. That submatrix has $\det B = \pm 1$, because $[A \mid I]$ is TU when $A$ is. By Cramer's rule, $x_B = \dfrac{\mathrm{adj}(B)\, b'}{\det B}$, which is integral. $\square$

"Only if" is harder: from a submatrix with determinant $\pm 2$ or more, construct an integral $b$ whose polyhedron has a fractional vertex.

Why $[A \mid I]$ is TU when $A$ is: expand the determinant of any square submatrix along its identity columns. Each such column has a single 1, and removing its row and column leaves a square submatrix of $A$ (up to sign). So every determinant of $[A\mid I]$ equals, up to sign, a determinant of $A$.

"For every integral $b$" is essential. A matrix that is not TU can still give an integral polyhedron for one particular $b$. TU is the property that survives changing the right-hand side, which is what you need when branching changes bounds or when the data changes.

Since the dual LP's constraint matrix is $A^\top$, which is TU whenever $A$ is, integral $c$ also gives integral dual vertices. That is how König's theorem ($\nu = \tau$ on bipartite graphs, unit 00) falls out of LP duality: both LPs have integral optima, and their values are equal.

section 2

Recognizing TU

In practice you spot the structure; you don't compute determinants.

06 · recognizing

Three families

bipartite incidence

Rows are the vertices, split into two sides; each column has one 1 on each side.

Proof by Ghouila-Houri: sign left-side rows +1 and right-side rows −1. Every column sums to 0 or ±1.

network matrices

Directed incidence: each column has one +1 and one −1. Tutte's network matrices generalize these (paths in a tree).

Ghouila-Houri: sign every row +1, and each column sums to 0 or ±1 over any subset.

interval matrices

Each row (or column) has its ones consecutive.

Ghouila-Houri: order the chosen rows by column index and alternate signs +, −, +, … Every interval then sums to 0 or ±1.

Ghouila-Houri, 1962

$A$ is TU if and only if every subset $R$ of rows has a signing $\sigma \in \{\pm1\}^R$ with $\sum_{r \in R} \sigma_r a_r \in \{-1, 0, 1\}^n$.

The interval argument, carefully: for a chosen set of columns (applying the criterion to $A^\top$, since TU is closed under transpose), sort them and alternate signs. A row whose ones form an interval covers a consecutive run of the chosen columns, and an alternating sum over a consecutive run is 0 or ±1.

Operations that preserve TU: transposing; multiplying a row or column by −1; permuting, duplicating, or deleting rows and columns; appending a unit row or column; and pivoting. These let you check a model's matrix by transforming it into a known family. A flow model with extra bound rows is still TU, for example.

The characterization theorem (Seymour, 1980): every TU matrix can be built by certain "1-, 2- and 3-sums" from network matrices, their transposes, and two special 5×5 matrices. This gives a polynomial recognition algorithm. It is deep, and in modelling you essentially never need it.

An $n\times n$ matrix has $\binom{2n}{n} - 1$ square submatrices, which grows like $4^n$.

in practice

You recognize the structure while modelling: "this is a flow with side constraints", "these rows are intervals". If extra side constraints break TU, expect to branch, and look for a different formulation that keeps the TU core.

The count: $\sum_k \binom{n}{k}^2 = \binom{2n}{n}$ by Vandermonde's identity, minus 1 for the empty submatrix. At $n = 10$ that is 184 755, which matches the table.

A common real-world situation is a TU core plus a few "complicating" constraints: a network flow with a budget row, or an assignment with a side constraint. The side constraints break integrality, but only mildly. Lagrangian relaxation (unit 11) exploits exactly this: dualize the few bad rows and solve the TU remainder as a fast combinatorial problem.

section 3

Where it ends

One odd cycle is enough.

06 · limits

The odd cycle

Max matching LP on a triangle: value $\tfrac32$ at the vertex $x = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12, \tfrac12)$; the best integer matching has value 1.

The incidence matrix of a triangle has determinant 2:

$$\det\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\0&1&1\\1&0&1\end{pmatrix} = 2$$
theorem

A graph's incidence matrix is TU if and only if the graph is bipartite. The obstruction is an odd cycle: the incidence matrix of an odd cycle has determinant $\pm 2$.

Lab step 3's test checks "TU iff bipartite" on 30 random graphs, and step 5 builds the odd cycle.

Determinant of an odd cycle's incidence matrix: expanding along the first row gives $1 + (-1)^{k+1} = 2$ for odd $k$, and 0 for even $k$. The even cycle is fine; the odd one is not.

This is also the triangle of unit 00: the matching lower bound on vertex cover failed there. Both facts have the same cause. The LPs for matching and vertex cover are dual, and on a triangle both have the fractional optimum $\tfrac32$.

06 · limits

Half-integrality, and Edmonds' fix

Balinski, 1965

Every vertex of the fractional matching polytope $\{x \ge 0 : \sum_{e \ni v} x_e \le 1\}$ has entries in $\{0, \tfrac12, 1\}$, and the $\tfrac12$ entries form vertex-disjoint odd cycles.

Lab then, part 2: on 197 random non-bipartite graphs the LP vertex was fractional 42 times, and the only fractional value ever seen was 0.5. On 203 bipartite graphs it was fractional 0 times.

Edmonds, 1965

Add the odd-set inequalities: for every vertex set $S$ with $|S|$ odd, $\displaystyle\sum_{e \subseteq S} x_e \le \frac{|S|-1}{2}$.

The result is exactly the matching polytope: conv of matchings, integral for every graph.

There are exponentially many such inequalities, and by unit 05 no polynomial extended formulation exists (Rothvoss). The way through is separation (unit 09), or Edmonds' blossom algorithm (unit 15).

The triangle violates its own odd-set inequality: $S = $ all three vertices, $|S| = 3$, and the inequality says the three edge values sum to at most 1, while the LP point sums to $\tfrac32$. Adding that one inequality makes the triangle's LP integral. This is a cutting plane in the sense of unit 08: valid for all matchings and violated by the fractional vertex.

Edmonds' 1965 papers are where "polynomial time" was first argued to be the right notion of efficient ("Paths, trees, and flowers"). The blossom algorithm solves maximum matching in polynomial time without ever writing the exponential LP down, and its correctness proof is an LP duality argument over the odd-set inequalities.

06 · limits

Beyond TU

balanced matrices

0/1 matrices with no odd submatrix cycle. Set packing, covering and partitioning over them have integral LPs (Berge; Fulkerson, Hoffman & Oppenheim).

perfect matrices

Clique constraints of perfect graphs: the stable-set LP with clique rows is integral (Chvátal; Lovász's perfect graph theorem).

TDI systems

Total dual integrality (Edmonds & Giles 1977): the dual has integral optima for every integral $c$. Then the primal polyhedron is integral, whatever $A$ is.

The general pattern for the rest of the course: integral polyhedra are the polynomial islands. Each has a combinatorial algorithm, and a min–max theorem that is LP duality in disguise.

TDI is the most general of these and the most useful for proofs. A system is TDI if its dual has integral optimal solutions whenever the objective is integral. Edmonds and Giles showed that a TDI system with integral right-hand side describes an integral polyhedron. Matroid intersection (unit 13), matching, and submodular flows are all proved integral this way.

Unit 23's approximation algorithms live on the opposite side of this: problems whose natural LPs are provably not integral, where the question becomes how far from integral, which is the integrality gap of unit 24.
