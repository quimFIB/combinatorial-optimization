04

Polynomial-time LP: ellipsoid and interior point

Two ways through the middle of the polyhedron: one that settled a theoretical question and is never used, and one that runs in every modern solver.

needs 02feeds 09~1½ h slides + 2½ h lab

This is a branch unit, so the express route skips it. It is worth doing for two reasons. First, unit 09's separation–optimization equivalence runs on the ellipsoid method, and that theorem makes much of Part II's machinery (exponentially many cuts, lazy constraints) theoretically respectable. Second, choosing between simplex and interior point is a real decision you make whenever you configure a solver, and this unit gives you the reasons.

Convention: $\max\{c^\top x : Ax \le b\}$ with $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ and no separate sign constraints. The barrier needs every inequality as a row.

section 1

Is LP in P?

Open from 1947 until 1979, even though LPs were being solved every day.

04 · history

What was known, and what wasn't

  • 1947 Dantzig's simplex method. Fast in practice.
  • 1972 Klee–Minty: exponential on a built family (unit 02).
  • 1970s LP is in NP ∩ coNP: short certificates both ways (units 01, 03). No NP-hardness was expected, but no polynomial algorithm was known.
  • 1979 Khachiyan: the ellipsoid method solves LP in polynomial time.
  • 1984 Karmarkar: a polynomial interior-point method that also looked practical.
  • 1990s– primal–dual IPMs (Mehrotra 1992) in every commercial solver.
"polynomial" for LP

Polynomial in the bit size $L$ of the input: the number of bits needed to write $A, b, c$ as integers.

Whether there is a strongly polynomial LP algorithm, polynomial in $m$ and $n$ alone, independent of the numbers, is still open. It is one of Smale's problems for the 21st century.

Why bit size matters: both polynomial methods converge to the optimum geometrically and stop when the accuracy is fine enough to round to the exact vertex. How fine "fine enough" is depends on how many bits the vertex's coordinates can have. By Cramer's rule that is polynomial in $L$, and that is where $L$ enters the running time.

Strongly polynomial algorithms are known for special LPs: network flows (Tardos 1985, unit 14), and LPs whose constraint matrix has small integer entries (Tardos 1986, where the running time depends only on the size of $A$, not of $b$ and $c$). The general question remains open.

section 2

The ellipsoid method

Binary search in $n$ dimensions, with ellipsoids as the intervals.

04 · ellipsoid

The idea

In 2-D the new ellipse has semi-axes $\tfrac23$ and $\tfrac{2}{\sqrt3}$ of the old radius, and $\tfrac23 \cdot \tfrac{2}{\sqrt3} \approx 0.77$ of the area.

  1. Start with a ball $E_0$ of radius $R$ that contains the feasible set, if nonempty.
  2. If the centre is feasible, stop.
  3. Otherwise a violated row $a^\top z \le \beta$ separates the centre from every feasible point. Keep the half $\{a^\top z \le a^\top \text{centre}\}$.
  4. Replace $E_k$ by the smallest ellipsoid containing that half, and repeat.

Each step shrinks the volume by a fixed factor, so the ellipsoid soon becomes too small to contain the feasible set. The set was therefore empty, or the centre found it.

Why a half-ellipsoid and not the exact intersection with the feasible set: the exact region would be a complicated polytope, and the method's power comes from keeping a description of constant size (a centre and a matrix) no matter how many cuts have been made.

The figure is exact for $n = 2$ and the unit ball cut through the centre. The new centre moves $\tfrac{1}{n+1} = \tfrac13$ of the radius away from the cut. The semi-axis along the cut normal is $\tfrac{n}{n+1} = \tfrac23$, and the others are $\tfrac{n}{\sqrt{n^2-1}} = \tfrac{2}{\sqrt3}$. The new ellipse passes through the three extreme points of the kept half-disc: the leftmost point, and the two endpoints of the cut.

04 · ellipsoid

The update, and why the volume shrinks

For $E = \{z : (z-x)^\top P^{-1}(z-x) \le 1\}$ and cut normal $a$:

$$g = \frac{P a}{\sqrt{a^\top P a}}, \qquad x' = x - \frac{g}{n+1}$$ $$P' = \frac{n^2}{n^2-1}\left(P - \frac{2}{n+1}\, g g^\top\right)$$

An affine map takes $E$ to the unit ball and $a$ to $e_1$, so it suffices to check the unit ball, where the new ellipsoid has the axes on the previous slide.

volume ratio $$\begin{aligned} \frac{\mathrm{vol}\,E'}{\mathrm{vol}\,E} &= \frac{n}{n+1}\left(\frac{n}{\sqrt{n^2-1}}\right)^{n-1} \\ &< e^{-1/(2(n+1))} \end{aligned}$$
so, with lab step 5

If the feasible set, when nonempty, contains a ball of radius $r$ inside the ball of radius $R$, then after

$$k \ge 2(n+1)\, n \ln(R/r)$$

steps the ellipsoid is smaller than that ball, and the method may stop.

The inequality: using $1 + u \le e^u$, $\frac{n}{n+1} = 1 - \frac{1}{n+1} \le e^{-1/(n+1)}$ and $\left(\frac{n^2}{n^2-1}\right)^{(n-1)/2} = \left(1 + \frac{1}{n^2-1}\right)^{(n-1)/2} \le e^{(n-1)/(2(n^2-1))} = e^{1/(2(n+1))}$. Multiplying gives $e^{-1/(n+1) + 1/(2(n+1))} = e^{-1/(2(n+1))}$. The first inequality is strict, so the whole thing is.

Lab step 4's test checks both properties numerically. It samples 400 points of the kept half and checks they lie in the new ellipsoid, and it compares the determinant ratio with the bound.

Step 5's bound: $\mathrm{vol}(E_k) < e^{-k/(2(n+1))}\mathrm{vol}(B_R)$, and $\mathrm{vol}(B_r) = (r/R)^n \mathrm{vol}(B_R)$. The ellipsoid is smaller than the $r$-ball once $e^{-k/(2(n+1))} < (r/R)^n$, that is, once $k > 2n(n+1)\ln(R/r)$.

04 · ellipsoid

From feasibility to polynomial-time LP

Three gaps between the lab's version and Khachiyan's theorem:

  • $R$ from the data. If a solution exists, one exists with $\|x\|_\infty \le 2^{O(L)}$ (Cramer's rule on a basis).
  • $r$ from the data. A feasible polytope may be flat. Relax each $b_i$ by $2^{-O(L)}$: the system stays feasible exactly when the original is, and now contains a ball of radius $2^{-O(L)}$.
  • Optimization. Add $c^\top x \ge \gamma$ and search over $\gamma$, or use "sliding objective" cuts.
Khachiyan, 1979

LP feasibility is decided in $O(n^2 L)$ ellipsoid iterations, each with polynomially many bits of precision. So LP ∈ P.

The exact constant in the iteration count depends on how $L$ is defined and on the variant; $O(n^2 L)$ is the order usually quoted. Each iteration must also be carried out with rounding, keeping $O(nL)$ bits per number, and the analysis has to show that the rounding errors don't break the volume argument. That is the technical heart of the proof, in Grötschel, Lovász & Schrijver ch. 3.

The relaxation trick for flat polytopes: if $Ax \le b$ has an integral description with bit size $L$, then $Ax \le b + \varepsilon\mathbf 1$ with $\varepsilon = 2^{-O(L)}$ is feasible if and only if $Ax \le b$ is. Proof sketch: if the relaxed system is feasible, its Farkas certificate for infeasibility of the original, which has bounded size, would still apply for tiny $\varepsilon$.

04 · ellipsoid

Useless in practice, indispensable in theory

why nobody solves LPs with it
  • The iteration count is close to its worst case on every instance. It never gets lucky the way simplex does.
  • It needs high numerical precision, and double precision breaks the volume argument.
  • Each iteration uses a dense $n \times n$ matrix, so sparsity doesn't help.
what it really needs

Look at step 3 again. The method never reads the constraint matrix. It only asks, of one point: "is this feasible, and if not, give me a violated inequality."

That is a separation oracle. It works even when there are exponentially many constraints, provided violated ones can be found efficiently. This is unit 09.

The consequence, due to Grötschel, Lovász and Schrijver (1981): optimization over a polytope is polynomial if and only if separation over it is polynomial. For example, the matching polytope has exponentially many odd-set inequalities, but a violated one can be found by a minimum cut computation, so maximum-weight matching can be solved as an LP in polynomial time, via the ellipsoid method, without Edmonds' algorithm. Unit 09 develops this for subtour elimination in the TSP.

The same "only needs a separation oracle" property is why cutting-plane methods for convex optimization (Vaidya, and more recent work) are studied. They are the practical descendants of the ellipsoid method.

section 3

Interior-point methods

Stay away from the boundary until the very end.

04 · interior point

The log barrier and the central path

Central path of $\max 2x + y$ on unit 01's polygon, computed by the lab's reference solution ($t = 0.01 \cdot 3^k$).

Replace the constraints by a penalty that is infinite on the boundary:

$$\min_x\; f_t(x) = -t\, c^\top x - \sum_{i=1}^m \log(b_i - a_i^\top x)$$
  • $t \to 0$: the objective hardly matters, and the minimizer is the analytic centre.
  • $t \to \infty$: the objective dominates, and the minimizer tends to an optimal point.

The minimizers $x^*(t)$ form a smooth curve, the central path.

$f_t$ is strictly convex whenever $A$ has full column rank: the Hessian $A^\top \mathrm{diag}(1/s^2) A$ is positive definite. So each $t$ has a unique minimizer, found by Newton's method. That is lab step 2.

The path bends sharply near the optimum, visible in the figure between $t = 0.81$ and $t = 7.3$. Each centering starts from the previous centre, which is close, so Newton converges quadratically in a handful of steps: about 6 per centre in the lab's runs.

The analytic centre depends on the description of the polytope, not just its shape: adding a redundant constraint moves it. That matters when the same polytope is written two ways (unit 05), and it is one of the reasons IPM behaviour is sensitive to the formulation.

04 · interior point

Duality comes for free on the path

At the minimizer the gradient vanishes. With $s = b - Ax$:

$$\begin{aligned} &-t\,c + A^\top (1/s) = 0 \\ \Longrightarrow\;\; &A^\top y = c \quad\text{for}\quad y = \frac{1}{t\, s} > 0 \end{aligned}$$

So $y$ is dual feasible, and the duality gap is exactly

$$b^\top y - c^\top x = y^\top (b - Ax) = \sum_i \frac{s_i}{t\, s_i} = \frac{m}{t}.$$
consequence

$x^*(t)$ is within $m/t$ of optimal, with a certificate. To reach accuracy $\varepsilon$, run until $t \ge m/\varepsilon$.

Path following: centre at $t$, then set $t \leftarrow \mu t$ and re-centre from there. With $\mu = 10$ the number of centerings is $\lceil \log_{10}(m/(t_0\varepsilon)) \rceil$.

Theory: with $\mu = 1 + 1/\sqrt{m}$, each centering needs $O(1)$ Newton steps, for $O(\sqrt m \log(m/\varepsilon))$ in total.

Compare with complementary slackness (unit 03), which asks for $y_i s_i = 0$. On the central path $y_i s_i = 1/t$ for every $i$: approximate complementary slackness, with the same value in every row. Primal–dual interior-point methods are built exactly on this. They solve the perturbed KKT system $Ax + s = b$, $A^\top y = c$, $y_i s_i = \mu$ by Newton's method in all of $(x, y, s)$ together, and drive $\mu$ to zero.

The $O(\sqrt m \log(1/\varepsilon))$ bound comes from self-concordance theory (Nesterov & Nemirovski, 1994), which also explains why the log barrier is the right choice. Practical codes use long steps and a predictor–corrector scheme (Mehrotra 1992), and take 20–60 iterations almost regardless of problem size. The lab's then saw 10 to 25 as $n$ went from 100 to 20 000.

why

The Hessian $A^\top \mathrm{diag}(1/s^2) A$ has entries of size $1/s_i^2$. Near the optimum some slacks are $\approx 10^{-7}$ and others $\approx 1$, so its condition number reaches $10^{14}$ and beyond, and the Newton direction becomes numerical noise.

Production IPMs solve the primal–dual system with careful factorizations, and hand over to simplex (crossover) for an exact vertex.

At $t = 10^7$ the Newton iteration hit its 100-step cap without meeting the stopping test, and the resulting point is worse than at $t = 10^6$. At $t = 10^8$ it happened to recover. Erratic behaviour past a threshold is the signature of floating-point breakdown, not of a bug. The lab's tests stop checking centrality at $t = 10^5$ for this reason, and the default stopping gap is $10^{-5}$.

Crossover: an IPM returns a point in the relative interior of the optimal face. Simplex-based branch-and-bound and sensitivity analysis need a basis (a vertex). Crossover (Megiddo 1991) pushes the interior point to a vertex of the same objective value with a sequence of simplex-like steps. It is usually quick, and occasionally the slowest part of the whole solve.

Simplex iterations grew faster than $n$ here. Each IPM iteration is more expensive (a sparse Cholesky factorization), but there are few of them.

Crossover on this family is at $n \approx 300$.

The $n = 20\,000$ row comes from then --big. Structured real LPs vary widely: some favour simplex even at large sizes, which is why solvers can run several methods concurrently.

What determines the cost of an IPM iteration is the sparsity of $A\,\mathrm{diag}(y/s)\,A^\top$ and of its Cholesky factor. For random sparse matrices the fill-in stays manageable. For LPs with a few dense columns (a column touching every row) the normal-equations matrix becomes dense, and IPMs slow down badly unless the dense columns are handled specially.

Gurobi's and HiGHS's default "concurrent" or "choose" settings exist because no rule predicts the winner reliably. If you care about one class of LPs, measure it, which is exactly what unit 28 is about.

04 · practice

Warm solves: why branch-and-bound wants simplex

Same LP family, $n = 3000$. Solve, add one constraint that cuts off the optimum, re-solve:

coldre-solve
HiGHS simplex0.832 s0.033 s (22 it)
HiGHS IPM0.131 s0.136 s (18 it)

Simplex re-solves 25× faster than it solves cold. IPM re-solves no faster than cold.

why IPMs can't warm-start well

The old optimum sits on the boundary, with some slacks $\approx 0$. That is the worst starting point for a barrier method, which needs to begin well inside and on the central path. The old basis, by contrast, is one dual simplex pivot or so from optimal (unit 03).

So in branch-and-bound (unit 07), solvers often use IPM or concurrent solving at the root LP, crossover to a basis, and dual simplex at every node after that. The tree's thousands of re-solves dominate the total time, and each is a small change from its parent.

Warm-starting IPMs is an active research topic. Techniques exist (shifting the old point inward, or storing an earlier iterate from before the path reached the boundary), but they give modest speedups compared with a simplex warm start.
