Lagrangian relaxation and subgradient methods

Price the hard constraints into the objective and solve what is left. Every price gives a bound; the best price gives exactly the bound you can predict in advance.

needs 03, 16~1¾ h slides + 2½ h lablab: uv run co lab 11

Unit 10 reached a strong bound by building an LP with exponentially many columns. This unit reaches the same bound without any LP solver: pick prices for the complicating constraints, solve an easy combinatorial problem, and adjust the prices. The two methods are dual views of one convexification, and the unit's main theorem (Geoffrion) says precisely which bound you get.

Lagrangian relaxation was the dominant bounding technique for combinatorial optimisation from the 1970s until LP solvers became fast in the 1990s. It is still the method of choice when the relaxed problem has a fast specialised algorithm: spanning trees, shortest paths, knapsacks.

How to read this deck

Two small examples carry it. A three-variable integer program with one dualised constraint, whose $L(u)$ is drawn exactly and climbed by hand; and five cities, where one subgradient step turns a 1-tree into an optimal tour and two edges are fixed. All values were computed with the lab's reference solution. The proof of Geoffrion's theorem is in the notes.

section 1

Dualising

Move the hard constraints into the objective.

11 · dualising

The Lagrangian function

$$z = \min \{\, c^\top x : Ax \ge b,\ x \in X \,\}$$

$Ax \ge b$ are the complicating constraints; optimising over $X$ alone is easy. Dualise them with multipliers $u \ge 0$, giving the Lagrangian function:

$$L(u) = \min_{x \in X}\ c^\top x + u^\top (b - Ax)$$
weak duality

For any feasible $x$ and $u \ge 0$, $u^\top(b - Ax) \le 0$, so $L(u) \le c^\top x$. Hence $L(u) \le z$.

Equality constraints get free-sign multipliers. The GAP lab dualises "each job exactly once" with $u \in \mathbb R^n$.

$L$ is the lower envelope of the affine functions $u \mapsto c^\top x + u^\top(b - Ax)$, one per $x \in X$. So it is concave and piecewise linear, and non-differentiable exactly at its maximum.

The Lagrangian dual is $z_{LD} = \max_{u \ge 0} L(u)$, a concave maximisation, so there are no local maxima to get stuck in. The price is non-smoothness. Its kinks are where two solutions $x \in X$ tie, and the optimum is almost always at a kink. That's why gradient methods don't apply directly, and section 2 uses subgradients.

Interpretation: $u_i$ is a price per unit of violation of constraint $i$. Too low, and the relaxed solution ignores the constraint. Too high, and it over-satisfies it. The best prices balance the two, which is the same economics as the duals in unit 03.

11 · dualising · example

L(u) on a three-variable example

$L(u)$ for the example. It rises while the items bought weigh less than 5, peaks at $u = \tfrac43$, and falls once they weigh more.

$$\begin{aligned}\min\ & 3x_1 + 5x_2 + 4x_3 \\ \text{s.t. } & 2x_1 + 4x_2 + 3x_3 \ge 5, \quad x \in \{0,1\}^3\end{aligned}$$

Dualise the one constraint. For a price $u$ each item costs $c_j - u\,w_j$; take it if that is negative:

$$L(u) = 5u + \textstyle\sum_j \min(0,\ c_j - u\,w_j)$$
two prices by hand

$u = 1$: item costs $3-2,\ 5-4,\ 4-3 = 1, 1, 1$, all positive, buy nothing. $L = 5$. Violation $g = 5 - 0 = 5$: the price is too low.

$u = 2$: costs $-1, -3, -2$, buy all three. $L = 10 - 6 = 4$. $g = 5 - 9 = -4$: too high.

The best price, $u = \tfrac43$, gives $\tfrac{19}{3} = 6.33$, below the true optimum 7 (buy items 1 and 3).

explore interactively

The pieces. Item $j$ becomes worth buying when $u > c_j / w_j$: item 2 at $\tfrac54$, item 3 at $\tfrac43$, item 1 at $\tfrac32$. So $L = 5u$ up to $\tfrac54$, then $5u + 5 - 4u = u + 5$, then $u + 5 + 4 - 3u = 9 - 2u$ after $\tfrac43$, then $12 - 4u$ after $\tfrac32$. The maximum is at $\tfrac43$: $\tfrac43 + 5 = \tfrac{19}{3}$. The slope changes from $+1$ to $-2$ there, and the subgradients at the kink are the violations $g = 1$ (buy item 2 only) and $g = -2$ (buy items 2 and 3).

The integer optimum. Sets that cover weight 5: $\{1,2\}$ cost 8, $\{1,3\}$ cost 7, $\{2,3\}$ cost 9, all three cost 12. So $z = 7$.

Why the bound equals the LP. $X = \{0,1\}^3$ is a box, whose LP is integral: the integrality property of the next slide. The LP relaxation takes $x_2 = 1$ and $x_3 = \tfrac13$, cost $5 + \tfrac43 = \tfrac{19}{3}$, the same number.

11 · dualising

Geoffrion 1974: which bound you get

theorem $$z_{LD} = \min \{\, c^\top x : Ax \ge b,\ x \in \mathrm{conv}(X) \,\}$$

So $z_{LP} \le z_{LD} \le z$, where $z_{LP}$ also relaxes $X$ to its LP description $\bar X$.

integrality property

If $\mathrm{conv}(X) = \bar X$ (the easy problem's LP has integral vertices), then $z_{LD} = z_{LP}$. The Lagrangian relaxation gives nothing the LP didn't, though it may compute it faster. The example's box $\{0,1\}^3$ is such an $X$: 6.33 both ways.

why: notes (N)

It is the same bound as Dantzig–Wolfe (unit 10), whose master convexifies $X$ explicitly.

Proof sketch

$L(u) = \min_{x \in \mathrm{conv}(X)} c^\top x + u^\top(b - Ax)$, because a linear function is minimised at an extreme point of the hull. Maximising over $u \ge 0$ is then LP duality applied to $\min\{c^\top x : Ax \ge b, x \in \mathrm{conv}(X)\}$, with only $Ax \ge b$ dualised.

The design choice in every Lagrangian relaxation: dualise enough that the rest is easy, but keep a subproblem without the integrality property, or you have reinvented the LP bound. The Held–Karp bound is the famous case where the subproblem has the property and that's still a good trade. The LP it matches (the subtour LP) has exponentially many constraints, and the 1-tree avoids them.

Subgradients

Climbing a function with no gradient.

11 · subgradients

Subgradient ascent

subgradient

If $x(u)$ attains $L(u)$, then $g = b - Ax(u)$ satisfies $L(v) \le L(u) + g^\top (v - u)$ for all $v$. Computing it is free: the violation of the dualised constraints.

$$u \leftarrow \bigl[u + t_k\, g\bigr]_+$$
  • Diminishing $t_k$ with $\sum t_k = \infty$ and $\sum t_k^2 < \infty$ converges, slowly: $O(1/\varepsilon^2)$ iterations.
  • Polyak: $t_k = \lambda_k (\bar z - L(u))/\lVert g\rVert^2$, with $\bar z$ an upper bound. Held–Wolfe–Crowder: halve $\lambda$ when progress stalls. That's lab step 2.
not an ascent method

A subgradient step can decrease $L$, even with a tiny step. The subgradient guarantees getting closer to the optimal multipliers, not a higher value. Always keep the best bound so far.

Lab then, bound A on GAP 8 × 60, 300 iterations:

Why a step can go down: the subgradient inequality says the whole function lies below the tangent plane through $u$. Moving along $g$ keeps the optimal $u^*$ on the side $g^\top(u^* - u) \ge L(u^*) - L(u) \ge 0$. So the distance to $u^*$ shrinks for small enough steps, but the function value along the path can fall. The Polyak step is the one that minimises the guaranteed distance bound when the optimum value is known.

The sparklines printed by then show the three behaviours (the zig-zag). A fixed large λ bounces between 887 and 899 indefinitely. A fixed small λ climbs smoothly and stalls well short. Halving on stalls combines the fast start with the settling.

The halving column uses a stricter rule than the lab (which waits 20 failures) so that the effect shows within a few steps. From $k = 3$ onward: $\lambda = 0.5$ gives a move of $-0.2$ to 1.2; there $L = 6$, $g = 5$, and with $\lambda = 0.25$ the move is $+0.05$ to 1.25, where item 2 is exactly indifferent and $L = 6.25$ whichever way it is decided. The steps continue toward the kink at $\tfrac43$.

Every iterate's $L$ is a valid lower bound on 7, including the bad ones. That is why the method only has to remember the best, not climb monotonically.

11 · subgradients

Beyond plain subgradients

cutting planes on the dual = column generation

Every $x \in X$ you've seen gives an affine upper piece of $L$. Maximising the envelope of the pieces so far is an LP: Kelley's cutting-plane method. Its dual is unit 10's restricted master, and its columns are the $x$'s. It has the same wild oscillation that unit 10 stabilised.

  • Bundle methods (Lemaréchal, Kiwiel): the cutting-plane model plus a quadratic proximal term. The stabilisation that works, with convergence guarantees.
  • Volume algorithm (Barahona & Anbil 2000): a subgradient method that also averages the relaxed solutions into an approximate primal LP solution.
  • Primal recovery from subgradients: averages of $x(u_k)$ with suitable weights converge to an optimal solution over $\mathrm{conv}(X)$.

Rule of thumb: subgradients for speed and simplicity with a cheap oracle; bundle or column generation when you need the exact bound or primal information.

The equivalence is worth checking by hand once. The LP $\max\{\eta : \eta \le c^\top x^k + u^\top(b - Ax^k)\ \forall k,\ u \ge 0\}$ has one dual variable $\lambda_k \ge 0$ per known solution $x^k$, with $\sum \lambda_k = 1$. Writing out its dual gives $\min \sum_k \lambda_k c^\top x^k$ subject to $\sum_k \lambda_k Ax^k \ge b$: the Dantzig–Wolfe master over the known columns. Pricing is evaluating $L$ at the new $u$.

11 · Held–Karp

1-trees and the Held–Karp bound (1970–71)

A 1-tree is a spanning tree on $\{1, \dots, n-1\}$ plus two edges at vertex 0. Every tour is a 1-tree with all degrees 2.

Dualise "degree = 2" with vertex penalties $\pi_v$: edge costs become $d_{ij} + \pi_i + \pi_j$, and

$$L(\pi) = \min_{T \text{ 1-tree}} \sum_{ij \in T} (d_{ij} + \pi_i + \pi_j) - 2 \sum_v \pi_v$$

Subgradient: $g_v = \deg_T(v) - 2$. Raise the price of vertices with too many edges. If $g = 0$, the 1-tree is a tour, and it's optimal.

the Held–Karp bound equals the subtour LP

The 1-tree polytope's LP description is integral (a matroid-type polytope, unit 13), so by Geoffrion

$$\max_\pi L(\pi) = z_{\text{subtour LP}}$$

This is the curriculum's "then" step: the Held–Karp bound and the root LP bound are close, and the reason is Geoffrion's theorem. The subtour LP is what a MIP solver reaches at the root after separating subtour cuts (unit 09), and the Lagrangian dual of the 1-tree relaxation is the same number. At $n = 150$ the ascent stopped at the 1000-iteration limit, 0.05% short.

Historically, Held and Karp's bound let branch-and-bound solve TSPs of 50–100 cities in the early 1970s. Concorde now uses LP with cutting planes instead, because an LP gives duals for cut separation and is exact. But LKH, the best heuristic, uses the Held–Karp multipliers to build its candidate edge lists (α-nearness, Helsgaun 2000). An edge's α-value is lab step 3's "cost of the cheapest 1-tree containing it" minus the minimum 1-tree's cost.

one step, and it is a tour

From the plain 1-tree, with $\bar z = 14$: $t = 2 \cdot (14 - 13) / 2 = 1$, so $\pi = (0, 0, -1, 0, 1)$. Edges at 2 get cheaper by 1, edges at 4 dearer by 1.

The new minimum 1-tree is the tour $0\!-\!3\!-\!2\!-\!1\!-\!4\!-\!0$, all degrees 2, length $3 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 2 = 14$. $L(\pi) = 14 = \bar z$: optimal, proved.

reduced-cost fixing, at π = 0

Force edge $13$ (length 5) into the plain 1-tree: it must replace the heaviest edge on the tree path $1\!-\!4\!-\!3$, which is 3. Cost $13 + 5 - 3 = 15 > 14$: no tour of length 14 uses edge 13. Fixed to 0. Edge $02$ is fixed the same way.

explore interactively

The minimum 1-tree. Prim's algorithm on vertices 1–4 takes $14$ (2), then the cheaper of $12$, $24$, $34$ (all 3): it picks $12$, then $34$ ($24$ would close a cycle). The reference one_tree returns bound 13, degrees $[2, 2, 1, 2, 3]$ and edges $14, 12, 34, 04, 03$.

The step. From the plain 1-tree, $\lVert g \rVert^2 = 2$, and with the lab's $\lambda = 2$ the Polyak step is $2 \cdot 1 / 2 = 1$. The reference held_karp_bound records exactly two values, 13 then 14. The optimal tour of the five cities, by brute force over the 24 orderings, has length 14.

Fixing. The reference fix_edges with $\bar z = 14$ returns $\{02, 13\}$. For $02$ (length 5): adding it at vertex 0 displaces the dearer of the two vertex-0 edges, 3, giving $13 + 5 - 3 = 15 > 14$. In a 5-city instance two edges out of ten is modest; the lab's larger instances fix around 90%.

11 · using the multipliers

Fixing variables, and repairing solutions

reduced-cost fixing

If every solution with $x_e = 1$ has Lagrangian value at least $L(u) + \rho_e$, and $L(u) + \rho_e > \bar z$, then $x_e = 0$ in every solution better than the incumbent $\bar z$.

For 1-trees: $\rho_e$ $= c_e - (\text{heaviest edge on the tree path})$, the matroid exchange of unit 13.

Lab then, with the optimum as $\bar z$: 89% / 89% / 93% / 96% of edges eliminated at $n$ = 30 / 60 / 100 / 150.

Lagrangian heuristics

The relaxed solution violates only the dualised constraints, and often only slightly. Repair it. For GAP from relaxation A: every agent is within capacity, jobs are over- or under-assigned, so keep the cheapest copy and place the rest by regret (lab step 5).

Fixing is the same logic as LP reduced-cost fixing in MIP solvers (unit 07's node LPs provide the reduced costs). It needs an upper bound, which is why good heuristics and good bounds reinforce each other. A better incumbent fixes more variables, and a smaller problem makes the next bound cheaper and tighter.

The repair heuristic's results are mixed, and honestly so. On tight instances a greedy repair can get stuck (8 × 60) or land 9% above the optimum (10 × 80) even from an excellent bound. Production codes run the repair every few iterations from different multipliers and follow it with local search.
