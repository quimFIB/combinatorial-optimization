Greedy set cover, purchase by purchase

Try: step through the three purchases and watch step 2 choose the cheaper-looking S3. Static version: greedy set cover on five elements.

What it shows. The deck's instance: elements 0–4, sets $S_0 = \{2\}$ (5), $S_1 = \{0\}$ (1), $S_2 = \{1,2,3,4\}$ (6), $S_3 = \{0,1,3,4\}$ (4). Each step shows every set's cost per still-uncovered element, the purchase, and the prices charged to the newly covered elements. The sum of the prices is always what greedy has paid.

Data: slides/viz/traces/u23.py runs the loop of the reference greedy_set_cover step by step and asserts it ends with the reference's own purchases and prices; the optimum 7 is by brute force over the 16 subsets.

The double tree and Christofides, step by step

Try: both algorithms on the six points, then on the eight points, where the matching has choices and Christofides wins. Static versions: double tree, Christofides, both on six points.

What it shows. Each algorithm's pieces in order: the minimum spanning tree (a lower bound), then either the doubled walk and its shortcut, or the odd vertices, the minimum-weight matching on them (red, drawn bent), the Euler circuit of tree plus matching, and its shortcut. Edge labels are distances.

  • Six points (the deck's, distances as on the slide): only vertices 3 and 5 are odd, so the matching is forced, and both algorithms find the same cycle, 62 against the optimum 54.
  • Eight points (random, seed recorded in the menu): all eight vertices odd, a real matching choice, and Christofides' tour is clearly shorter than the double tree's.

Data: slides/viz/traces/u23.py, from the reference prim, double_tree, odd_vertices, euler_circuit, shortcut and christofides, with the optimum by brute force over all tours from 0.
