Combinatorial approximation algorithms

Greedy, matchings, spanning trees and sorting, each with a lower bound on the optimum it can compute, and a proof that fits on one slide.

23 · guarantees

Every proof in this unit has one shape

OPT is unknown, and computing it is NP-hard. So find a lower bound $\mathrm{LB}$ with

$$\mathrm{ALG} \le \rho \cdot \mathrm{LB} \quad\text{and}\quad \mathrm{LB} \le \mathrm{OPT}$$

That makes it a $\rho$-approximation. The algorithm constructs its solution and its lower bound together. The lab returns both, so tests check each inequality separately.

the limit of the method

If some instance has $\mathrm{OPT} / \mathrm{LB} = r$, no analysis against that $\mathrm{LB}$ can prove a ratio below $r$. That's the integrality gap of unit 05 again, for bounds that aren't LPs.

For maximisation the shape flips: an upper bound $\mathrm{UB} \ge \mathrm{OPT}$ and $\mathrm{ALG} \ge \rho\,\mathrm{UB}$. Unit 25's Goemans–Williamson uses the SDP value as UB.

The ratio convention: for minimisation, ρ ≥ 1 and smaller is better. Some texts write maximisation ratios as ρ ≤ 1 (0.878) and some as 1/ρ ≥ 1 (1.139). This course writes Max-Cut's as 0.878, a fraction of the optimum.

Greedy set cover and its prices

Greedy set cover: buy the set with the least cost per newly covered element. Charge each new element that price.

price lemma

For every set $S$: $\sum_{e \in S} \mathrm{price}_e \le H_{|S|}\, c_S$.

why

While some of $S$ was still uncovered, $S$ itself was on offer at $c_S$ divided by what it still covered, and greedy never pays more than the best offer. The last element of $S$ costs at most $c_S$, the one before at most $c_S/2$, and so on: $H_{|S|}\,c_S$. Add over an optimal cover to get $\mathrm{ALG} \le H_d \cdot \mathrm{OPT}$.

full proof in the notes (N)

Johnson 1974; Lovász 1975; Chvátal 1979. Unit 22: $(1-\varepsilon)\ln n$ is NP-hard, so this is optimal.

tight family

Singletons $\{i\}$ at cost $L/(i+1)$, and the whole universe at $L + 1$ ($L = \mathrm{lcm}(1..n)$). Greedy takes every singleton, at total cost $L \cdot H_n$.

Full proof of the price lemma

List $S$'s elements in the order greedy covered them, last first: $e_k, \dots, e_1$. When $e_j$ was covered, at least $j$ elements of $S$ were uncovered, so $S$ offered a ratio of at most $c_S / j$. Greedy paid no more: $\mathrm{price}_{e_j} \le c_S / j$. Summing over $j$ gives $H_k\, c_S$. Applied to each set of an optimal cover, whose sets together contain every element: $\mathrm{ALG} = \sum_e \mathrm{price}_e \le H_d \cdot \mathrm{OPT}$. $\square$

The price lemma is an LP duality argument in disguise. Scaled down by $H_d$, the prices satisfy every dual constraint $\sum_{e \in S} y_e \le c_S$ of the set cover LP. So $\mathrm{ALG}/H_d = \sum_e y_e \le \mathrm{LP} \le \mathrm{OPT}$. This is dual fitting, and the guarantee is really against the LP, which is stronger. Unit 24 turns the idea into the primal–dual method.

The tight family uses weights. For unit costs greedy's worst case is still $\Theta(\log n)$, but the classic instance is more intricate (two disjoint "rows" and exponentially growing "columns") and only reaches about $\tfrac12 \log_2 n$.

23 · packings

Vertex cover by matching; k-center by farthest-first

vertex cover

Take a maximal matching $M$ (scan edges, keep those with both ends free). Cover: all $2|M|$ endpoints (matching-based vertex cover).

  • Feasible: an uncovered edge could have joined $M$.
  • $\mathrm{OPT} \ge |M|$: matching edges share no vertex, so each needs its own.

Tight on $K_{n,n}$: $2n$ against $n$. UGC-hard to beat (unit 22).

Measured, G(n, p) with n = 50 to 200: mean 1.53–1.59, worst 1.74.

k-center (Gonzalez 1985)

$k$-center by farthest-first traversal: start anywhere; repeatedly add the point farthest from the chosen centres. Radius $r$ = the farthest distance left.

  • The $k$ centres and the final farthest point are $k + 1$ points pairwise $\ge r$ apart: a packing witness.
  • Two share an optimal centre, so $r \le 2\,\mathrm{OPT}$ by the triangle inequality.

NP-hard to beat $2 - \varepsilon$ (Hsu & Nemhauser 1979).

Measured, 80 points: mean 1.64 (k = 3), 1.42 (k = 6), 1.28 (k = 10); worst 1.91.

Both sit closer to their bound on random instances than anything else in the unit. Unit 24 gets the same factor 2 for weighted vertex cover, where the matching argument no longer works, by rounding the LP.

The k-center ratio falls as k grows on these uniform random points (1.64, 1.42, 1.28). The lab doesn't test why. One experiment worth running: fix the instance, vary the first point, and see how much of the spread at k = 3 is the arbitrary starting choice.

Metric TSP

Trees, matchings, Euler, and a shortcut.

23 · metric TSP

The double tree: 2

The double-tree algorithm for metric TSP.

Solid: the MST. Dashed: the one shortcut, replacing 5 → 4 → 2 → 1 → 0 → 3.

  1. $\mathrm{MST} \le \mathrm{OPT}$ (minimum spanning tree): delete one edge of the optimal tour and a spanning path remains.
  2. Walk around the tree, using every edge twice: length $2\,\mathrm{MST}$.
  3. Skip vertices already visited (shortcutting). Each skip replaces a path by one edge, no longer by the triangle inequality.

So $\mathrm{tour} \le 2\,\mathrm{MST} \le 2\,\mathrm{OPT}$. The walk's shortcut is exactly a preorder of the tree.

Measured, 30 instances with $n \le 60$: mean 1.30–1.37, worst 1.49.

without the triangle inequality

Step 3 fails, and unit 22's Hamiltonian-cycle gadget shows no ratio at all is possible.

The measured MST/OPT ratio averages 0.82–0.87 on these instances. A stronger lower bound is the Held–Karp 1-tree bound of unit 11, which is typically within 1% of OPT on Euclidean instances. It doesn't give a better proof for the double tree, but it's what you'd report as a gap.

The dashed shortcut in the figure is long (it crosses the whole picture). Shortcutting can cost a lot on a particular instance while staying within the bound: the whole walk is $2\,\mathrm{MST}$, and the shortcut replaced four walk edges.

23 · metric TSP

Christofides: 3/2

Christofides' algorithm, five steps, each a unit of the course.

  1. MST $T$ (unit 13). $w(T) \le \mathrm{OPT}$.
  2. Odd vertices $O$ of $T$: an even number (handshake lemma).
  3. Min-weight perfect matching $M$ on $O$ (unit 15's blossom, weighted). $w(M) \le \mathrm{OPT}/2$.
  4. Euler circuit of $T + M$: every degree is now even.
  5. Shortcut: triangle inequality.
why the matching costs at most half the tour

Shortcut the optimal tour to visit only $O$: length $\le \mathrm{OPT}$. Its edges alternate between two perfect matchings on $O$. The cheaper is $\le \mathrm{OPT}/2$, and $M$ is cheaper still.

45 years at 3/2

explore interactively

The checkpoint asks you to point at the line of code for each step of this proof. In the reference solution christofides is three lines, and each of the five steps above is one call. The proof's structure is the code's structure.

Christofides' 3/2 is tight: there are instances (a "zig-zag" strip of unit triangles, where the MST is a path whose two ends are far apart) where the matching edge is nearly OPT/2 and no shortcut helps. The Held–Karp LP's integrality gap for metric TSP is between 4/3 and 3/2, and closing that interval is open. A follow-up by the same authors (2022) showed the gap is strictly below 3/2 as well.

Serdyukov's paper was in Russian and went unnoticed in the West for decades. Some authors now call it the Christofides–Serdyukov algorithm.

23 · scheduling

List scheduling and LPT (Graham 1966, 1969)

$n$ jobs, $m$ identical machines, minimise the makespan (identical machines). Lower bounds: $\mathrm{OPT} \ge \sum_j p_j / m$ and $\mathrm{OPT} \ge \max_j p_j$.

list scheduling: each job to the least-loaded machine

Let job $j$ finish last. Its machine was least loaded, so it held $\le (\sum p - p_j)/m$:

$$\begin{aligned} C &\le \tfrac{1}{m}\textstyle\sum p + (1 - \tfrac1m)\,p_j \\ &\le (2 - \tfrac1m)\,\mathrm{OPT}\end{aligned}$$
LPT: sort longest first

If the last job $j$ sets the makespan and $j > m$, then $p_j \le p_{m+1}$ and two of the first $m+1$ jobs share a machine in OPT: $p_j \le \mathrm{OPT}/2$, giving $3/2$. A finer counting argument gives $4/3 - 1/(3m)$.

Better: a PTAS (Hochbaum & Shmoys 1987) and, for fixed $m$, an FPTAS (Sahni 1976). Strongly NP-hard when $m$ is part of the input, so no FPTAS then.

The next slide runs both tight families for $m = 3$: list scheduling's and LPT's.

Why LPT is so good on random data. The analysis charges the worst case to the smallest job placed last. With sorted jobs, that's small, and with many jobs per machine the loads even out to within about one small job. On uniform times with n/m ≥ 4, the loss is a percent or two.

The fixed-m FPTAS is unit 16's DP over load vectors, rounded like the knapsack FPTAS of unit 22. The PTAS for general m guesses the placement of the large jobs (a constant number of distinct rounded sizes) and fills in the small ones greedily. It runs in time $n^{O(1/\varepsilon^2)}$ and is purely theoretical.

23 · beyond

Local search as an approximation algorithm: k-median

$k$-median: open $k$ facilities, minimising the sum of distances from clients to their nearest open facility (k-center minimises the maximum).

Arya et al. (2004)

Local search that swaps up to $p$ facilities at a time, stopping at a local optimum, is a $(3 + 2/p)$-approximation: local search with a guarantee.

The proof pairs each optimal facility with a swap and adds up the local optimality inequalities of all the swaps: the same "compare against OPT piece by piece" as the price lemma.

To stop in polynomial time, accept a swap only if it improves by a factor $1 - \varepsilon/\mathrm{poly}(n)$. That costs a $(1 + \varepsilon)$ factor in the ratio.

Unit 26 uses local search for speed; this is local search with a proof.

This slide isn't in the lab. It's here because it connects the two halves of Part V: local search is usually a heuristic judged by measurement (unit 26), but for some problems its local optima come with a worst-case guarantee.

Other classic results in the same spirit: local search for max-cut (every local optimum under single flips cuts at least half the edges), and for maximum-weight independent sets in $k$-claw-free graphs.
