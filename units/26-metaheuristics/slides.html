26

Metaheuristics, benchmarked honestly

Local search engineered properly, the handful of metaheuristics worth knowing, and what a comparison between randomised algorithms has to hold fixed before it means anything.

needs 24, 19~1 h slides + 3 h lablab: uv run co lab 26

Units 22–25 were about guarantees. This unit gives them up. A metaheuristic promises nothing, so the only evidence for one is measurement, and measurement of randomised algorithms is easy to get wrong. The curriculum's framing is deliberate: the literature has a replication problem, and knowing what an honest comparison requires is worth more than knowing a tenth metaphor.

section 1

Local search, engineered

The same neighbourhood, a hundred ways to search it.

26 · local search

2-opt, neighbour lists, don't-look bits

2-opt (Croes 1958): remove edges $ab$ and $cd$, add $ac$ and $bd$, reversing the stretch between.

why scanning near neighbours suffices

An improving move has $d_{ac} + d_{bd} < d_{ab} + d_{cd}$, so $d_{ac} < d_{ab}$ or $d_{bd} < d_{cd}$. Either way, one endpoint sees the move while scanning its sorted neighbour list, and can stop at the first neighbour farther than its tour edge.

Don't-look bits (Bentley 1992): a city whose surroundings haven't changed since it last failed isn't examined again. Keep a queue, and re-queue the four endpoints of every move.

The stopping rule does most of the work: even full lists are cheap once scanning stops early. Building the lists (0.4 s) cost more than searching with them. A good start matters: from a random tour 2-opt ends 5% worse.

The result "k = 10 sweep is slightly worse than full lists" (246 410 vs 244 044) is one instance and one run. Short lists can miss moves whose good endpoint is the eleventh neighbour. On uniform instances the effect is small; on clustered instances it can be large, which is why LKH uses α-nearness (from 1-trees, unit 11) instead of plain distance to choose candidates.

Or-opt is the slow row because the lab version rebuilds the reduced tour for every candidate segment, which is O(n) each. Production implementations use a doubly linked list or an array with position lookups, and for very large instances a splay tree or two-level list so reversals cost O(√n).

26 · local search

From 2-opt to Lin–Kernighan and LKH

  • Or-opt (Or 1976): move a segment of 1–3 cities, possibly reversed. Cheap, and finds moves 2-opt can't.
  • 3-opt: remove three edges and reconnect. $O(n^3)$ naively; neighbour lists make it practical.
  • Lin–Kernighan (1973): a variable-depth sequence of exchanges, extended while the cumulative gain stays positive. The move size adapts to the instance.
  • LKH (Helsgaun 2000, 2009): 5-opt basic moves, candidates by α-nearness from 1-trees, and many refinements. The reference heuristic for TSP, holding best-known tours on many large benchmark instances.
what "state of the art" means here

For symmetric TSP, Concorde proves optimality on instances of thousands of cities, and LKH finds tours within a small fraction of a percent on much larger ones. A new TSP heuristic that doesn't compare against LKH, under the same time budget, hasn't been compared.

LKH isn't installed in this course environment. then section 2 has a hook for the elkai wrapper, untested here because it isn't installed.

The α-nearness idea connects to unit 11: α(i, j) is how much the minimum 1-tree's length increases if edge ij is forced in. Edges of the optimal tour tend to have small α, much more reliably than they have small length. Helsgaun reports that five α-nearest candidates capture the optimal tour's edges far better than five nearest neighbours.

Why LK's variable depth matters: the best k for k-opt varies across the tour and over the run. Fixing k = 3 everywhere wastes effort where 2 would do, and misses improvements that need 4.

annealing's theorem

With $T_k \ge c / \log k$ and $c$ large enough, annealing converges to a global optimum in probability (Hajek 1988). That schedule is far too slow to use. Practical schedules carry no guarantee.

the double bridge

A 4-opt move $A\,B\,C\,D \to A\,C\,B\,D$ that sequential 2-opt and 3-opt can't undo in one step. That's why it's a good kick: the descent doesn't just fall back.

Nearly every metaheuristic is local search plus a rule for escaping. Sörensen's point (reading list) is that most "novel" metaheuristics since 2000 are one of these five with a new metaphor, and the metaphor obscures which of the five it is.

In the lab, annealing's start temperature was tuned on a separate training instance over four values, in units of the mean edge length. Tuning in the right units matters more than the exact value, because a temperature of 20 means something different on a 100 × 100 square and on a 10 000 × 10 000 one.

ALNS is unit 19's LNS with a learning rule on top. Its adaptive weights rarely matter much when there are two operators, as in the lab. They earn their keep with a portfolio of many operators of uneven quality.

26 · metaheuristics

Genetic algorithms: usually the wrong answer, with one notable exception

why usually wrong
  • Crossover ignores structure. Recombining two tours or schedules position by position mostly produces infeasible or poor children, and repairing them is where the work goes.
  • Population = many restarts, with a lot of machinery. Against ILS at equal budget, a plain GA rarely keeps up.
  • Parameters multiply: population size, crossover and mutation rates, selection pressure. They're easy to tune on the test set by accident.
the exception: memetic algorithms done well

Hybrid genetic search for vehicle routing (Vidal et al. 2012; HGS-CVRP, Vidal 2022) is among the best CVRP heuristics. It works because every child is improved by a strong local search, crossover (OX on giant tours) preserves route structure, and the population is managed for diversity as well as quality.

Read the exception carefully: the local search does most of the optimisation. The population chooses where to start it.

This is the "frank account" the curriculum promises. It isn't that population-based search is useless. The plain GA of textbooks, with bit-string encodings and generic crossover, is a poor choice for structured combinatorial problems, and its popularity comes from being easy to describe, not from winning comparisons.

HGS is also the counterexample to "never combine methods". Its components are the lessons of this unit: a strong, engineered local search; diversity management instead of a metaphor; and published, open-source code evaluated on standard instances.

best of N runs reportedreport the distribution over seeds: median, quartiles, TTT plot
a weak or badly implemented baselinecompare against the state of the art (LKH, HGS, OR-Tools), engineered equally
a few hand-picked instancesstandard benchmark sets, or a generator with stated parameters

time-to-target plot (Aiex, Resende & Ribeiro 2007)

Fix a target value. Run each algorithm from many seeds and record the time to reach it (or a failure at the cap). Plot the empirical CDF, with failures kept in the denominator.

Mann–Whitney U

Rank-based, so no normality assumption, and tolerant of capped runs (count failures as the cap). It tests whether one algorithm's times tend to be smaller. Not rejecting is not "equal", only "these data can't tell".

Budget equality is subtler than it looks. A C++ heuristic and a Python heuristic under the same wall clock aren't being compared as algorithms. The lab's section 2 has OR-Tools calling a Python distance callback, which costs it a lot. Say what was held fixed, and what wasn't.

Many comparisons, many tests: with six pairwise tests at α = 0.05 the chance of at least one false positive is about 26% if nothing differs. Unit 28 adds corrections (Holm) and effect sizes, and the question of which comparisons were planned in advance.

A schematic sketch of out/ttt.png, not to scale; the plot file has the data. The two ILS curves cross.

The two ILS curves are the checkpoint in picture form. k = 6 is faster in the middle of the distribution (lower median), and k = 10 is more reliable at the tail (28 against 25 successes). Mann–Whitney weighs both and can't separate them at 30 seeds. Declaring k = 6 "faster" from its median would be the classic mistake.

Annealing's failure is itself a result worth reporting honestly: pure-Python annealing with uniformly random 2-opt moves (no neighbour lists) wastes most proposals on long edges, and 4 seconds of Python buys few of them. That's an implementation-and-budget finding, not a verdict on annealing as an idea, and the report should say so.

Before believing "ALNS beats OR-Tools": 5 instances, no lower bound, one machine, and OR-Tools pays for a Python callback on every arc. The claim that survives: at this size and budget, a simple ALNS is competitive.

Both tables are "measured against the best found", not against optimality. For the TSP the gaps are small enough that the best found is probably close to optimal, and unit 11's Held–Karp bound would say how close. For the CVRP there is no bound here at all. Unit 10's column generation gives one for small n, but not in 10 seconds at n = 60.

What would make the CVRP claim publishable: the standard X instances (Uchoa et al. 2017) with best-known solutions, ten seeds per instance, the same language or a compiled baseline, and HGS as the state-of-the-art reference. Unit 28 is the unit for that.

Several tests replay a scripted random stream, so the order of random draws is part of the specification: positions first, then the uniform, every iteration. That's what makes a randomised algorithm testable exactly, and it's the same discipline that makes experiments reproducible.
