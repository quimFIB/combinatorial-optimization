01

Polyhedra, cones, and Farkas' lemma

The shape of a feasible region, three names for its corners, and a short proof that a linear system has no solution.

needs 00~1½ h slides + 2½ h lablab: uv run co lab 01

Why the course starts here

Integer programming, Part II, is the study of what happens when you take the LP relaxation seriously: the LP you get by dropping integrality. To take it seriously you need to know what its feasible region looks like (a polyhedron), where optima live (at corners), and what kind of object certifies a statement about it. This unit covers those three things. It is geometry and one lemma, with no algorithm for optimizing yet: that is unit 02.

Everything is in $\mathbb{R}^n$ with rational data. Rationality matters exactly once, when we ask how large a certificate is.

Notation

$A \in \mathbb{Q}^{m\times n}$ with rows $a_1,\dots,a_m$, $b \in \mathbb{Q}^m$. Vectors are columns. $a_i x$ means the inner product of row $a_i$ with $x$. Inequalities between vectors hold componentwise.

01 · overview

The route

The destination is Farkas' lemma. Unit 03 builds LP duality on it, and through duality it reaches most of the course.

The surprise: the constructive proof of Farkas is a bookkeeping exercise on Fourier–Motzkin elimination. You will do it in lab step 5.

A plan for reading: the section on corners is where the definitions pay off. Read the three-way equivalence proof once properly, because unit 02's simplex method is the BFS characterization turned into an algorithm. Minkowski–Weyl can be taken on trust the first time through. Fourier–Motzkin and Farkas should be read with a pencil, and the lab will make them concrete.

FTLP is the fundamental theorem of linear programming: an LP that is feasible and bounded attains its optimum at a vertex, provided a vertex exists.

section 1

Polyhedra

Finitely many linear inequalities, and the shapes they cut out.

01 · polyhedra

Halfspaces, polyhedra, polytopes

definitions

A halfspace is $\{x \in \mathbb{R}^n : a x \le \beta\}$ with $a \ne 0$; its boundary $\{a x = \beta\}$ is a hyperplane.

A polyhedron is an intersection of finitely many halfspaces: $$P = \{x : Ax \le b\}.$$

A polytope is a bounded polyhedron.

Every polyhedron is convex and closed. Equalities, $\ge$, and sign constraints all fit this form.

one form to another

$a x = \beta \;\to\; a x \le \beta,\ -a x \le -\beta$

$a x \ge \beta \;\to\; -a x \le -\beta$

free $x_j \;\to\; x_j^+ - x_j^-,\ x_j^\pm \ge 0$

$a x \le \beta \;\to\; a x + s = \beta,\ s \ge 0$

Standard form $\{x : Ax = b,\ x \ge 0\}$ is what simplex works in (unit 02). This unit uses $Ax \le b$.

Convexity: if $Ax \le b$ and $Ay \le b$ then $A(\lambda x + (1-\lambda)y) \le \lambda b + (1-\lambda) b = b$. Closedness: each halfspace is the preimage of a closed set under a continuous map.

The conversions change the dimension and the number of constraints, but not the problem. They are not free for algorithms, though. Splitting a free variable into $x^+ - x^-$ doubles a column, and every value of $x_j$ is then represented in infinitely many ways, which is why solvers handle free variables directly. Adding slacks is what turns the inequality view of this unit into the equality view simplex uses.

Beware one convention difference between textbooks: Bertsimas & Tsitsiklis write $Ax \ge b$ as the general form, and many others use $Ax \le b$. Nothing changes except signs, but duality (unit 03) is a place where sign conventions cost an afternoon.

01 · polyhedra

Faces

definition

An inequality $c x \le \delta$ is valid for $P$ if every $x \in P$ satisfies it. A face of $P$ is $F = P \cap \{x : c x = \delta\}$ for a valid inequality.

  • Faces of dimension $0$: vertices. Dimension $1$: edges.
  • Dimension $\dim P - 1$: facets.
  • Every face is itself a polyhedron: set some of the inequalities of $Ax \le b$ to equality.

A full-dimensional polyhedron has a unique minimal description: one inequality per facet. Every other inequality is redundant.

$x \ge 0,\ y \ge 0,\ x+y \le 4,\ x-y \le 1$. Four facets (edges), four vertices.

The empty set and $P$ itself are faces, called improper: take $0x \le 1$ (face $\emptyset$) or $0x \le 0$ (face $P$). Some texts exclude them.

The face lattice of a polyhedron (all faces ordered by inclusion) is finite, since each face is obtained by turning a subset of the defining inequalities into equalities, and there are at most $2^m$ subsets. That bound is the first hint of why corners can be enumerated: lab step 3 does it by trying subsets of size $n$.

The claim about minimal descriptions: if $P$ is full-dimensional, every inequality in $Ax \le b$ either defines a facet or can be deleted without changing $P$, and the facet-defining inequalities are unique up to positive scaling. For lower-dimensional $P$ there is freedom to add multiples of the equations. This is Schrijver §8.4, and it is why "how many facets does this polytope have" is the honest measure of how hard it is to describe. Unit 05 comes back to it: the TSP polytope has exponentially many facets, and no small extended formulation.

The figure is used again for Fourier–Motzkin later in the unit.

section 2

Three names for a corner

A geometric one, a convexity one, and an algebraic one, and why they agree.

01 · corners

Vertex, extreme point, basic feasible solution

Let $P = \{x : Ax \le b\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and $x \in P$. Write $I(x) = \{i : a_i x = b_i\}$ for the rows tight at $x$.

vertex · geometry

Some $c$ makes $x$ the unique maximizer of $c y$ over $P$.

extreme point · convexity

$x$ is not $\lambda y + (1-\lambda) z$ for $y \ne z$ in $P$ and $0 < \lambda < 1$.

BFS · algebra

The tight rows $\{a_i : i \in I(x)\}$ have rank $n$.

theorem

For $x \in P$ the three are equivalent.

The geometric definition is what optimization needs. The algebraic one is what a computer can check, and simplex walks on it.

Terminology. "Basic feasible solution" is usually defined in standard form: $x \ge 0$, $Ax = b$, and the columns of $A$ for the positive entries of $x$ are linearly independent. The rank condition here is the same idea in inequality form. Converting one form into the other maps BFSs to BFSs. A basic solution (not necessarily feasible) is a point where $n$ linearly independent rows are tight, feasible or not. Lab step 3 enumerates basic solutions and throws away the infeasible ones.

Degeneracy. $|I(x)|$ can be larger than $n$: more constraints than necessary pass through the corner, like the apex of a square pyramid, where four facets meet in $\mathbb{R}^3$. The vertex is still one point, but it corresponds to several different choices of $n$ tight rows (several bases). Degeneracy is harmless geometrically and is the reason simplex can cycle (unit 02). The lab's pyramid test exists to catch the double counting it causes.

01 · corners

Proof, round the cycle

vertex ⇒ extreme

If $x = \lambda y + (1-\lambda) z$ with $y \ne z$, one of them differs from $x$, so $c y < c x$ or $c z < c x$, and neither can exceed $c x$. Then $c x = \lambda c y + (1-\lambda) c z < c x$. Contradiction.

extreme ⇒ BFS

If the tight rows have rank $< n$, pick $d \ne 0$ with $a_i d = 0$ for all $i \in I(x)$. Rows not tight have slack, so $x \pm \varepsilon d \in P$ for small $\varepsilon > 0$, and $x$ is their midpoint. Contradiction.

BFS ⇒ vertex

Take $c = \sum_{i \in I(x)} a_i$. For $y \in P$: $c y = \sum_{I} a_i y \le \sum_I b_i = c x$, with equality only if every $i \in I(x)$ is tight at $y$. Rank $n$ forces $y = x$. $\square$

At $(2.5, 1.5)$ the tight rows are $x+y\le 4$ and $x-y\le 1$. Their sum $c=(2,0)$ is maximized over $P$ at this point and nowhere else.

The step to reread is BFS ⇒ vertex, because it is constructive. Adding up the tight constraints gives an objective that certifies the point is a vertex. Adding constraints with nonnegative weights to bound an objective is the move behind LP duality, and it appears here first.

In extreme ⇒ BFS, the $\varepsilon$ is concrete: for each row $j \notin I(x)$ with $a_j d \ne 0$, you need $\varepsilon |a_j d| \le b_j - a_j x$, and the minimum of those finitely many positive bounds works.

Corollary

A BFS is determined by its tight set: it is the unique solution of $a_i y = b_i,\ i \in I(x)$. So there are at most $\binom{m}{n}$ vertices, and they can be found by trying every $n$-subset of rows. That is lab step 3, and it is lab 00's brute force again, over bases instead of 0/1 vectors.

The real maximum is much smaller than $\binom{m}{n}$ but still exponential. By McMullen's upper bound theorem, a polytope in $\mathbb{R}^n$ with $m$ facets has $O(m^{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor})$ vertices, attained by duals of cyclic polytopes. The $n$-cube has $2n$ facets and $2^n$ vertices.

01 · corners

Not every polyhedron has a corner

$\{(x, y) : y \ge 0,\ y \le 1\}$ is a strip. It contains whole lines, so no point is extreme.

theorem

For nonempty $P = \{x : Ax \le b\}$, the following are equivalent:

  • $P$ has a vertex (it is pointed);
  • $P$ contains no line;
  • $\operatorname{rank} A = n$.
why you rarely notice

Any polyhedron inside $x \ge 0$ is pointed: the rows $x_j \ge 0$ already have rank $n$. Standard form is pointed by construction.

So "the optimum is at a vertex" needs a hypothesis. The fundamental theorem at the end of the unit states it.

Sketch. The lineality space is $\{d : Ad = 0\}$. If it contains $d \ne 0$, then $x + t d \in P$ for all real $t$: a line. Conversely, a line $x + t d$ in $P$ needs $A d \le 0$ and $-A d \le 0$, so $A d = 0$. And $\{d : Ad = 0\} = \{0\}$ exactly when $\operatorname{rank} A = n$.

For the vertex direction: if $\operatorname{rank} A = n$, start at any $x \in P$. While the tight rows have rank $< n$, pick $d$ in their null space but not in the null space of $A$, and move along $\pm d$ until a new row becomes tight. Each move increases the rank of the tight set, so after at most $n$ moves you are at a BFS. The same walk is how crossover turns an interior-point solution into a basic one (unit 04).

01 · corners

Two descriptions: Minkowski–Weyl

theorem (Minkowski 1896, Weyl 1935)

$P$ is a polyhedron if and only if $\;P = \operatorname{conv}(V) + \operatorname{cone}(R)\;$ for finite sets $V, R$.

In particular, a polytope is the convex hull of its vertices, and the convex hull of finitely many points is a polytope.

H-descriptionV-description
$n$-cube$2n$ facets$2^n$ vertices
$n$-cross-polytope$2^n$ facets$2n$ vertices

Either description can be exponentially larger than the other.

definitions

$\operatorname{conv}(V) = \{\sum \lambda_v v : \lambda \ge 0,\ \sum \lambda_v = 1\}$

$\operatorname{cone}(R) = \{\sum \mu_r r : \mu \ge 0\}$

For pointed $P$: $V$ = vertices, $R$ = extreme rays of the recession cone $\{d : Ad \le 0\}$.

This is the theorem that lets integer programming exist as a subject. The set of integer points in a polytope is finite, so its convex hull is a polytope by the "if" direction, and therefore has some H-description $\{x : Cx \le d\}$. Optimizing a linear function over the integer points is the same as optimizing it over that polytope, which is an LP. The catch is that $C$ is unknown and typically enormous. Cutting planes (unit 08) try to find the parts of $C$ that matter.

Proof idea, not needed for the lab. H ⇒ V: vertices and extreme rays are finite in number (bases), and every point decomposes. V ⇒ H: write $\operatorname{conv}(V)+\operatorname{cone}(R)$ as the projection of a polyhedron in the variables $(x, \lambda, \mu)$, then eliminate $\lambda$ and $\mu$ with Fourier–Motzkin, the next section's tool. Projections of polyhedra are polyhedra, and that gives the H-description.

Computing one description from the other is what qhull and cddlib do. For polytopes, whether vertex enumeration can be done in time polynomial in input plus output size is still open. For unbounded polyhedra it is NP-hard to decide whether a given list of vertices is complete (Khachiyan, Boros, Borys, Elbassioni & Gurvich, 2008). Your lab compares a $\binom{m}{n}$ enumeration with qhull.

section 3

Fourier–Motzkin elimination

Gaussian elimination for inequalities. It is correct and complete, and its output explodes.

01 · fourier–motzkin

Eliminating one variable

Goal: describe the projection $\;\{x' : \exists x_k,\ (x', x_k) \in P\}\;$ by inequalities in $x'$ only.

Sort the rows by the sign of $a_{ik}$:

$Z$: $a_{ik} = 0$ — don't mention $x_k$; keep them.

$U$: $a_{pk} > 0$ — upper bounds: $\;x_k \le \dfrac{b_p - a_p' x'}{a_{pk}}$

$L$: $a_{qk} < 0$ — lower bounds: $\;x_k \ge \dfrac{b_q - a_q' x'}{a_{qk}}$

theorem (Fourier 1826, Motzkin 1936)

$x'$ extends to a point of $P$ if and only if it satisfies the rows in $Z$ and, for every $p \in U,\ q \in L$, $$(-a_{qk})\,(a_p x) + a_{pk}\,(a_q x) \le (-a_{qk})\, b_p + a_{pk}\, b_q,$$ a row in which $x_k$ cancels.

Both multipliers are positive. Inequalities survive nonnegative combinations and nothing else, and that is the whole difference from Gaussian elimination.

Proof. Fix $x'$. The feasible values of $x_k$ form the interval $\max_{q\in L} \ell_q(x') \le x_k \le \min_{p \in U} u_p(x')$, intersected with the requirement that the $Z$ rows hold. That interval is nonempty exactly when every lower bound is at most every upper bound: $\ell_q(x') \le u_p(x')$ for all pairs. Clear the denominators. Multiplying by $a_{pk} > 0$ and by $-a_{qk} > 0$ preserves the direction, and you get the displayed combined row. If $U$ or $L$ is empty there are no pairs, the interval is unbounded on one side and nonempty, and only $Z$ survives. The lab's hint ladder calls this out because it is the case people special-case wrongly.

Integer data stays integer. The multipliers are the coefficients themselves, so no division ever happens. That is why the lab can do everything in exact integers and divide by a gcd only to tidy up.

Count. The new system has $|Z| + |U|\cdot|L|$ rows. With $m$ rows split evenly that is about $m^2/4$.

Fourier described the method in 1826. Motzkin rediscovered it in his 1936 thesis, and Dines had it in 1919; the name "Fourier–Motzkin" stuck.

01 · fourier–motzkin

On the example: eliminate $x$

rowclass
$-x \le 0$$L$
$-y \le 0$$Z$
$x + y \le 4$$U$
$x - y \le 1$$U$

Pairs, with multipliers $1$ and $1$:

$(x+y \le 4) + (-x \le 0) \;\Rightarrow\; y \le 4$

$(x-y \le 1) + (-x \le 0) \;\Rightarrow\; -y \le 1$ — redundant

Keep $Z$: $-y \le 0$.   Projection: $\;0 \le y \le 4$. ✓

Projecting along $x$: every $y \in [0, 4]$ has some $x$ that puts $(x,y)$ in $P$.

Check the redundant row: $-y \le 1$ says $y \ge -1$, which $-y \le 0$ already implies. FM has no idea it is redundant. Detecting redundancy exactly means solving an LP per row (is $\max\,(-y)$ over the other rows at most $1$?), so FM carries every such row forward and pairs it again at the next step. That is where the explosion comes from.

Two refinements keep most of the junk out. Chernikov's rule: track which original rows each derived row came from, and discard a derived row whose history is a superset of another's. After $k$ eliminations, a row built from more than $k+1$ original rows is redundant. Imbert's acceleration theorems sharpen this. With them the growth is singly exponential, which is the best possible, since projections can have exponentially many facets.

The lab doesn't implement these. It deduplicates normalized rows, and then shows you how far that is from enough.

$Ax \le b$ is feasible $\iff$ every remaining $c \ge 0$.

Iterating $m \mapsto m^2/4$ gives roughly $4\,(m/4)^{2^k}$ after $k$ steps: doubly exponential in the number of eliminated variables. Even with perfect redundancy removal the output can be exponential, so the problem is not only bad bookkeeping.

Look at the table's third column. The projections themselves stay small (under 40 facets) while FM's description of them grows past seventy thousand rows. The gap between "the object" and "a description FM can produce" is enormous.

FM is still useful. It proves theorems (projection of a polyhedron is a polyhedron; Minkowski–Weyl; Farkas), and it is used in practice where variables are few: in compilers, for loop-bound analysis in the polyhedral model, and in decision procedures for linear arithmetic inside SMT solvers (unit 21), where it is one of the options alongside simplex.

section 4

Farkas' lemma

A system with no solution always has a short, checkable reason why.

01 · farkas

A certificate of infeasibility

definition

$y \in \mathbb{R}^m$ is a Farkas certificate for $Ax \le b$ if $$y \ge 0, \qquad y^\top A = 0, \qquad y^\top b < 0.$$

it proves infeasibility

If $Ax \le b$ and $y \ge 0$, then $\;0 = (y^\top A)\,x = y^\top (Ax) \le y^\top b < 0$. $\square$

In words: a nonnegative combination of the rows whose left side vanishes and whose right side is negative, which gives $0 \le -1$.

Example: $x \le 1$ and $-x \le -2$. Take $y = (1, 1)$ and add: $0 \le -1$.

Checking it needs one matrix–vector product. No solver and no trust: lab step 4.

All three conditions are necessary. $y=0$ gives $0 \le 0$, and a negative $y_i$ flips an inequality.

This is the easy direction, and it is only one line. The content of Farkas' lemma is the converse: whenever the system is infeasible, such a $y$ exists. There is never an infeasible linear system whose infeasibility cannot be shown by adding up its rows.

Contrast with unit 00. For vertex cover, a short lower-bound certificate sometimes did not exist (the triangle). For linear systems over the reals, the certificate always exists. That difference, integers versus reals, is roughly the difference between NP-hard and polynomial.

In the language of unit 00: linear feasibility has short "yes" certificates (a solution $x$) and short "no" certificates (a Farkas $y$). So it is in NP ∩ coNP. The size argument is on the next slides.

01 · farkas

Farkas' lemma

theorem (Farkas, 1902) · inequality form

Exactly one of these holds:

$\quad$(I) $\;\exists\, x:\ Ax \le b$ $\qquad\qquad$ (II) $\;\exists\, y \ge 0:\ y^\top A = 0,\ y^\top b < 0$

equivalent · standard form

Exactly one of these holds:

$\quad$(I′) $\;\exists\, x \ge 0:\ Ax = b$ $\qquad\qquad$ (II′) $\;\exists\, y:\ A^\top y \ge 0,\ b^\top y < 0$

"Not both" is the one-line proof on the previous slide. "At least one" is the theorem, and there are two proofs worth knowing: constructive, by FM, and geometric, by separation.

"Not both" for the standard form: if $x \ge 0$, $Ax = b$ and $A^\top y \ge 0$, then $b^\top y = x^\top A^\top y \ge 0$, contradicting $b^\top y < 0$.

There are many variants (Gordan, Stiemke, Motzkin's transposition theorem), all "theorems of the alternative", and all can be derived from either form above by rewriting the system. Deriving one yourself is good practice. For example, derive the form for $\{x \ge 0 : Ax \le b\}$: its alternative is $y \ge 0,\ y^\top A \ge 0,\ y^\top b < 0$.

Gyula Farkas published the lemma in 1902 in Crelle's journal, after earlier versions with gaps, in work on mechanical equilibrium. It sat unnoticed until von Neumann and Gale, Kuhn and Tucker built LP duality on it in the 1940s.

01 · farkas

Farkas, constructively

proof of "at least one"

Invariant. Every row FM produces is a nonnegative combination $y^\top(A, b)$ of the original rows. This holds by induction, since each new row is a positive combination of two rows that already satisfy it.

Run FM to the end. Each final row reads $0 \le c$ and carries its $y \ge 0$ with $y^\top A = 0$ and $y^\top b = c$.

If some $c < 0$: that $y$ is a certificate, so (II) holds.
If every $c \ge 0$: FM's correctness says the system is feasible, so (I) holds. $\square$

in code · lab step 5

Append the unit vector $e_i$ to row $i$ as $m$ extra columns that are never eliminated. FM combines rows linearly, so the extra columns accumulate exactly $y$.

When a row with zero $x$-part and $b < 0$ appears, its tail is the certificate.

Read the proof again with the example from two slides back: $x \le 1$, $-x \le -2$. Eliminating $x$ pairs row 1 (upper bound) with row 2 (lower bound) with multipliers $1, 1$, giving $0 \le -1$. With the tails attached the row is $(0 \mid 1, 1) \le -1$, and $y = (1, 1)$ can be read off.

The lab asks you to see the proof and the algorithm as the same thing. That habit is useful throughout the course. Many existence theorems in optimization come with an algorithm that finds the object, and many algorithms, run to completion, prove a theorem.

How large is the certificate?

FM's multipliers can have many bits. But if a certificate exists, then $\{y \ge 0 : y^\top A = 0,\ y^\top b = -1\}$ is a nonempty polyhedron. It is pointed, because it lies in $y \ge 0$, so it has a vertex. A vertex solves a square subsystem, so by Cramer's rule its entries have bit size polynomial in that of $(A, b)$. So short certificates always exist, and linear feasibility is in coNP. Together with the solution itself as a "yes" certificate, which also has polynomial size by the same vertex argument, this placed LP in NP ∩ coNP decades before Khachiyan put it in P (unit 04).

01 · farkas

Farkas, geometrically

$b \notin \operatorname{cone}(a_1, a_2)$, so a line through the origin separates them. Its normal $y$ has $y \cdot a_j \ge 0$ and $y \cdot b < 0$.

Read the standard form geometrically. With $a_j$ the columns of $A$:

(I′) says $b \in \operatorname{cone}(a_1, \dots, a_n)$.

(II′) says some hyperplane $\{z : y^\top z = 0\}$ has every $a_j$ on one side and $b$ strictly on the other.

so farkas is

a point outside a finitely generated cone can be separated from it by a hyperplane.

Here $a_1=(2,1)$, $a_2=(1,3)$, $b=(-1,2)$, $y=(3,-1)$: $y\cdot a_1 = 5$, $y \cdot a_2 = 0$, $y\cdot b = -5$.

The geometric proof: a finitely generated cone $K$ is closed and convex. If $b \notin K$, let $p$ be the point of $K$ nearest to $b$. The vector $y = p - b$ then satisfies $y^\top z \ge y^\top p$ for all $z \in K$, with $y^\top p = 0$ because $K$ is a cone, and $y^\top b < y^\top p$. That is (II′).

The subtle step is closedness. It is obvious for a polyhedral cone, but a finitely generated cone is only known to be polyhedral after Minkowski–Weyl, or after Carathéodory's theorem, which writes it as a finite union of cones on linearly independent generators, each of which is closed. For a general convex cone that is not closed the statement fails, which is why duality in conic programming (unit 25, SDP) needs extra "constraint qualification" hypotheses that LP never does.

Keep both pictures. FM is the one you compute with. Separation is the one that generalizes to convex optimization (unit 29): KKT conditions are what this picture becomes when the cone is generated by gradients of the active constraints.

01 · farkas

Why this is the root of the trunk

LP duality (unit 03). "No $x$ has $c^\top x > z$" is an infeasibility statement about $Ax \le b,\ -c^\top x \le -z$ with a small perturbation. Its Farkas certificate is a dual solution. Strong duality is Farkas applied once.

Solvers return certificates. Gurobi: set InfUnbdInfo=1 and read FarkasDual. HiGHS: getDualRay. An infeasible model comes with the $y$ you are building.

Cutting planes (unit 08). A Chvátal–Gomory cut is a nonnegative combination of rows whose coefficients happen to be integers, with the right side rounded down. It is Farkas plus rounding.

The KKT conditions (unit 29) are the separation picture for curved sets.

Solvers compute in floating point, so their $y$ satisfies $y^\top A \approx 0$. Your integer $y$ satisfies it exactly. Exact verification of solver claims is a live research area.

The duality sketch, to be done properly in unit 03. Suppose $\max\{c^\top x : Ax \le b\}$ is feasible with optimum $z^*$. For any $\varepsilon > 0$ the system $Ax \le b,\ -c^\top x \le -(z^* + \varepsilon)$ is infeasible. Farkas gives $(y, t) \ge 0$ with $y^\top A - t\,c^\top = 0$ and $y^\top b - t\,(z^* + \varepsilon) < 0$. Feasibility of $Ax \le b$ forces $t > 0$ (otherwise $y$ alone would be a certificate for $Ax \le b$). Scale to $t = 1$: $y^\top A = c^\top$, $y \ge 0$, and $y^\top b < z^* + \varepsilon$. That $y$ is a dual feasible solution with value within $\varepsilon$ of $z^*$, and a finiteness argument removes the $\varepsilon$.

On exactness: the SCIP team's exact SCIP and the VIPR certificate format let a MIP solver emit a proof of optimality that an independent checker verifies in rational arithmetic. For LP, the certificate is exactly the kind of object this unit builds.

01 · closing the loop

The fundamental theorem of LP

theorem

Let $P = \{x : Ax \le b\}$ be nonempty and pointed, and $c \in \mathbb{R}^n$. Then either $\max\{c^\top x : x \in P\}$ is unbounded, or it is attained at a vertex of $P$.

proof, via minkowski–weyl

Write $P = \operatorname{conv}(V) + \operatorname{cone}(R)$ with $V$ the vertices. If $c^\top r > 0$ for some $r \in R$, then $x + t r$ is feasible for every $t \ge 0$ and the objective is unbounded. Otherwise, for $x = \sum \lambda_v v + \sum \mu_r r$, $$c^\top x = \textstyle\sum \lambda_v\, c^\top v + \sum \mu_r\, c^\top r \;\le\; \max_{v \in V} c^\top v. \qquad \square$$

So an LP is infeasible, unbounded, or solved at a corner. There is no fourth case, and in particular no finite supremum that fails to be attained.

This is the theorem that makes LP a combinatorial problem in disguise. The optimum lies in a finite set, the vertices, which lab step 3 can enumerate in $\binom{m}{n}$ tries. Simplex (unit 02) is the idea of not enumerating: move from a vertex to an adjacent, better one until none is better. That works because the objective is linear and $P$ is convex, so a local optimum over the vertex graph is global.

"No fourth case" is not true for nonlinear problems. $\inf\{e^{-x} : x \ge 0\} = 0$ is finite and never attained. Finitely many linear constraints rule this out.

Pointedness is needed only for the words "at a vertex". Without it, a bounded LP still attains its optimum, on a face that contains a line.

The lab's rows are (a, b) tuples of Python ints, meaning $a \cdot x \le b$. Integers are deliberate. Floats would need a tolerance, and a proof checked up to a tolerance is not a proof. colib.polyhedra provides solve_exact so you do not write Gaussian elimination again.
