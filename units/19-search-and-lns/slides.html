19

Search, restarts, and large neighbourhood search

Which variable next, which value first, when to give up and start again — and how destroy-and-repair turns an exact engine into a heuristic that scales.

needs 18~1¾ h slides + 2½ h lablab: uv run co lab 19

Units 17 and 18 were about inference, what propagation can deduce. This unit is about decisions: where the tree branches, and when a search should abandon its tree entirely. The unit's claim is empirical, and the lab measures it. Backtracking search effort on a single instance, across random seeds, often has a heavy tail. Restarts are justified by that tail, and only where it exists.

The second half, large neighbourhood search, is the bridge to Part V. It uses the exact engine as a subroutine inside a heuristic loop, and it's how CP solvers find good solutions to problems they cannot prove optimal.

How to read this deck

Three small worked examples sit next to the lab's measurements: dom/wdeg's weights changing over three failures on four variables, the first fifteen Luby cutoffs, and one destroy-and-repair step on a three-job shop drawn as a Gantt chart. All three were computed with the lab's reference solution. The Luby optimality theorem is stated as an idea, with the precise statement in the notes.

section 1

Heuristics

Fail first; succeed first.

19 · heuristics

Variable and value ordering

Variables: fail first (Haralick & Elliott 1980). Branch where a dead end is most likely, so it is found near the root.

  • first-fail: smallest domain.
  • dom/deg: domain size over degree.
  • dom/wdeg (Boussemart et al. 2004): each constraint's weight counts its failures, so search is drawn to the hard core of the problem.
  • impact-based (Refalo 2004): prefer variables whose assignments shrank the search space most in the past.
  • activity-based (Michel & Van Hentenryck 2012): a VSIDS-like count of how often each variable's domain changes.

Values: succeed first. Try the value most likely to lead to a solution. It matters only for satisfiable subtrees; for proofs, every value is tried anyway.

lab step 2: a hidden core

14 easy three-valued variables in a chain, plus 5 variables that must all differ over 4 values. first-fail branches on the chain (smaller domains): more than 20 000 nodes, unfinished. dom/wdeg learns from the first failures that the ≠ clique is where they come from: under 500 nodes to prove infeasibility.

On QCP, where all constraints look alike, dom/wdeg is worse than randomised first-fail (lab then). Heuristics are instance-family bets.

dom/wdeg is the CP cousin of SAT's VSIDS (unit 20): both learn from conflicts which part of the problem is hard. The difference is granularity. VSIDS bumps the variables in each learned clause, and dom/wdeg bumps the constraint that failed.

Symmetry breaking during search (SBDS, Gent & Smith 2000; SBDD) adds, after each failed branch, constraints excluding the symmetric images of that subtree. The static alternative adds lexicographic ordering constraints to the model. Both prevent the search from rediscovering the same failure in a symmetric disguise.

19 · heuristics · example

dom/wdeg, on four variables

Four variables on a cycle of $\ne$ constraints: $c_1: x \ne y$, $c_2: y \ne z$, $c_3: z \ne w$, $c_4: x \ne w$. Domains: $x, y \in \{0,1,2\}$, $z, w \in \{0,1\}$. Every weight starts at 1.

Score $= |D| / \text{wdeg}$, where wdeg adds the weights of the variable's constraints. Branch on the smallest score (ties: lowest index).

what changes, and what doesn't

At the start plain first-fail and dom/wdeg agree: $z$, the smallest domain. After failures in $c_1$, the two variables of $c_1$ get cheaper to pick, and by the second failure $x$ wins outright. The variables far from the failures, $z$ and $w$, never move.

Three failures turn a variable with a larger domain into the preferred branch: search moves to where the conflicts are.

The arithmetic. $x$ sits in $c_1$ and $c_4$, so its wdeg is $w(c_1) + w(c_4)$: $1 + 1 = 2$ at the start, then 3, 4, 5 as $c_1$'s weight grows. Its domain has 3 values, so its score goes $1.5 \to 1 \to 0.75 \to 0.6$. $y$ is in $c_1$ and $c_2$ and follows the same numbers. $z$ is in $c_2$ and $c_3$, $w$ in $c_3$ and $c_4$: neither touches $c_1$, so both stay at $2/2 = 1$.

Computed with the reference DomWDeg, calling its on_failure hook with $c_1$ three times and reading its choose after each. In a real search the domains shrink too, and a constraint only counts toward wdeg while another of its variables is unfixed.

Gomes, Selman, Crato & Kautz 2000

Backtracking search on many CSP and SAT families has runtimes with Pareto-like tails, a heavy tail: $P(T > t) \sim C t^{-\alpha}$, often with $\alpha < 1$, which means infinite mean in the limit.

Cause: an early mistake puts search in a subtree with no solution that it must exhaust. Its size is exponential in how early the mistake happened.

A heavy tail makes the mean a poor summary and a single run a lottery ticket. It also points to the fix: don't wait.

How to see the tail properly: plot $\log P(T > t)$ against $\log t$. A straight line with slope $-\alpha$ is a power-law tail. An exponential tail curves down sharply. With 100 samples you can see the shape, but not estimate $\alpha$ well. Gomes et al. used thousands of runs.

Another order-12 instance, at 58% holes (the lab's LIGHT), has p90/median 1.3: runtimes cluster tightly, and no single decision is catastrophic. Heavy tails depend on the instance and the heuristic together, and are not a property of the problem class.

section 3

Restarts and nogoods

Cut the tail off.

19 · restarts

Luby's universal schedule

$$1, 1, 2, 1, 1, 2, 4, 1, 1, 2, 1, 1, 2, 4, 8, \dots$$
why this sequence (Luby, Sinclair & Zuckerman 1993)

You don't know the right cutoff. The Luby sequence tries every power of two, and spends about the same total effort on each, so whichever cutoff was right, it is used often enough. Nothing that doesn't know the distribution does much better.

precise statement in the notes (N)

Geometric schedules ($c \cdot r^i$) are the common alternative. SAT solvers now also restart adaptively, when recent conflicts look worse than average (Audemard & Simon 2012).

explore interactively

The theorem, precisely

For a Las Vegas algorithm (always correct, random running time) with an unknown runtime distribution, running it with cutoffs $c \cdot \text{luby}(i)$, restarting after each, has expected total running time within an $O(\log)$ factor of the best fixed-cutoff strategy, which knows the distribution. No universal strategy can do better by more than a constant factor.

Why restarts need randomisation, or learning: an identical deterministic restart repeats the same search. The lab's restarts vary through random tie-breaking or through dom/wdeg's weights, which change with every failure. SAT solvers restart deterministically but keep their VSIDS activities and learned clauses, so the new search differs.

The maximum dropping from over 3 000 to 202 is the practical win: restarts turn a heavy-tailed distribution into one with an exponentially decaying tail, so worst cases become predictable.

How it grows: the first $2^k - 1$ terms are two copies of the first $2^{k-1} - 1$ terms, then $2^{k-1}$. Runs 1–7 repeat as runs 8–14, then 8 appears.

reading it against the histogram

On the heavy instance, half the runs finish within 31 nodes. A cutoff of 30 or 60 catches those, and twelve of the first fifteen runs have one of those cutoffs.

The rare long runs never get to run long: the biggest cutoff in 960 nodes of work is 240, where plain search once spent over 3 000.

Each cutoff size gets about equal total effort: runs of 30 use 240 nodes, runs of 60 use 240, runs of 120 use 240, and the single 240.

19 · restarts

What to remember across restarts

  • Heuristic state: dom/wdeg weights, activity, impacts. The next search branches differently because it learned.
  • Nogoods: a set of decisions proved to have no solution below it. Record it as a constraint and the next search never enters that subtree again.
  • Nogoods from restarts (Lecoutre et al. 2007): at each restart, extract the nogoods implied by the current partial tree's refuted branches.
  • The incumbent, for optimisation: the objective bound carries over.
nogoods, done right

A decision nogood such as "$x_1 = 3 \wedge x_4 = 1$ fails" is weak: it mentions decisions, not reasons. The strong form records why it failed, the propagators and removed values responsible. That's clause learning, the core of CDCL SAT solvers (unit 20), and lazy clause generation brings it to CP (unit 21).

Lab then's 3-colouring in unit 17 was that difference: 120 334 nodes without learning, 3 106 branches with.

The honest limitation of this unit's engine is that it forgets everything except weights. Units 20 and 21 fix that. The interesting measurement once you have both is how much of CP-SAT's advantage comes from learning and how much from propagation strength. Unit 21's lab measures exactly that on job-shop.

Large neighbourhood search

The exact engine, as a heuristic's inner loop.

19 · LNS

Destroy and repair

  1. Start from a solution (lab: a greedy dispatch schedule).
  2. Destroy: free a part of it. Lab: a random 30% of the jobs; the other jobs keep their order on every machine.
  3. Repair: search the freed part with the full propagation engine and objective < best, under a small node limit.
  4. Accept an improvement and repeat.

Large neighbourhood search: Shaw (1998) introduced it for vehicle routing with CP repair. ALNS (Ropke & Pisinger 2006) chooses among several destroy operators adaptively.

Lab then, random job-shops, 22 500 nodes each, makespan:

instancegreedyLNSbranch-and-bound
8 × 4544957
10 × 5726677
12 × 61177884

Branch-and-bound spends its budget at the bottom of one subtree. LNS spends it around a good schedule, where the improvements are.

Why the propagator makes a good repair engine: with most of each machine's order fixed, propagation pins down most start times immediately, and the search space is the freed jobs' insertions. The neighbourhood is exponentially large, but it's explored by propagation-guided search rather than enumerated. That's the difference from classical local search (unit 26), whose neighbourhoods are small enough to scan.

The choice of what to destroy matters as much as the repair. Related destroy, jobs sharing machines around the critical path, usually beat random ones. Neighbourhood design is where domain knowledge enters LNS.

19 · LNS · example

One LNS step, on a 3 × 3 job-shop

joboperations (machine, duration)
J0M1 5, then M0 2, then M2 4
J1M2 3, then M0 2, then M1 2
J2M0 1, then M2 4, then M1 5
destroy, then repair

Destroy: free job J1. On every machine, J0 and J2 keep their order: M0 J2 before J0, M1 J0 before J2, M2 J2 before J0.

Repair: search where J1 goes, with makespan $\le 15$. Propagation places it in the gaps: M0 at 3–5, M1 at 5–7. J2's last operation moves up to 7–12.

Makespan 16 → 12, which brute force confirms is optimal.

Outline colour is the job; J1, red, is the freed job. In the greedy schedule J1 waits for M0 until time 7, which pushes J2's M1 operation to 11–16.

explore interactively

The greedy schedule (reference greedy_schedule, round-robin over jobs): first operations J0 on M1 at 0, J1 on M2 at 0, J2 on M0 at 0; second round J0 on M0 at 5, J1 on M0 at 7 (M0 busy until 7), J2 on M2 at 3; third round J0 on M2 at 7, J1 on M1 at 9, J2 on M1 at 11 (after J1). Makespan 16.

The step is the reference lns_jobshop with seed 0, one iteration, relax 0.34: it frees exactly one job, J1, keeps the other jobs' machine orders as precedences, and asks for makespan at most 15. The repaired schedule: J1 on M2 0–3, M0 3–5, M1 5–7; J2's last operation 7–12. Kept orders check: M0 has J2 (0–1) before J0 (5–7), M1 has J0 (0–5) before J2 (7–12), M2 has J2 (3–7) before J0 (7–11). The optimum over all $3!^3 = 216$ machine orders, by brute force, is 12.

The greedy schedule's weakness is typical: dispatch commits J1's M0 operation to after J0's without looking ahead, and one freed job was enough to undo it.
