Where discrete meets differentiable

Every decision in this course is an argmax. Four ways to get a useful gradient through one, and an honest test of whether learning helps inside an exact solver.

needs 30, 07, 15~1 h slides + 3 h lablab: uv run co lab 31

The last unit. It joins the course's two halves in both directions: continuous tools applied to discrete decisions (relaxations, estimators, argmin layers), and learning applied inside a discrete algorithm (a branching rule for unit 07's branch and bound).

The curriculum's framing of the state of the art is worth keeping in view: learned methods are still behind LKH and CP-SAT on classical problems, and genuinely promising as guidance inside exact solvers rather than as replacements for them. The lab's own result is a modest version of that finding.

section 1

Relax the decision

A permutation becomes a doubly stochastic matrix.

31 · relax

Sinkhorn: the Hungarian algorithm, softened

$$\begin{aligned}\min_{P \in \mathcal{B}}\ & \langle C, P \rangle + \varepsilon \textstyle\sum_{ij} P_{ij}(\log P_{ij} - 1)\end{aligned}$$

$\mathcal{B}$: doubly stochastic matrices (the Birkhoff polytope, whose vertices are the permutations; unit 06). The entropy makes the minimiser unique, interior and smooth in $C$:

$$P_{ij} = \exp\big((f_i + g_j - C_{ij}) / \varepsilon\big)$$

Sinkhorn alternates: fix $g$ and choose $f$ so rows sum to 1, then fix $f$ and choose $g$ so columns do. In log domain it's two logsumexp operations per iteration.

the gradient (envelope theorem)

$\nabla_C \big[\min_P \langle C, P\rangle - \varepsilon H(P)\big] = P_\varepsilon^*$

The hard assignment cost has "gradient" equal to the optimal permutation matrix, which jumps. Sinkhorn replaces the jump by a smooth plan. Lab step 2: jax.grad through the iterations equals $P$ to 1e−6.

related relaxations

Gumbel-Sinkhorn (Mena et al. 2018): noise plus Sinkhorn, for learning latent permutations. Differentiable sorting and ranks via optimal transport (Cuturi, Teboul & Vert 2019) or projections onto the permutahedron (Blondel et al. 2020).

ε is a temperature. As ε → 0 the plan concentrates on an optimal permutation (if unique), but the number of Sinkhorn iterations needed grows roughly like 1/ε, and the kernel underflows unless you work in log space. The lab uses log-domain updates throughout.

The same construction is the core of computational optimal transport (Cuturi 2013), where it replaced network-flow solvers for large, approximate transport problems. Assignment is the special case with uniform marginals.

A random assignment costs 0.5 per row.

  • 180× slower than your Hungarian at n = 200, and 2.5× the cost after rounding.
  • Colder didn't help at n = 200, probably because the plan is still spread over many entries and greedy rounding picks badly among them.
  • The cost differences between good assignments shrink like 1/n, so ε has to shrink with n, and iterations grow like 1/ε.
what it's for

A smooth, batched, GPU-friendly plan with a gradient, as a layer inside a model that must output a matching. There, "approximately a permutation, differentiably" is the requirement, and the Hungarian algorithm can't meet it.

This is the "accuracy, speed, and the gradient the Hungarian cannot give you" comparison the curriculum asks for. The first two go to the Hungarian algorithm by orders of magnitude on a CPU. The third is the only reason to use Sinkhorn, and it's a good reason in the right setting.

section 2

Sample the decision

Gradients of expectations over discrete choices.

31 · sample

Gumbel, straight-through, REINFORCE

Gumbel-max and Gumbel-softmax

$k = \arg\max_i (\ell_i + G_i)$ with $G_i$ Gumbel noise samples exactly from $\mathrm{softmax}(\ell)$. Replacing argmax by $\mathrm{softmax}((\ell + G)/\tau)$ gives a differentiable sample (Jang, Gupta & Poole 2017; Maddison, Mnih & Teh 2017).

straight-through (Bengio, Léonard & Courville 2013)

Forward the hard one-hot, backward the soft sample's gradient: hard + soft − stop_gradient(soft). Biased: the gradient belongs to a different function than the one evaluated.

REINFORCE (Williams 1992) $$\nabla_\ell\, \mathbb{E}[f(k)] = \mathbb{E}\big[f(k)\, (e_k - p)\big]$$

Unbiased, needs no derivative of $f$, and high variance.

Norms of vectors. REINFORCE's bias is within its noise. Straight-through trades variance for bias as τ grows: at τ = 1, 4.5× less spread and a bias 15 noise levels wide.

The practical rules that follow. REINFORCE needs a baseline (subtract a running mean of f) to be usable, which cuts variance without adding bias. Straight-through and Gumbel-softmax work well when f is smooth in the relaxed output and a little bias is acceptable, as in discrete latent variables. Both are the tools neural combinatorial optimisation uses: attention models for routing are trained with REINFORCE and a baseline (Kool et al. 2019).

section 3

Differentiate the optimum

The KKT conditions of unit 29, differentiated.

31 · argmin

The implicit function theorem on KKT

$x^*(q) = \arg\min_x \tfrac12 x^\top Q x + q^\top x$ s.t. $Ax \le b$. With the active set $S$ fixed (strict complementarity), the KKT conditions are linear:

$$\begin{bmatrix} Q & A_S^\top \\ A_S & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ \lambda_S \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -q \\ b_S \end{bmatrix}$$

Differentiate in $q$: the same matrix times $[\partial x/\partial q;\ \partial \lambda/\partial q]$ equals $[-I;\ 0]$. One linear solve, no matter how the solver found $x^*$.

OptNet (Amos & Kolter 2017) for QPs; cvxpylayers (Agrawal et al. 2019) for disciplined parametrized convex programs.

where it breaks

At a degenerate point (a constraint active with zero multiplier) the active set can change under an infinitesimal change of $q$, and $x^*(q)$ isn't differentiable there. For LPs, $x^*$ is piecewise constant: the Jacobian is zero almost everywhere, the same problem as argmax.

The LP remark is why differentiable optimisation for combinatorial problems adds smoothing: perturbed optimisers (Berthet et al. 2020) average the argmax over random perturbations of the costs, and regularised LPs add a quadratic or entropic term. Sinkhorn in section 1 is exactly the entropic version for assignment.

Unrolling (differentiating through the solver's iterations) and implicit differentiation agree here because 500 projected-gradient steps converge. With fewer iterations, unrolling gives the gradient of the truncated algorithm, which can be a different and sometimes more useful object.

section 4

Learn to optimise

Guidance inside exact solvers, and the honest state of the field.

31 · learning

Learned branching, cuts, and neural combinatorial optimisation

inside exact solvers
  • Learning to branch: imitate strong branching's choices cheaply (Khalil et al. 2016; Alvarez, Louveaux & Wehenkel 2017). Graph neural networks on the variable–constraint graph (Gasse et al. 2019) beat SCIP's default reliability branching on some benchmark families.
  • Cut selection (Tang, Agrawal & Faenza 2020; Paulus et al. 2022), node selection, primal heuristics, and solver configuration.

The solver keeps its guarantees: a bad learned choice costs time, never correctness.

instead of solvers
  • Pointer networks (Vinyals, Fortunato & Jaitly 2015), attention models trained with REINFORCE (Kool, van Hoof & Welling 2019), POMO (Kwon et al. 2020) for routing.
the honest current state

On classical problems at benchmark sizes, neural constructive methods remain behind LKH for TSP and behind CP-SAT, HGS and MIP solvers on their home ground, especially in generalisation to larger or differently distributed instances. As learned guidance inside exact or classical solvers, the results are genuinely promising.

Bengio, Lodi & Prouvost, EJOR 290 (2021), is the methodological survey.

The distinction that matters for evaluation. A learned heuristic that replaces a solver must be compared with the best classical heuristic under the same time budget (unit 26's lesson), and it often loses. A learned policy inside a solver is compared with the solver's default policy on node counts and time, with the solver's correctness untouched. That's a much easier bar, and it's the one the lab uses.

Generalisation is the recurring weakness: models trained on 100-city instances often degrade badly on 1 000-city ones, while LKH doesn't care. Unit 28's instance-selection warnings apply with full force.

verdict: it does not beat most-fractional, and here's why
  • No evidence of a difference: paired interval [0.94, 1.03] over 20 held-out instances.
  • It learned fractionality: weight 2.27 against at most 0.62 on the other features, so it's nearly most-fractional.
  • The features can't carry the signal: held-out imitation accuracy 0.58, against 0.55 for most-fractional's own agreement with strong branching. Strong branching decides from child LP bounds, which none of the five features contains.

Next experiment, same protocol: add node-LP features (reduced costs, dual values, a one-pivot bound estimate), as Gasse et al. did.

This is the checkpoint answered in the second form it allows, and it's a more useful answer than a lucky win would be. The imitation-accuracy baseline is the key diagnostic: if a trivial rule already agrees with the expert 55% of the time and the learned one 58%, the model class or features are the bottleneck, and no amount of training data will fix it.

End of the units. The capstone takes one problem (CVRP with time windows, or job-shop) through four methods: a MIP with your own cuts, branch-and-price, CP-SAT with a deliberate search strategy, and ALNS. Then it referees them with unit 28's harness under a shared time budget.
