Independence systems, and one algorithm for all of them

independence system

A ground set $E$ and a family $\mathcal I$ of "independent" subsets, containing $\emptyset$ and closed under taking subsets.

greedy (max weight)

Sort $E$ by decreasing weight. Take each element with positive weight if the chosen set stays independent.

13 · greedy

The matroid axioms

matroid
  1. $\emptyset \in \mathcal I$
  2. $B \in \mathcal I,\ A \subseteq B \Rightarrow A \in \mathcal I$ (hereditary)
  3. $A, B \in \mathcal I,\ |A| < |B| \Rightarrow \exists\, b \in B \setminus A$ with $A + b \in \mathcal I$ (exchange)

Consequence of exchange: all maximal independent sets (bases) of any subset have the same size, its rank $r(S)$.

matroidindependent sets
graphicforests of a graph
uniform $U_{k,n}$sets of size $\le k$
partitionat most $c_b$ elements from each block $b$
linearlinearly independent sets of vectors
transversalsets matchable into a bipartite graph's other side

Linear algebra is where the axioms come from: Whitney (1935) abstracted exactly the exchange property of bases of a vector space.

Graphic matroids: two forests $A$, $B$ with $|A| < |B|$. $A$ has more connected components than $B$ (a forest on $n$ vertices with $k$ edges has $n - k$ components), so some edge of $B$ joins two different components of $A$, and adding it to $A$ creates no cycle. That is the exchange axiom, proved.

Scheduling unit-time jobs with deadlines is a transversal matroid: a set of jobs is independent if they can all be scheduled on time. That's why "take the most profitable job that still fits" is optimal there. It's the first half of the lab's checkpoint exercise.

13 · greedy

Rado–Edmonds: greedy works exactly on matroids

theorem (Rado 1957; Edmonds 1971)

For an independence system $(E, \mathcal I)$: greedy finds a maximum-weight independent set for every weight function if and only if $(E, \mathcal I)$ is a matroid.

matroid ⇒ greedy optimal, the idea

If some better set had a heavier $i$-th element than greedy's, exchange would hand greedy an element it could have taken earlier. Greedy never skips anything it could use.

full proof in the notes (N)

not a matroid ⇒ weights that fool greedy

Find $A, B$ where exchange fails, give $A$ slightly more weight than $B$. For matchings on the path $a\!-\!b\!-\!c\!-\!d$: $A = \{bc\}$, $B = \{ab, cd\}$, neither edge of $B$ fits next to $bc$. Weights $2, 3, 2$ are exactly such weights: greedy 3, optimum 4.

Lab step 3 finds the $(A, B)$, an exchange witness. Step 2's greedy on the graphic oracle is Kruskal, matched against networkx on 25 graphs.

Full proof

Matroid ⇒ greedy optimal. Let greedy pick $g_1, g_2, \dots$ in order, and let $O$ be optimal, sorted by weight. If $w(o_i) > w(g_i)$ at the first index where they differ, exchange between $\{g_1..g_{i-1}\}$ and $\{o_1..o_i\}$ gives an element of weight $\ge w(o_i) > w(g_i)$ that greedy could have taken before $g_i$: a contradiction.

Not a matroid ⇒ some weights fool greedy. Take $A, B$ violating exchange with $|A| < |B|$. Give $A$ weight $1 + \varepsilon$, $B \setminus A$ weight 1, everything else 0. Greedy takes all of $A$ first, then gets stuck at $|A|(1+\varepsilon)$, while $B$ is worth at least $|B|$.

The second direction is constructive and practical: it tells you how to build the failing instance for any proposed greedy algorithm whose feasible sets aren't a matroid. That is the curriculum's checkpoint.

For the weights: with $|A| < |B|$ and $\varepsilon$ small enough that $|A|(1+\varepsilon) < |B|$, greedy's total is below $|B|$. After taking $A$, no element of $B \setminus A$ can be added (by the choice of $A$, $B$), and the rest have weight 0.

Beyond cardinality: the minimum-weight base problem (spanning trees) is the same theorem with weights negated and "stop at non-positive" removed.

13 · greedy

The matroid polytope

Edmonds 1970

The convex hull of incidence vectors of independent sets, the matroid polytope, is

$$\begin{aligned} P(M) = \{x \ge 0 :\ & \textstyle\sum_{e \in S} x_e \le r(S) \\ & \text{for all } S \subseteq E\}\end{aligned}$$

Greedy's output is an optimal vertex, and a dual solution built from the same order proves it by LP duality (unit 03): the greedy dual certificate.

Why unit 06 and unit 09 meet here:

  • The system is TDI: integrality comes from the dual, not from total unimodularity.
  • There are exponentially many constraints, but separation over $P(M)$ is submodular function minimization (min over $S$ of $r(S) - x(S)$), which is polynomial.
  • So by GLS, optimizing over two matroids' polytopes together is polynomial too: that is intersection, next.

The dual certificate: order the elements $e_1, \dots, e_n$ by decreasing weight and let $S_i = \{e_1, \dots, e_i\}$. Set $y_{S_i} = w(e_i) - w(e_{i+1})$ (nonnegative because of the order), and 0 on all other sets. Then $\sum_{S \ni e} y_S = w(e)$, and $\sum_S r(S) y_S$ equals the weight of greedy's set. That is LP duality proving greedy optimal, with the rank function doing the work matchings did in unit 00.

Edmonds' matroid intersection theorem says the intersection of two matroid polytopes is also integral. With three it isn't, which is the polyhedral face of three-matroid intersection being NP-hard.

13 · intersection

Matroid intersection

Schematic. Arcs out of I-vertices are M1-exchanges; arcs into them are M2-exchanges. A shortest source→sink path is flipped, and |I| grows by one.

Matroid intersection: the largest set independent in both $M_1$ and $M_2$.

Edmonds' min–max theorem $$\max |I| = \min_{A \subseteq E}\; r_1(A) + r_2(E \setminus A)$$
  • Bipartite matching: two partition matroids, "one edge per left vertex" and "one edge per right vertex". König's theorem is this min–max.
  • Rainbow spanning forests: graphic ∩ "one edge per colour".
  • Arborescences in digraphs, and more.

Algorithm (lab step 4): the exchange graph, then a shortest augmenting path, then flip. The next slide does one. Polynomial in the number of oracle calls.

The exchange graph arcs: $x \to y$ ($x \in I$, $y \notin I$) when $I - x + y$ is independent in $M_1$; $y \to x$ when $I - x + y$ is independent in $M_2$. Sources are $y$ with $I + y \in \mathcal I_1$, sinks are $y$ with $I + y \in \mathcal I_2$. A source→sink path alternates out, in, …, out, and flipping it adds one element. Shortest paths are required. The correctness proof shows the flipped set stays independent in each matroid using a "unique perfect matching" lemma, and that lemma only applies to shortest paths. Hint rung 3 says why.

When no path exists, the set $A$ of vertices reachable from the sources gives the min–max certificate: $|I| = r_1(A) + r_2(E \setminus A)$. It is the same shape as max-flow min-cut (unit 14): the unreachable set is the cut.

Reading each arc. $e_0 \to e_1$: removing $e_1$ and adding $e_0$ gives $\{e_0\}$, which uses $r_0$ once: fine in $M_2$. $e_1 \to e_2$: removing $e_1$ and adding $e_2$ gives $\{e_2\}$, which uses $\ell_1$ once: fine in $M_1$. 13 · intersection

Two is polynomial, three is NP-hard

three matroids

A directed Hamiltonian path is a set of $n - 1$ arcs that is independent in three matroids at once:

  • graphic (no cycle, ignoring directions);
  • partition: at most one arc into each vertex;
  • partition: at most one arc out of each vertex.

So a size-$(n-1)$ common independent set exists iff a Hamiltonian path does. That is NP-complete: three-matroid intersection is NP-hard.

This is where the islands end. The sequence runs:

  • one matroid: greedy;
  • two: augmenting paths, the polytope still integral;
  • three: NP-hard, and the TSP of unit 09 lives here;
  • matroid parity (matching's generalization): polynomial for linear matroids (Lovász), intractable in the oracle model.

Unit 22 turns reductions like this one into code.

Check the reduction in one direction: a Hamiltonian path has in-degree and out-degree at most 1 everywhere, and no cycle, so it is independent in all three, with $n-1$ arcs. Conversely, $n - 1$ arcs with in- and out-degree at most 1 form vertex-disjoint directed paths and cycles. No cycle, and $n - 1$ arcs on $n$ vertices, leaves exactly one path covering everything.

The ATSP connection: a tour is a Hamiltonian cycle, so ATSP is essentially min-weight three-matroid intersection. Held–Karp's 1-tree bound (unit 11) works by dropping one of the three constraints (the degree partition) into the objective with Lagrange multipliers, which leaves a one-matroid problem that greedy solves.

13 · submodular

Diminishing returns, and 1 − 1/e

submodular

$f : 2^E \to \mathbb R$ with $\;f(A + e) - f(A) \ge f(B + e) - f(B)\;$ for $A \subseteq B$, $e \notin B$: the marginal gain shrinks.

Adding $e$ helps a small set at least as much as a big one. Matroid rank functions and coverage functions are submodular.

Nemhauser, Wolsey & Fisher (1978)

For monotone submodular $f$ and a cardinality limit $k$, greedy (add the element of largest marginal gain) achieves $f(G) \ge (1 - \tfrac1e)\,\mathrm{OPT}$.

maximum coverage, k = 2

Sets $\{1,2,3,4\}$, $\{1,2,5\}$, $\{3,4,6\}$, $\{7\}$.

Greedy: takes $\{1,2,3,4\}$ (4 new), then any other set adds only 1: covers 5.

Optimum: $\{1,2,5\} + \{3,4,6\}$ covers 6. Ratio $\tfrac56 = 0.83$, above the guarantee for $k = 2$, $1 - (\tfrac12)^2 = 0.75$.

why greedy is never worse than 1 − 1/e: notes (N)

and it's tight

For max coverage, no polynomial algorithm beats $1 - 1/e$ unless P = NP (Feige 1998).

Proof idea

Each step, the $k$ optimal elements together would add at least $\mathrm{OPT} - f(G_i)$, so the best single one adds at least $\frac1k(\mathrm{OPT} - f(G_i))$. The gap shrinks by a factor $(1 - \frac1k)$ per step, and $(1 - \frac1k)^k \le \frac1e$.

The example. The biggest set looks best first, but it overlaps both halves of the optimal pair. The reference greedy_coverage picks indices 0 then 1 (a tie with 2 and 3, broken by lowest index), covering 5; brute force over the six pairs gives 6.

Tightness of the analysis: there are coverage instances on which greedy achieves exactly $(1 - (1-1/k)^k)\,\mathrm{OPT}$, which tends to $1 - 1/e$. They are built with nested sets of carefully decreasing sizes. Feige's hardness result says no cleverer polynomial algorithm does better in the worst case, so greedy is the best possible algorithm for max coverage in that sense.

Submodular maximization is everywhere in practice: sensor placement, influence maximization in networks, document summarization, active learning. In all of them greedy, or a lazy-evaluation version of it, is the default method because of this theorem.
