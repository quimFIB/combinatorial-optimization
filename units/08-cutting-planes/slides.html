08

Cutting planes and branch-and-cut

Inequalities that every integer solution satisfies and the LP optimum doesn't: how to derive them, how to find them, and why solvers throw most of them away.

needs 07~2 h slides + 3 h lablab: uv run co lab 08

Unit 05 ended with "a valid inequality never changes the MILP and may tighten its relaxation". Unit 07 ended with a list of hypotheses about why SCIP needs so many fewer nodes, with cuts first on the list. This unit derives cuts in general (Chvátal–Gomory), specifically (knapsack covers and lifting), and as an architecture (branch-and-cut). The lab measures how much of the gap each closes.

The history in one line: Gomory proposed pure cutting planes in 1958; they were considered impractical for thirty years; then problem-specific cuts (Crowder, Johnson & Padberg 1983) and branch-and-cut (Padberg & Rinaldi 1991) made them the backbone of every modern MIP solver.

How to read this deck

Two small examples carry the unit: Gomory's two-variable IP, whose two cuts are drawn, and one knapsack constraint, on which a cover is separated and lifted. Every number on them comes from the lab's reference solution in exact arithmetic. The theorems about closures and convergence are stated, with their arguments in the notes.

section 1

General cuts

Rounding a combination of constraints: all of integer programming's valid inequalities, eventually.

08 · general

Valid inequalities and separation

definitions

For $S$ = the integer feasible set: $a^\top x \le \beta$ is valid if every $x \in S$ satisfies it.

A cut for an LP point $x^*$ is a valid inequality with $a^\top x^* > \beta$.

The strongest valid inequalities are the facets of $\mathrm{conv}(S)$.

the separation problem

Given $x^*$ and a family of valid inequalities: find a member violated by $x^*$, or prove there is none.

The cutting-plane method:

  1. Solve the LP: $x^*$.
  2. If $x^*$ is integral, it is optimal.
  3. Separate. If a cut is found, add it and re-solve with the dual simplex (unit 03). Otherwise branch (unit 07).

On a two-variable example, three slides on, two rounds of this loop take the LP from $(1, 1.5)$ to the integer optimum $(1, 1)$.

A family whose separation problem is hard is useless in practice, however strong its members. Separation is the central question for every family.

By Minkowski–Weyl (unit 01) and Meyer's theorem (unit 05), $\mathrm{conv}(S)$ has finitely many facets. If you could separate over all of them, the LP would be exact. Unit 09 makes this precise: separation over a polytope is polynomially equivalent to optimization over it. So for NP-hard problems, exact separation over all facets is NP-hard too. Practical cut families are subfamilies whose separation is tractable, exactly or heuristically.

Re-solving after a cut is exactly unit 03's warm-start situation: one new constraint, a dual feasible basis, and a few dual simplex pivots.

08 · general

Chvátal–Gomory rounding

For $Ax \le b$, $x \ge 0$ integer, and any multipliers $u \ge 0$:

$$\begin{aligned} u^\top A\, x &\le u^\top b \\ \Longrightarrow\quad \lfloor u^\top A \rfloor x &\le u^\top b \quad (x \ge 0) \\ \Longrightarrow\quad \lfloor u^\top A \rfloor x &\le \lfloor u^\top b \rfloor \quad (x \text{ integer}) \end{aligned}$$

Step 1 uses $x \ge 0$ (rounding coefficients down only lowers the left side). Step 2 uses integrality: the left side is an integer.

example

$2x_1 + 2x_2 \le 3$ with $u = \tfrac12$: $x_1 + x_2 \le \tfrac32$, which rounds to $x_1 + x_2 \le 1$. The point $(\tfrac34, \tfrac34)$ is cut off, and no integer point is: $(0,0), (1,0), (0,1)$ all satisfy it.

Chvátal 1973, Schrijver 1980

Adding all CG cuts gives the CG closure. Repeating finitely many times reaches $\mathrm{conv}(S)$ for any bounded pure integer program. So every valid inequality has a finite CG derivation.

The number of rounds needed is the CG rank. It can be large: the matching polytope's odd-set inequalities have rank 1, but some inequalities for TSP have rank that grows with $n$.

This is unit 01's Farkas combination with one rounding step added. Over the reals, nonnegative combinations of constraints derive every implied inequality (Farkas). Over the integers, nonnegative combinations plus rounding derive every valid inequality (Chvátal). It makes CG derivations a proof system for integer infeasibility, and they have been studied as such (cutting-plane proofs; lower bounds on their length relate to unit 20's resolution lower bounds).

The odd-set inequalities of unit 06: for an odd vertex set $S$, sum the degree constraints of the vertices in $S$ with $u = \tfrac12$. The edges inside $S$ get coefficient 1, and the right side is $|S|/2$, which rounds down to $(|S|-1)/2$. So Edmonds' inequalities are rank-1 CG cuts.

08 · general

Gomory's cut, read off the tableau

A tableau row $\;x_i + \sum_{j \in N} \bar a_{ij} z_j = \bar b_i\;$ with $\bar b_i$ fractional. With the fractional part $f(v) = v - \lfloor v \rfloor$, the Gomory fractional cut is

$$\sum_{j \in N} f(\bar a_{ij})\, z_j \;\ge\; f(\bar b_i)$$

It is valid for all integer solutions, and violated by the current vertex, where every nonbasic $z_j = 0$ and so the left side is $0 < f(\bar b_i)$.

It is the CG cut with multipliers taken from row $i$ of $A_B^{-1}$, rearranged.

Worked: $\max x_2$, $3x_1 + 2x_2 \le 6$, $-3x_1 + 2x_2 \le 0$. LP optimum $(1, \tfrac32)$. Final tableau row for $x_2$:

$$x_2 + \tfrac14 s_1 + \tfrac14 s_2 = \tfrac32$$

Cut: $\tfrac14 s_1 + \tfrac14 s_2 \ge \tfrac12$. Substitute $s_1 = 6 - 3x_1 - 2x_2$ and $s_2 = 3x_1 - 2x_2$:

$$\tfrac14(6 - 4x_2) \ge \tfrac12 \iff x_2 \le 1$$

One more round adds $x_2 \le x_1$, and the LP reaches the integer optimum $(1, 1)$. Lab step 1's test is this example; the next slide draws it.

The derivation is in hint rung 1 of lab step 1. The fractional cut needs all variables, slacks included, to be integers, which is why the lab requires integer $A$ and $b$, and why each cut is scaled to integers before being added: its own slack must be integral for the next round's cuts to be valid.

For mixed-integer programs the right version is the Gomory mixed-integer (GMI) cut, which treats continuous variables with a different coefficient formula. GMI cuts are among the most effective general-purpose cuts in modern solvers. They are also equivalent to mixed-integer rounding (MIR) cuts derived from the tableau row.

Cut 1: add the two constraints with $u = (\tfrac14, \tfrac14)$: $\;x_2 \le \tfrac32$, rounded to $x_2 \le 1$.

Cut 2: round 1's tableau row for $x_1$ gives $\tfrac23 s_2 + \tfrac23 s_3 \ge \tfrac23$, i.e. $s_2 + s_3 \ge 1$; with $s_2 = 3x_1 - 2x_2$ and $s_3 = 1 - x_2$ that is $x_2 \le x_1$.

The first cut moved the bound from 1.5 to 1; the second did not move it at all, and only changed which optimal point the LP returns. Rounds that change little are the start of stalling.

explore interactively

The regions. The original LP region has corners $(0,0)$, $(2,0)$, $(1, \tfrac32)$. After $x_2 \le 1$ the top is cut flat between $(\tfrac23, 1)$ (on $-3x_1 + 2x_2 = 0$) and $(\tfrac43, 1)$ (on $3x_1 + 2x_2 = 6$). After $x_2 \le x_1$ the left end moves to $(1,1)$. The integer points are $(0,0)$, $(1,0)$, $(2,0)$ and $(1,1)$, and the final region is exactly their convex hull.

Round 1's tableau row (reference two-phase with the first cut as constraint 3, slack $s_3$): $x_1 - \tfrac13 s_2 + \tfrac23 s_3 = \tfrac23$. Fractional parts: $f(-\tfrac13) = \tfrac23$, $f(\tfrac23) = \tfrac23$, $f(\tfrac23) = \tfrac23$, giving the cut on the slide. The reference gomory_cut returns it scaled to integers as $-2x_1 + 2x_2 \le 0$.

Why round 1 returned $(\tfrac23, 1)$: after the first cut the whole top edge is optimal for $\max x_2$, and the simplex stopped at one end of it. With a different pivoting rule it could have stopped at $(\tfrac43, 1)$, and then the second cut would have come from a different row. Cut sequences depend on the LP solver's choices.

Efficacy, for section 3: cut 1 moves $(1, 1.5)$ by $(1.5 - 1)/1 = 0.5$; cut 2 moves $(\tfrac23, 1)$ by $(1 - \tfrac23)/\sqrt2 = 0.24$.

Convergence in theory, stalling in practice

Lab then, pure Gomory on 20 random 3-variable IPs, up to 80 rounds:

seedLPIPfinalcutscoef digits
340.543535.0082
721.481919.00363
1625.822324.508013

19 of 20 reach the optimum. One stalls, and its coefficients reach 13 digits.

why solvers ration them
  • Each round cuts less deeply: the bound improvements shrink geometrically.
  • Coefficients and cut density grow: the LP gets slower and numerically worse.
  • In floating point, the cuts become invalid, since tableau entries carry rounding error.

In theory, with a careful choice of tableau row the loop always finishes (Gomory 1963). The practical fix for stalling is to stop early and branch.

Gomory's finiteness theorem. With the lexicographic simplex method and the cut always taken from the first fractional row, the pure fractional cutting-plane algorithm terminates after finitely many cuts for any bounded pure integer program. The bound on the number of cuts is astronomically large, which is why the theorem reassures without guiding practice.

The exact arithmetic in the lab hides the third problem. With floats, a tableau entry of 0.9999999 has fractional part 0.9999999, not 0, and the resulting "cut" can remove integer points. Early codes that ran many rounds of Gomory cuts produced wrong answers for exactly this reason. Modern solvers generate GMI cuts from a few rounds of tableau rows, check their numerical safety (coefficient range, dynamism), and discard doubtful ones.

Balas, Ceria, Cornuéjols and Natraj (1996) showed that Gomory cuts, when used carefully (a few rounds, in branch-and-cut), are very effective. That paper is the turning point after which Gomory cuts came back into commercial solvers.

section 2

Structural cuts

Families derived from one constraint's structure, where separation is cheap.

08 · structural

Knapsack cover inequalities

One knapsack constraint $\sum_j w_j x_j \le c$, with $x \in \{0,1\}^n$ and $w \ge 0$.

cover

A set $C$ with $\sum_{j \in C} w_j > c$: not all of $C$ fits, so the cover inequality

$$\sum_{j \in C} x_j \le |C| - 1.$$

It is minimal if removing any item makes it fit.

separation is a knapsack problem

$x^*$ violates the cover inequality for $C$ iff $\sum_{j \in C} (1 - x^*_j) < 1$. So find

$$\min \sum_{j \in C} (1 - x_j^*) \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \sum_{j \in C} w_j > c.$$

NP-hard in general, but pseudo-polynomial by DP over integer weights (unit 16). That is lab step 3.

Solvers usually separate heuristically, sorting by $(1-x_j^*)/w_j$, and exactly only on small constraints.

Crowder, Johnson and Padberg (1983) showed that adding lifted cover inequalities from each knapsack constraint of large 0/1 problems let them solve instances that had been out of reach. It was the first big practical demonstration of problem-specific cuts.

The separation DP is a knapsack in the complement: choose items to exceed the capacity at minimum cost. Because only violated covers matter (cost below 1), solvers can stop the DP early or fix variables with $x_j^* = 0$ out of the cover: they cost 1 and can never be in a violated cover's cheapest version unless forced.

Separating a cover, on one constraint

Constraint: $5x_0 + 5x_1 + 5x_2 + 5x_3 + 8x_4 \le 12$. LP point $x^* = (1,\ 0.7,\ 0.7,\ 0,\ 0)$, weight $5 + 3.5 + 3.5 = 12$: feasible for the LP.

Cost below 1 means violated. The cheapest cover is $\{0,1,2\}$, and indeed $x^*_0 + x^*_1 + x^*_2 = 2.4 > 2$.

why "cost below 1" is the test

The inequality $\sum_C x_j \le |C| - 1$ rewrites as $\sum_C (1 - x_j) \ge 1$. So it is violated exactly when that sum is below 1: each item "pays" how far it is from 1.

Items at $x^*_j = 1$ are free, items at 0 cost a full unit. That is why covers are built from the items the LP has nearly packed.

The reference separate_cover (a DP over weights, lab step 3) returns $(0, 1, 2)$. The next slide strengthens the resulting cut by lifting.

explore interactively

The table lists the minimal covers that matter; there are others, like $\{0,2,3\}$ or $\{2, 4\}$, all with cost at least 1. Every cover of this constraint needs total weight at least 13: any three of the weight-5 items, or item 4 with any one other item.

The DP in the reference is a knapsack in disguise: for each total weight $t$ it keeps the cheapest set of items reaching at least $t$, and reads off the cheapest set reaching 13. Its cost 0.6 is below 1, so it returns the set.

Note the tight cover $\{0, 4\}$: its inequality $x_0 + x_4 \le 1$ holds with equality at $x^*$. It is valid and supporting, but not a cut for this point.

08 · structural

Lifting: strengthening a cut variable by variable

$5x_0 + 5x_1 + 5x_2 + 5x_3 + 8x_4 \le 12$, cover $C = \{0,1,2\}$: $\;x_0 + x_1 + x_2 \le 2$.

Lifting. Lift $x_3$ (weight 5): with $x_3 = 1$, capacity 7 remains, and at most one item of $C$ fits. So $\alpha_3 = 2 - 1 = 1$.

Lift $x_4$ (weight 8): with $x_4 = 1$, capacity 4 remains, and nothing fits. So $\alpha_4 = 2 - 0 = 2$.

$$x_0 + x_1 + x_2 + x_3 + 2x_4 \le 2$$
checked at points

$(0,0,0,0,1)$: weight 8 fits, left side $2 \le 2$, tight. $\;(1,0,0,1,0)$: weight 10, left side 2, tight. The earlier $x^* = (1, 0.7, 0.7, 0, 0)$: left side 2.4, still cut off.

Raise $x_4$'s coefficient to 3 and $(0,0,0,0,1)$ gives $3 > 2$: invalid. So 2 is the most it can take.

sequential up-lifting

For the next $j \notin C$: $\;\alpha_j = r - \max\{\sum_{\text{lifted}} \alpha_i x_i : \sum w_i x_i \le c - w_j\}$.

The largest coefficient that keeps the inequality valid, given the ones already lifted.

facets

For a minimal cover, sequential lifting yields a facet of the knapsack polytope (Balas 1975; Wolsey 1975). A different lifting order can give a different facet.

explore interactively

Lifting is a general technique, not specific to covers: start from a valid inequality for a restriction of the problem (here, the variables outside $C$ fixed to 0), then bring variables back one at a time, each with the best coefficient that preserves validity. Each lifting step is an optimization problem of the same kind as the original, which is why solvers lift approximately (with superadditive lifting functions) when constraints are large.

Lab step 4's test checks maximality exactly: raising any lifted coefficient by one must make the inequality invalid for some feasible point.

Unit 05's weak facility-location models were rescued by implied bound cuts and flow covers, which is why presolve-plus-cuts made them look fine.

Clique cuts come from the conflict graph: an edge between two binaries (or a binary and a complemented binary) that cannot both be 1. A clique $K$ in that graph gives $\sum_{j \in K} x_j \le 1$, which is much stronger than the pairwise conflicts. Set packing and scheduling models are full of them.

Bixby and Rothberg's analyses of CPLEX version histories (around 2004–2007) attributed the largest single share of the performance improvements of that period to cutting planes, with Gomory cuts and MIR among the most important families, and presolve and heuristics next. Unit 27 revisits which features matter on your own models.

section 3

Branch-and-cut

Cuts and branching together, and the engineering that makes that work.

08 · architecture

Cut loops and cut management

At the root (the root cut loop of branch-and-cut): several rounds of solve, separate every family, select, add, and re-solve, until the bound stops moving enough.

Selection scores candidates by:

  • efficacy: the Euclidean distance the cut moves $x^*$, $(a^\top x^* - \beta)/\|a\|$. Gomory's cut 1: 0.5, cut 2: 0.24;
  • parallelism: skip cuts nearly parallel to one already chosen;
  • numerics: skip huge coefficient ranges.
the pool

Cuts that have been slack for several rounds (aging) are removed from the LP but kept in a pool, and re-added if violated again. The lab's age_pool is the core of it.

most cuts are discarded

Solvers generate far more cuts than they keep. Each kept cut is a dense constraint the LP must carry at every node of the tree, so a cut must earn its place.

The trade-off is the same one strong branching had in unit 07: cuts improve the bound, which means fewer nodes, but make every node's LP larger and slower. A cut that closes 1% of the gap but doubles LP time can be a net loss.

Local cuts: cuts derived at a node, valid only in that subtree because they use the node's bound changes. They must be removed when the search leaves the subtree. Global cuts, valid everywhere, go to the pool and can be reused anywhere.

reading it
  • Covers alone close a modest share. SCIP closes 2–9× more by combining families: GMI and MIR act on sums of constraints, which covers of one constraint can't see.
  • Nodes fell by 12–25% here. Cuts matter most on models whose LP is far from the hull, and these knapsacks are already fairly tight.

Why cuts from one constraint are limited here: with two knapsack constraints, the hull of the intersection has facets that are not facets of either constraint alone. Lifted covers of each constraint separately never see them. MIR and GMI cuts, derived from combinations of constraints (an aggregated constraint, or a tableau row), can.
