section 1

Convexity

Local information that is global.

29 · convexity

Convex, smooth, strongly convex

convex function

$f(\theta x + (1-\theta) y) \le \theta f(x) + (1-\theta) f(y)$. For differentiable $f$, equivalently

$$f(y) \ge f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top (y - x)$$

The tangent plane is a global under-estimator, so $\nabla f(x) = 0$ means a global minimum.

subgradient

Any $g$ with $f(y) \ge f(x) + g^\top(y - x)$ for all $y$. Unit 11's Lagrangian dual is concave and non-smooth, and subgradient ascent used exactly this.

propertydefinitionfor $\frac12 x^\top Q x$
$L$-smooth$\|\nabla f(x) - \nabla f(y)\| \le L \|x - y\|$$L = \lambda_{\max}(Q)$
$\mu$-strongly convex$f(y) \ge f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top(y-x) + \frac\mu2 \|y-x\|^2$$\mu = \lambda_{\min}(Q)$
condition number$\kappa = L / \mu$eigenvalue spread

$L$ bounds how far a gradient step can safely go (step $1/L$). $\mu$ bounds how flat the bottom can be. Their ratio $\kappa$ decides how many steps you need.

Lab step 1 also builds the gradient check that every later unit relies on: central differences, error $O(h^2)$.

What continuity changes, compared with Parts I–V. In a combinatorial problem, local optimality (no improving 2-opt move) says nothing about global optimality. For a convex function it says everything. The whole difficulty of unit 26 disappears, and the question becomes purely about speed.

LPs are convex too, which is why the simplex method's local optimality test (no improving reduced cost) certifies a global optimum.

Gradient descent, momentum, and the lower bound

methodupdaterate, $L$-smooth convexrate, strongly convex
GD, step $1/L$$x - \frac1L \nabla f(x)$$\frac{L R^2}{2k}$$(1 - \frac1\kappa)^k$

Nesterov (1983)gradient at $x_k + \frac{k-1}{k+2}(x_k - x_{k-1})$$\frac{2 L R^2}{(k+1)^2}$$(1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt\kappa})^k$ with constant momentum

$R = \|x_0 - x^*\|$. Lab tests check GD's and Nesterov's bounds at every iterate on 12 quadratics.

lower bound (Nemirovski & Yudin 1983; Nesterov)

Any method whose iterates lie in the span of past gradients has, on some $L$-smooth convex function, error $\Omega(L R^2 / k^2)$ for $k \le n/2$. Nesterov's method is optimal up to constants.

heavy ball's catch

Its optimal parameters need $\mu$ and $L$, and outside quadratics it can fail to converge at all (Lessard, Recht & Packard 2016 give a smooth strongly convex example).

The √κ in accelerated rates is the whole story of momentum. With κ = 10 000 and a target of 1e−8 in f, the rates predict about 92 000 iterations for plain GD and about 460 for tuned heavy ball (both printed by then). It measured 542 for heavy ball and over 20 000 for GD.

Stochastic gradients (the ML case) replace ∇f with an unbiased estimate. The rate drops to O(1/√k) for convex f and O(1/k) for strongly convex f with decreasing steps, which is optimal for general stochastic oracles. For finite sums, variance reduction (SAG, SVRG) recovers linear rates. Unit 30's MLP uses mini-batch gradients.

Unit 28's method applies here too. "Heavy ball is fastest" is a claim about tuned parameters on quadratics, and it won't transfer to a problem whose curvature you don't know. The honest summary: with unknown conditioning and cheap gradients, L-BFGS is the default, and momentum methods matter most when gradients are stochastic or memory is scarce, as in deep learning.

Constraints

Project, or ask a linear oracle.

29 · constraints

Projected gradient and Frank–Wolfe

projected gradient

$x_{k+1} = \Pi_C(x_k - \frac1L \nabla f(x_k))$. Same rates as GD, if projecting is cheap. Onto the simplex: sort, prefix sums, a threshold, $O(n \log n)$ (lab step 3).

Frank–Wolfe (1956)

$s_k = \arg\min_{s \in C} \nabla f(x_k)^\top s$ (an LP), then $x_{k+1} = x_k + \frac{2}{k+2}(s_k - x_k)$.

  • No projection, only a linear oracle: your unit-02 simplex method.
  • A free certificate: $g_k = \nabla f(x_k)^\top(x_k - s_k) \ge f(x_k) - f^*$.
  • Rate $O(LD^2/k)$, and no faster in general.

Over a random polytope (n = 30), your simplex method as the oracle cost 2.1 ms per call, HiGHS 1.2 ms, with identical iterates.

the handshake

Frank–Wolfe is where the two halves of this course meet: a continuous loop whose inner step is a combinatorial optimisation. Over matroid, flow or matching polytopes, the oracle is units 13–15.

The measurement is a fair warning. When projection is cheap (a simplex, a box, a ball), projected gradient is simply faster. Frank–Wolfe earns its place where projection is expensive and linear optimisation is cheap: the convex hull of spanning trees, perfect matchings or permutations, where projecting is a hard quadratic problem and the linear oracle is Kruskal, blossom or Hungarian.

"Frank–Wolfe iterates are sparse" is often quoted. On the lab's instance, after 1 000 iterations, it had 116 nonzeros against projected gradient's 107. Sparsity after k iterations is at most k + 1 vertices, which is only a strong statement when k is small relative to n.

29 · KKT

The KKT conditions, and the LP case

$\min f(x)$ s.t. $g_i(x) \le 0$, with $f$, $g_i$ convex and a Slater point. $x^*$ is optimal iff there are $\lambda \ge 0$ with

$$\begin{aligned} &\nabla f(x^*) + \textstyle\sum_i \lambda_i \nabla g_i(x^*) = 0 && \text{stationarity} \\ &g_i(x^*) \le 0,\ \ \lambda_i \ge 0 && \text{feasibility} \\ &\lambda_i\, g_i(x^*) = 0 && \text{complementarity} \end{aligned}$$

Karush (1939); Kuhn & Tucker (1951). With linear constraints no Slater point is needed.

max c·x, Ax ≤ b, x ≥ 0

Multipliers $y \ge 0$ for $Ax \le b$ and $w \ge 0$ for $-x \le 0$. Stationarity: $c = A^\top y - w$, so $w = A^\top y - c \ge 0$: dual feasibility. Complementarity: $y_i (b - Ax)_i = 0$ and $x_j (A^\top y - c)_j = 0$: complementary slackness, exactly unit 03's certificate.

Lab then, HiGHS on an LP with m = 30, n = 50: residuals 3.6e−13, 0, 3.3e−13, 1.8e−13. Unit 03's exact certificate rejects the same pair rounded to 9 decimals: "primal row 12 violated".

in floating point

"Optimal" means "KKT residuals below a tolerance". Every solver has one. An exact certificate needs exact arithmetic, as unit 03 used.

The derivation is the checkpoint, so do it on paper before reading the box. Write max c·x as min −c·x, form the Lagrangian with y for the rows and w for the sign constraints, set the gradient to zero, and read off the conditions. The only extra step is noticing that w is determined by y, so the conditions are about (x, y) alone.

For QPs the same conditions hold with stationarity Qx + q + Aᵀλ = 0. Lab step 5 checks CVXPY's primal and dual against them. Unit 31 differentiates through exactly these equations to get gradients of an argmin.
