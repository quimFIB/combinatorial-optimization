section 1

Convexity

Local information that is global.

29 · convexity

Convex, smooth, strongly convex

convex function

$f(\theta x + (1-\theta) y) \le \theta f(x) + (1-\theta) f(y)$. For differentiable $f$, equivalently

$$f(y) \ge f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top (y - x)$$

The tangent plane is a global under-estimator, so $\nabla f(x) = 0$ means a global minimum.

subgradient

Any $g$ with $f(y) \ge f(x) + g^\top(y - x)$ for all $y$. Unit 11's Lagrangian dual is concave and non-smooth, and subgradient ascent used exactly this.

propertydefinitionfor $\frac12 x^\top Q x$
$L$-smooth$\|\nabla f(x) - \nabla f(y)\| \le L \|x - y\|$$L = \lambda_{\max}(Q)$
$\mu$-strongly convex$f(y) \ge f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top(y-x) + \frac\mu2 \|y-x\|^2$$\mu = \lambda_{\min}(Q)$
condition number$\kappa = L / \mu$eigenvalue spread

$L$ bounds how far a gradient step can safely go (step $1/L$). $\mu$ bounds how flat the bottom can be. Their ratio $\kappa$ decides how many steps you need.

Example: $f = \tfrac12(x^2 + 10y^2)$ has $Q = \mathrm{diag}(1, 10)$, so $L = 10$, $\mu = 1$, $\kappa = 10$: a valley ten times steeper across than along.

Lab step 1 also builds the gradient check that every later unit relies on: central differences, error $O(h^2)$.

What continuity changes, compared with Parts I–V. In a combinatorial problem, local optimality (no improving 2-opt move) says nothing about global optimality. For a convex function it says everything. The whole difficulty of unit 26 disappears, and the question becomes purely about speed.

LPs are convex too, which is why the simplex method's local optimality test (no improving reduced cost) certifies a global optimum.

29 · first-order

Gradient descent, momentum, and the lower bound

methodupdaterate, $L$-smooth convexrate, strongly convex
GD, step $1/L$$x - \frac1L \nabla f(x)$$\frac{L R^2}{2k}$$(1 - \frac1\kappa)^k$
heavy ball (Polyak 1964)$+\ \beta(x_k - x_{k-1})$no general guarantee$(\frac{\sqrt\kappa - 1}{\sqrt\kappa + 1})^k$ on quadratics
Nesterov (1983)gradient at $x_k + \frac{k-1}{k+2}(x_k - x_{k-1})$$\frac{2 L R^2}{(k+1)^2}$$(1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt\kappa})^k$ with constant momentum

$R = \|x_0 - x^*\|$. Heavy ball and Nesterov add momentum; $(1-\frac1\kappa)^k$ is linear convergence. Lab tests check GD's and Nesterov's bounds at every iterate on 12 quadratics.

lower bound (Nemirovski & Yudin 1983; Nesterov)

Any method whose iterates lie in the span of past gradients has, on some $L$-smooth convex function, error $\Omega(L R^2 / k^2)$ for $k \le n/2$. Nesterov's method is optimal up to constants.

heavy ball's catch

Its optimal parameters need $\mu$ and $L$, and outside quadratics it can fail to converge at all (Lessard, Recht & Packard 2016 give a smooth strongly convex example).

explore interactively

The √κ in accelerated rates is the whole story of momentum. With κ = 10 000 and a target of 1e−8 in f, the rates predict about 92 000 iterations for plain GD and about 460 for tuned heavy ball (both printed by then). It measured 542 for heavy ball and over 20 000 for GD.

Stochastic gradients (the ML case) replace ∇f with an unbiased estimate. The rate drops to O(1/√k) for convex f and O(1/k) for strongly convex f with decreasing steps, which is optimal for general stochastic oracles. For finite sums, variance reduction (SAG, SVRG) recovers linear rates. Unit 30's MLP uses mini-batch gradients.

explore interactively

Why the factors. For a diagonal quadratic, one gradient step with step size $t$ multiplies each coordinate by $1 - t\lambda_i$. With $t = 0.1$: $1 - 0.1 = 0.9$ for $x$ and $1 - 1 = 0$ for $y$. With $t = 0.19$: $0.81$ and $1 - 1.9 = -0.9$. Convergence needs every $|1 - t\lambda_i| < 1$, i.e. $t < 2/L$; the best fixed step balances the extreme eigenvalues, $t = 2/(L + \mu)$, giving factor $(\kappa - 1)/(\kappa + 1) = 9/11$ on both.

All iterates are from the reference gradient_descent. The reference nesterov with step 0.1 gives the same first two points and then $(0.709, 0)$ with $f = 0.251$: its momentum has started to push along the flat direction.

Plot: out/convergence.png.

Unit 28's method applies here too. "Heavy ball is fastest" is a claim about tuned parameters on quadratics, and it won't transfer to a problem whose curvature you don't know. The honest summary: with unknown conditioning and cheap gradients, L-BFGS is the default, and momentum methods matter most when gradients are stochastic or memory is scarce, as in deep learning.

Constraints

Project, or ask a linear oracle.

29 · constraints

Projected gradient and Frank–Wolfe

projected gradient

$x_{k+1} = \Pi_C(x_k - \frac1L \nabla f(x_k))$, with projection $\Pi_C$. Same rates as GD, if projecting is cheap. Onto the simplex: sort, prefix sums, a threshold, $O(n \log n)$ (lab step 3).

Frank–Wolfe (1956)

$s_k = \arg\min_{s \in C} \nabla f(x_k)^\top s$ (an LP), then $x_{k+1} = x_k + \frac{2}{k+2}(s_k - x_k)$.

  • No projection, only a linear oracle: your unit-02 simplex method.
  • A free certificate, the Frank–Wolfe gap: $g_k = \nabla f(x_k)^\top(x_k - s_k) \ge f(x_k) - f^*$.
  • Rate $O(LD^2/k)$, and no faster in general.

Lab then, least squares on the simplex, n = 200, f − f*:

Over a random polytope (n = 30), your simplex method as the oracle cost 2.1 ms per call, HiGHS 1.2 ms, with identical iterates.

the handshake

Frank–Wolfe is where the two halves of this course meet: a continuous loop whose inner step is a combinatorial optimisation. Over matroid, flow or matching polytopes, the oracle is units 13–15.

explore interactively

The measurement is a fair warning. When projection is cheap (a simplex, a box, a ball), projected gradient is simply faster. Frank–Wolfe earns its place where projection is expensive and linear optimisation is cheap: the convex hull of spanning trees, perfect matchings or permutations, where projecting is a hard quadratic problem and the linear oracle is Kruskal, blossom or Hungarian.

"Frank–Wolfe iterates are sparse" is often quoted. On the lab's instance, after 1 000 iterations, it had 116 nonzeros against projected gradient's 107. Sparsity after k iterations is at most k + 1 vertices, which is only a strong statement when k is small relative to n.

29 · constraints · example

Projection and Frank–Wolfe, on one point

Find the point of $\{x \ge 0,\ x_1 + x_2 + x_3 \le 1\}$ closest to $p = (0.8,\ 0.6,\ -0.2)$: minimize $f(x) = \tfrac12\|x - p\|^2$.

projection onto the simplex, in one shot

Sorted: $0.8, 0.6, -0.2$. Keep the top two and shift them down equally until they sum to 1: $\theta = (0.8 + 0.6 - 1)/2 = 0.2$.

$p - 0.2 = (0.6,\ 0.4,\ -0.4)$, negatives clipped to 0: $\Pi(p) = (0.6,\ 0.4,\ 0)$, with $f = 0.06$.

Keeping all three would need $\theta = (1.2 - 1)/3 = 0.2/3$ and leave the third coordinate negative, so two is right.

29 · KKT

The KKT conditions, and the LP case

$\min f(x)$ s.t. $g_i(x) \le 0$, with $f$, $g_i$ convex and a Slater point (Slater's condition). $x^*$ is optimal iff there are multipliers $\lambda \ge 0$ with the KKT conditions

$$\begin{aligned} &\nabla f(x^*) + \textstyle\sum_i \lambda_i \nabla g_i(x^*) = 0 \\ &g_i(x^*) \le 0,\ \ \lambda_i \ge 0 \\ &\lambda_i\, g_i(x^*) = 0 \end{aligned}$$

Karush (1939); Kuhn & Tucker (1951). With linear constraints no Slater point is needed. The lines are stationarity, feasibility and complementarity.

the LP case

For $\max c^\top x$, $Ax \le b$, $x \ge 0$: multipliers $y \ge 0$ for $Ax \le b$ and $w \ge 0$ for $-x \le 0$. Stationarity: $c = A^\top y - w$, so $w = A^\top y - c \ge 0$: dual feasibility. Complementarity: $y_i (b - Ax)_i = 0$ and $x_j (A^\top y - c)_j = 0$: complementary slackness, exactly unit 03's certificate.

Lab then, HiGHS on an LP with m = 30, n = 50: residuals 3.6e−13, 0, 3.3e−13, 1.8e−13. Unit 03's exact certificate rejects the same pair rounded to 9 decimals: "primal constraint 12 violated".

in floating point

"Optimal" means "KKT residuals below a tolerance". Every solver has one. An exact certificate needs exact arithmetic, as unit 03 used.

The derivation is the checkpoint, so do it on paper before reading the box. Write max c·x as min −c·x, form the Lagrangian with y for the constraints Ax ≤ b and w for the sign constraints, set the gradient to zero, and read off the conditions. The only extra step is noticing that w is determined by y, so the conditions are about (x, y) alone.

For QPs the same conditions hold with stationarity Qx + q + Aᵀλ = 0. Lab step 5 checks CVXPY's primal and dual against them. Unit 31 differentiates through exactly these equations to get gradients of an argmin.

29 · KKT · example

KKT, on unit 01's polygon

$\max 2x + y$ over $x + y \le 4$, $x - y \le 1$, $x, y \ge 0$. Optimum $(2.5, 1.5)$, value 6.5. Try multipliers $y = (1.5,\ 0.5)$ on the two constraints.

the four checks
conditionon the example
primal feasible$4 \le 4$, $\ 1 \le 1$, $\ x, y \ge 0$✓
stationarity (dual feasible)$1.5\,(1,1) + 0.5\,(1,-1) = (2, 1) = c$✓
complementarity, constraintsboth constraints tight: slack 0 × price✓
complementarity, variables$x, y > 0$ and their reduced costs are 0✓
the same multipliers at a wrong point

At the corner $(1, 0)$: constraint $x + y \le 4$ has slack 3 but price 1.5. Complementarity residual $1.5 \times 3 = 4.5$. Everything else is fine.

So KKT rejects $(1, 0)$ for exactly the reason unit 03's certificate would: a priced constraint with room left.

The multipliers $1.5$ and $0.5$ are unit 02's shadow prices: loosening $x + y \le 4$ by one unit raises the optimum by 1.5. In the continuous world they are called Lagrange multipliers, and they are the same numbers.

Stationarity, written out. The implied multipliers for $x \ge 0$ and $y \ge 0$ are $A^\top y - c = (1.5 + 0.5 - 2,\ 1.5 - 0.5 - 1) = (0, 0)$. They are nonnegative (dual feasible), and they multiply $x = 2.5$ and $y = 1.5$ to zero (complementarity for the variables). The constraints' slacks are $4 - 4 = 0$ and $1 - 1 = 0$, so complementarity for the constraints holds whatever the prices.

The reference lp_kkt_residuals returns all four residuals 0 at $(2.5, 1.5)$, and slack_rows = 4.5 at $(1, 0)$ with the same $y$. This is the checkpoint derivation run on numbers: KKT for an LP is complementary slackness.
