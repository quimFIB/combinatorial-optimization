Column generation, Dantzig–Wolfe, branch-and-price

LPs with more columns than atoms, solved by asking a pricing problem one question at a time. The duals of unit 03 become the objective of a combinatorial subproblem.

A formulation that is strong because it is enormous.

10 · too many columns

Cutting stock, two ways

Cutting stock: cut rolls of width $W$ into $d_i$ pieces of width $w_i$, using as few rolls as possible.

Kantorovich 1939 · compact

$y_k$: roll $k$ used. $x_{ik}$: copies of item $i$ from roll $k$.

$$\begin{aligned} \min\ & \textstyle\sum_k y_k \\ & \textstyle\sum_i w_i x_{ik} \le W y_k \\ & \textstyle\sum_k x_{ik} \ge d_i \end{aligned}$$

LP bound $= \sum_i w_i d_i / W$: the trivial area bound. Rolls are interchangeable, giving $K!$ symmetric copies of every solution.

Gilmore–Gomory 1961 · extended

One variable per pattern $a \in \mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^m$ with $w^\top a \le W$. Rolls of 10, pieces 3, 4, 6: $(3,0,0)$ is three 3s, $(2,1,0)$ is two 3s and a 4.

$$\min \textstyle\sum_p x_p \quad \text{s.t.}\ \sum_p a_{ip} x_p \ge d_i,\ x \ge 0$$

No symmetry. The LP bound is so strong that $\lceil z_{LP} \rceil$ is almost always optimal. But the number of patterns is exponential in $m$.

Lab then, 10 items: Kantorovich LP 108.5; after 30 s HiGHS has no incumbent. Pattern LP 110.71; restricted IP 111 = ⌈LP⌉, optimal.

The pattern LP is the Kantorovich LP with each roll's knapsack constraint replaced by the convex hull of its integer solutions. That is Dantzig–Wolfe in section 2, and it's why the bound is stronger. Stronger formulations by convexifying a substructure is the same idea as unit 05's facility location, done systematically.

The integer round-up property (IRUP): for most cutting-stock instances the optimum is $\lceil z_{LP} \rceil$. Marcotte (1986) found instances where it fails, and the modified conjecture (MIRUP) says the optimum is at most $\lceil z_{LP} \rceil + 1$. It is still open. In the lab's then table, every instance had restricted IP = ⌈LP⌉, which proves optimality on the spot.

10 · too many columns

Pricing is minimising the reduced cost

Revised simplex (unit 02) never looks at a column until it asks whether that column should enter:

$$\bar c_j = c_j - y^\top A_j, \qquad \text{enter } j \text{ if } \bar c_j < 0$$

Dantzig's rule picks the most negative. With columns defined implicitly as the feasible set of a combinatorial problem, "find the most negative" is an optimisation problem:

$$\min_{a \in \mathcal A}\ c(a) - y^\top a$$

Cutting stock: $c(a) = 1$, so maximise $y^\top a$ over patterns, which is a knapsack with the duals as values: the pricing problem of the restricted master.

the checkpoint, in unit 03's words

The restricted master's optimal duals $y$ are feasible for its dual, over the columns it contains. They are optimal for the full dual iff no column violates $y^\top a \le c(a)$. Pricing is the separation problem for the dual. Its objective coefficients are the duals because the dual constraint for column $a$ has coefficients $a$ and right-hand side $c(a)$.

If pricing returns $\bar c \ge 0$, the master's solution is optimal for the full LP: complementary slackness holds for every column, present or not.

This is the slide to be able to reproduce. Master: $\min c^\top x$, $Ax \ge d$, $x \ge 0$. Dual: $\max d^\top y$, $A^\top y \le c$, $y \ge 0$. The restricted master has a subset of columns, so its dual has a subset of constraints: a relaxation of the full dual. Its optimal $y$ may violate the missing constraints. Finding a violated one means finding a column with $c(a) - y^\top a < 0$. That's unit 09's separation, applied to the dual, and the equivalence of separation and optimisation (via the ellipsoid method) says the full LP is polynomial iff pricing is.

Why the duals are the "values" in the knapsack: $y_i$ is the marginal rate at which the master's objective falls per unit of demand $i$ covered. A pattern is worth adding if the demand it covers is worth more than the roll it costs.

10 · too many columns

The loop

The master grows by one column (or a few) per iteration. The pricing problem never sees the master's size.

  • The master value never increases: an added column can only help.
  • Finite: each column enters at most once (with care over degeneracy), and there are finitely many.
  • Warm starts: add the column to HiGHS's model and re-solve from the previous basis. It's one primal simplex pivot in the common case.
  • Multiple columns per iteration: return every negative reduced-cost column pricing found (lab step 6 returns up to 10).
  • Heuristic pricing is allowed until the last iteration. Only an exact "nothing negative" proves optimality.

Lab step 3's test checks the value against the LP over all patterns: 25 instances, exact agreement.

The lab rebuilds the master from scratch each iteration for simplicity. A real implementation keeps the HiGHS model and calls addCol, and HiGHS's primal simplex warm-starts. Unit 03 measured warm re-solves at about one pivot versus three or more cold. Here the difference is a constant factor per iteration.

Why heuristic pricing is fine: any column with negative reduced cost is a valid improving column. Early iterations often use a greedy pricer and switch to the exact DP only when the heuristic fails. For NP-hard pricers (ESPPRC), this is essential.

section 2

Dantzig–Wolfe

Where the columns come from, and what bound they give.

10 · Dantzig–Wolfe

Decomposition of block-angular programs

Linking constraints over independent blocks (block-angular structure):

$$\begin{aligned} \min\ & \textstyle\sum_k c_k^\top x_k \\ & \textstyle\sum_k D_k x_k \ge d && \text{(linking)} \\ & x_k \in X_k && \text{(block } k\text{)} \end{aligned}$$

Convexification: replace each $X_k$ by $\mathrm{conv}(X_k)$, written as a convex combination of its extreme points $\{x_k^p\}$ (Minkowski–Weyl, unit 01):

$$x_k = \textstyle\sum_p \lambda_{kp} x_k^p, \quad \sum_p \lambda_{kp} = 1, \quad \lambda \ge 0$$
master and pricing

The Dantzig–Wolfe master: linking constraints plus one convexity constraint per block, over the $\lambda$ columns. Pricing for block $k$, with linking duals $y$ and convexity dual $\mu_k$:

$$\min_{x \in X_k}\ (c_k - D_k^\top y)^\top x - \mu_k$$
the bound (Geoffrion 1974)

$z_{DW} = \min\{c^\top x : Dx \ge d,\ x_k \in \mathrm{conv}(X_k)\} \ge z_{LP}$, with equality whenever each block's LP relaxation is already integral (the integrality property). This is the Lagrangian dual bound of unit 11.

Cutting stock is the case of identical blocks (every roll is the same knapsack), so the blocks aggregate into one pricing problem, and the convexity constraints combine into the objective "number of rolls". That aggregation is also what removes the $K!$ symmetry.

The integrality property is the key design question. If the pricing problem is an LP with integral vertices (a shortest path without resources, an assignment), the DW bound equals the compact LP bound. You gain nothing in bound, though perhaps something in speed. The bound improves exactly when pricing is a hard integer problem, like a knapsack or a resource-constrained path. Hence the next slide.

10 · Dantzig–Wolfe

A hard pricing problem is a feature

why it is fine
  • The hardness buys the bound. Geoffrion says a pricer with the integrality property gives no improvement.
  • Pricers are small: one roll, one vehicle, one crew member. Exponential in the size of one block is often cheap.
  • Many are weakly NP-hard, like knapsack, which is pseudo-polynomial (unit 16). Or they have strong DP structure, like labelling for paths with resources.
  • Heuristic pricing does most of the work, and exact pricing only certifies the end.
when it is not fine

ESPPRC, pricing for vehicle routing, is strongly NP-hard (Dror 1994). Lab then: roughly ×10–30 pricing time per four more customers, from 0.09 s at $n = 12$ to 27.8 s at $n = 20$.

So production codes relax the pricing problem. They allow non-elementary routes, or ng-routes (Baldacci, Mingozzi & Roberti 2011), which forbid only cycles within small neighbourhoods. That gives a weaker bound with a far cheaper pricer, and cuts recover the strength.

The design trade-off is a dial between two extremes. Pricing over $\mathrm{conv}(X_k)$ exactly gives the strongest bound and the hardest pricer. Pricing over a relaxation $\mathrm{conv}(X'_k) \supseteq \mathrm{conv}(X_k)$ gives a weaker bound and an easier pricer. ng-routes sit at a tuned point on that dial, and for CVRP they come close to the elementary bound at a fraction of the cost.

10 · bounds and stability

A lower bound at every iteration

Lagrangian bound (Lasdon 1970)

If some optimal solution has $\sum_p x_p \le \kappa$, then

$$z_{RMP} + \kappa \cdot \min_a \bar c(a)\ \le\ z_{LP} \le z_{RMP}$$
Farley 1990 · unit costs

For any $y \ge 0$ with $v = \max_a y^\top a$, $\ y / v$ is dual feasible, so

$$z_{LP} \ge d^\top y \,/\, v$$

At the master duals, that is $z_{RMP} / v$ (lab step 2). On the small instance: 3.44, 3.57, 4.

why: notes (N)

Lab then: early termination when $\lceil\text{bound}\rceil = \lceil z_{RMP}\rceil$, since the integer bound is then known. On the small instance, iteration 1 already has $\lceil 3.57 \rceil = 4$ but $\lceil 4.17 \rceil = 5$, so it can't stop yet.

The Farley argument in full. The dual of the full master is $\max d^\top y$ subject to $y^\top a \le 1$ for all patterns and $y \ge 0$. For any $y \ge 0$, the scaled point $y/v$ satisfies every constraint by the definition of $v$, so weak duality gives the bound. No optimality of $y$ is needed. That's what lets lab step 5 compute bounds at the smoothed points, which aren't master duals at all.

The bound is not monotone in the iterations, which is why the lab records the best so far. The tests check that the recorded sequence never decreases and ends equal to the LP value. At the last iteration $v \le 1$, so the bound is at least $d^\top y$, which is the master value.

10 · bounds and stability

Dual oscillation, and smoothing

The restricted master is highly degenerate: many dual optima. Its duals jump between extreme points of the dual face, far from the full dual optimum. Pricing at a wild dual returns a column that is useless later. The tail of the iteration count is mostly this oscillation.

stabilisation, three ways
  • Boxstep (Marsten, Hogan & Blankenship 1975): constrain $y$ to a box around a centre.
  • Penalties (du Merle et al. 1999): penalise leaving the box, with surplus columns in the primal.
  • Smoothing (Wentges 1997): price at $\alpha \hat y + (1 - \alpha) y$ around a stability centre $\hat y$, and fall back on a mis-price. No change to the master at all.

explore interactively

Why the mis-price fallback is necessary: a column that prices negatively at the smoothed point may have nonnegative reduced cost at the master duals. It wouldn't enter the basis, and the loop would stall. Only pricing at the master duals can certify optimality, so every termination decision is made there.

Pessoa, Sadykov, Uchoa and Vanderbeck (2018) made the choice of α automatic, adjusting it from the angle between the subgradient and the direction to the centre. Their VRPSolver uses that. The lab's fixed α = 0.8 is the textbook default.

10 · branch-and-price

Why naive branching destroys pricing

branch on a master variable

Down branch $x_p \le \lfloor x_p^* \rfloor$, typically $x_p = 0$. The pricing problem doesn't know pattern $p$ is forbidden. Its optimum is that same pattern, so it regenerates it forever. Fixing that needs the $k$-th best pattern, with $k$ growing with depth.

Also unbalanced: $x_p = 0$ removes one of exponentially many columns and barely moves the bound.

Price-and-branch (the lab's restricted IP) sidesteps this with no guarantee. Lab then: exact for cutting stock, and a 10% gap for CVRP at $n = 12$ and $20$.

branch on the original structure

Ryan–Foster 1981 (set partitioning, bin packing): pick items $i, j$ that are fractionally together. One branch says always together (merge them in pricing), the other never together (a conflict constraint in pricing). The pricer changes shape slightly but stays a knapsack-like problem.

VRP: branch on arcs or edges of the original graph (forbid, or force, arc $u \to v$) or on the number of vehicles. Both are easy to impose in labelling.

Vanderbeck (2000, 2011) gives general branching schemes for identical blocks, where aggregation hides which block a column belongs to.

The principle: branch on something the pricing problem can see. Original variables (arcs, item pairs, assignments) are visible to pricing, and master variables are not. Branching on original variables also produces balanced trees, since each decision removes a constant fraction of the columns.

Why the restricted IP gap for CVRP is large: columns generated at the LP optimum are those with small reduced cost there. They cover the fractional solution well, but rarely contain a complete integer partition of low cost. Adding columns during branching fixes that, and so does a heuristic column pool.

Bin packing: three items of size 4, bins of size 10. Any two share a bin, all three don't.

the root LP

Patterns $\{1,2\}$, $\{1,3\}$, $\{2,3\}$ at $\tfrac12$ each. Every item is covered exactly once, using 1.5 bins. The optimum is 2.

10 · branch-and-price

Vehicle routing: where column generation stops being a toy

Set partitioning over routes $r$ for the CVRP, with $\alpha_{vr} = 1$ if $r$ visits $v$:

$$\min \textstyle\sum_r c_r x_r \quad \text{s.t.}\ \sum_r \alpha_{vr} x_r = 1\ \forall v,\ x \in \{0, 1\}$$

Pricing: an elementary depot-to-depot path within capacity, with arc costs $c_{uv} - \pi_v$. Negative cycles are everywhere, which is why elementarity must be enforced.

Labelling (lab step 6): labels (cost, load, visited set) per vertex, extended forward, with dominance pruning. Bidirectional labelling and completion bounds are the next speed-ups.

Branch-price-and-cut with ng-routes, rounded capacity and limited-memory subset-row cuts, bidirectional labelling, and route enumeration. It solves CVRP instances with several hundred customers to optimality (Pessoa et al., VRPSolver 2020).

The dominance rule is subtle, and lab step 6 tests it deliberately. A label dominates another only if its visited set is a subset. Otherwise the dominated label might still reach a customer that the dominator has already used. Dropping the subset condition makes the pricer return routes that miss the true minimum, and the tests with high duals and large capacity catch it.

Why the set-partitioning LP bound is strong: it convexifies over entire routes, so capacity and elementarity are enforced inside each column. Compact CVRP formulations (two-index with rounded capacity cuts) have bounds several percent weaker, and that difference determines whether an instance is solvable at all.

A model answer to the checkpoint. The master's dual has one constraint per column, $y^\top a \le c(a)$. The restricted master's duals satisfy those for the columns present. Complementary slackness makes them optimal for the full LP iff they satisfy all of them. Checking that means computing $\min_a c(a) - y^\top a$ over all feasible columns. That expression is linear in $a$ with coefficients $-y$ plus the column's own cost, so the duals are the pricing problem's objective coefficients.
