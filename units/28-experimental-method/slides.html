Arithmetic, geometric, shifted geometric

Two solvers, ten instances:

9 instances1 instancearith. meangeo. meanSGM, shift 10
A1 s each1 000 s100.92.07.3
B2 s each100 s11.83.05.0

The arithmetic mean is one instance's number. The geometric mean treats 1 s → 2 s like 500 s → 1 000 s: ratios, which is what comparisons are about. The shift keeps trivial instances from dominating.

PAR-2 on the example, with a 300 s limit: A's 1 000 s run becomes 600 s, and A's arithmetic mean drops from 100.9 to 60.9. The limit and $k$ moved the number, not the solver.

$$\mathrm{SGM}_s(t) = \exp\Big(\tfrac1n \textstyle\sum_i \log(t_i + s)\Big) - s$$
timeouts

An unsolved run has no time, only a lower bound. PAR-k counts it as $k \times$ the limit. The result depends on the limit and on $k$, so report both, and report the solved count separately.

censoring, in this course

Unit 19 reported a mean of 153.6 nodes with a 3 000-node cap. Three capped runs contributed 90 of it: without them the mean is 65.6. A higher cap would give a higher mean. The number described the cap as much as the solver.

SCIP and most MIP benchmark reports use shift 10 s (Achterberg 2007). For sub-second runs, pick a shift on their scale: the lab's audit uses 0.1 s.

The numbers in the table: A's geometric mean is 1000^(1/10) ≈ 2.0 and B's is 2^(9/10) · 100^(1/10) ≈ 3.0. With shift 10, A is exp((9 log 11 + log 1010)/10) − 10 ≈ 7.3 and B is exp((9 log 12 + log 110)/10) − 10 ≈ 5.0 (all checked by computation). A wins the geometric mean, and B wins the other two. None of them is wrong, and each answers a different question. Pick it before looking at the data.

The PAR-2 line: with a 300 s limit A's slow instance is unsolved and counts $2 \times 300 = 600$, so A's mean is $(9 \cdot 1 + 600)/10 = 60.9$; B's 100 s instance is solved and its mean stays 11.8. With a limit of 2 000 s it would be solved and count 1 000. Report the limit, $k$ and the solved counts.

For node counts, shifted geometric means work the same way. This unit's audit uses shift 10 nodes.

28 · pictures

Performance profiles (Dolan & Moré 2002)

For problem $p$ and solver $s$, the performance ratio $r_{p,s} = t_{p,s} / \min_{s'} t_{p,s'}$, or $\infty$ if $s$ failed. The performance profile:

$$\rho_s(\tau) = \tfrac{1}{|P|}\,\big|\{p : r_{p,s} \le \tau\}\big|$$
  • $\rho_s(1)$: the share of problems $s$ wins (ties count for everyone).
  • $\rho_s(\tau)$ for large $\tau$: the share $s$ solves at all.
  • A curve that starts high is fast; one that ends high is robust. They can cross.
a known trap (reordering)

Ratios are to the best solver in the comparison. Adding or removing a solver can reorder the others' curves (Gould & Scott 2016). Compare two at a time when the question is two-way.

virtual best solver

Per problem, the fastest solver's time. It's the ceiling for any portfolio of these solvers, and the gap to it says how much choosing per instance could buy. Here pseudocost is the virtual best on 22 of 24.

explore interactively

Time-to-target plots (unit 26) are the per-instance analogue for randomised algorithms: a distribution over seeds on one instance, where a profile is a distribution over instances. Use TTT plots when the question is "how long until good enough, and how reliably"; use profiles when it's "across a benchmark set, how often and by how much".

Gould and Scott's note ("A note on performance profiles for benchmarking software", ACM TOMS 2016) shows the reordering effect concretely. The fix is simple: keep pairwise profiles for pairwise claims.

28 · inference

Seeds, pairing, intervals, and many tests

  • Seeds. A deterministic MIP solver still varies with seeds and model permutations (unit 27). Run several, and treat (instance, seed) as the unit of observation.
  • Pairing. Two solvers on the same instances: analyse the per-instance log ratios, not two independent means. Most of the variance is between instances, and pairing removes it.
  • Bootstrap. Resample the per-instance ratios, recompute the geometric mean each time, and take percentiles. No normality assumption.
$$\text{ratio} = \exp\Big(\tfrac1n\textstyle\sum_i \log\frac{a_i + s}{b_i + s}\Big)$$
Wilcoxon signed-rank

Paired, rank-based: do the differences tend to be positive? Its p-value answers "is there a difference", and the bootstrap interval answers "how big" (the effect size). Report both.

multiple comparisons

Three solvers mean three pairwise tests. At α = 0.05 each, the chance of at least one false positive is about 14% if nothing differs. Holm: sort the p-values, multiply the $k$-th smallest by $m - k + 1$, and keep them monotone. Uniformly more powerful than Bonferroni.

Holm (1979), Scand. J. Statistics 6.

explore interactively

What the bootstrap interval is and isn't: an estimate of how much the summary would move if the instances were drawn again from the same distribution. It says nothing about instances from a different distribution. That's instance selection, the next slide's topic, and no statistics can fix it.

Why 14%: 1 − 0.95³ ≈ 0.14 for three independent tests. Pairwise tests on shared data aren't independent, but Holm's guarantee doesn't need independence.

explore interactively

The resamples are random.Random(1).choices(values, k=5) five times; the reference bootstrap_ci with those five resamples returns (0.76, 0.84), the smallest and fourth of the sorted means. Five resamples are only for showing the mechanics: the interval from 2 000 resamples, same seed, is (0.68, 0.98). In practice resample log ratios, as the lab's geometric_ratio_ci does, so that 0.5× and 2× are symmetric.

Holm's adjusted values are the reference holm([0.01, 0.04, 0.03]) = [0.03, 0.06, 0.06], in the original order. Reading it: sort the p-values, multiply the smallest by the number of tests, the next by one fewer, and so on, then carry the running maximum.

28 · inference

Instance selection, per-instance claims, negative results

instance selection bias
  • Instances chosen because the new method does well on them (or generated by a generator tuned during development).
  • Instances "too easy" or "too hard" dropped after seeing results.
  • One family standing in for a problem: unit 27's triplets and uniform instances disagreed about arc flow.

Fix the instance set before running, and say how it was chosen.

per-instance or aggregate?

"Faster on average" (an aggregate claim) and "faster on most instances" (a per-instance claim) are different. The audit of unit 07 reports both: pseudocost has the lower SGM and wins 22 of 24.

a negative result worth reading

State the hypothesis, the instances and budget, the effect size with its interval, and what it rules out. "Restarts don't reliably change the median on QCP(12, 55%): 3/8 instances worse, p = 0.79, and the original instance's intervals overlap" rules out a specific claim, which is useful.

Hooker's 1995 paper ("Testing heuristics: we have it all wrong") argued for exactly this: experiments designed to test hypotheses about why an algorithm works, rather than competitions. Thirty years on it's still the right criticism of much of the heuristics literature.

verdict: survives, now quantified

What changed besides the verdict: the claim is now per-instance as well as aggregate, it has an effect size with an interval, and it's on 24 instances chosen by a rule (all seeds 0–7 at three sizes) before running. The node saving of strong branching is also much smaller than the three-instance totals suggested, where one instance dominated.

28 · audit

Unit 19: restarts, with intervals and eight more instances

Claims (one instance, 100 seeds, 3 000-node cap): restarts cut the mean from 153.6 to 52.1, and make the median worse, 31 → 49.5.

strategycappedmean [95% CI]median [95% CI]
first-fail3153.6 [66.6, 267.5]31.0 [28.5, 35.0]
Luby restarts052.1 [45.0, 59.7]49.5 [28.5, 53.5]

Eight more instances, 30 seeds each: restarts lower the mean on 7/8 (p = 0.03) and raise the median on 3/8 (p = 0.79).

verdict: the median claim is withdrawn

The intervals overlap on the original instance, and the family shows no consistent direction. Unit 19's lab sheet and slides have been corrected in place.

what survives

Restarts cut the tail. On the two instances where first-fail hit the cap 6 times in 30, restarts never did (means 1 067 → 194 and 1 141 → 191).

How the wrong claim arose: a difference in medians on one instance, from one set of seeds, with a mechanism that sounded right ("every run pays for aborted starts"). A possible reason it didn't generalise, untested here: the first Luby restart comes after 30 nodes, close to a typical run's median, so aborted starts may touch the median only on some instances. A plausible story plus one number is the pattern to distrust.

The mean claim is also restated. The mean of capped counts isn't a property of the solver alone, so the surviving claim is about the tail (capped runs and upper quantiles), not about "the mean".
