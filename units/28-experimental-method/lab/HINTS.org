One rung at a time; then ~solution/lab.py~ or ~solution/functional.py~.

* Step 1 — The run store

** Rung 1
~CREATE TABLE IF NOT EXISTS runs (...)~ with ~PRIMARY KEY (experiment, solver, instance, seed)~, so
that ~INSERT OR REPLACE~ overwrites a re-run instead of duplicating it. Use ~?~ placeholders, never
string formatting for values, and ~conn.commit()~ after writes, or a crash loses the batch.

** Rung 2 — why SQLite
A CSV is fine until you want "the median time of solver X on instances Y, seeds 0–29, excluding
timeouts". Then it's a query. The primary key also makes resuming trivial: skip what's already there.

** Functional route
SQLite is state by design, so there's no functional store. What the reference does is build every row and
query as a value first: the insert is one ~tuple(run[f] for f in FIELDS)~, the optional solver filter chooses
between two ~(where, args)~ pairs, and loading is ~[dict(zip(FIELDS, row)) for row in rows]~.

* Step 2 — A resumable runner

** Rung 1
Load the existing keys once into a set of ~(solver, instance, seed)~. Loop instance → solver → seed,
in that order, so a crash mid-benchmark leaves every solver with the same instances done. Call
~clock()~ right before and right after the solver, and nothing else in between.

** Rung 2 — timeouts you can't enforce
A Python function can't be killed cleanly from inside the process. The harness trusts solvers to
respect ~timeout~, and /records/ any overrun as a timeout at exactly ~timeout~ seconds, so an overrun
can't win a comparison. For hard enforcement, run each solver in a subprocess.

** Functional route
Compute the work as data before doing any: ~have~ is a frozenset of stored keys, and ~todo~ is a comprehension
over instances × solvers × seeds minus ~have~. Then ~sum(perform(*job) for job in todo)~ runs them, where
~perform~ times one call, builds the row as a dict and saves it. The effects are the solver calls and the
saves; the plan is a value.

* Step 3 — Shifted geometric means

** Rung 1
~math.exp(mean(log(v + shift))) - shift~. The shift stops tiny times from dominating: on a
geometric mean, 0.01 s against 0.02 s counts as much as 100 s against 200 s. Solver papers use shift
10 s for MIP, but pick it for the time scale at hand: ~then~ uses 0.1 s for sub-second runs.

** Rung 2 — why not the arithmetic mean
One instance at 1 000 s and nine at 1 s gives a mean of 100.9 s, which describes no instance. Ratios
between solvers, which is what people actually compare, are multiplicative, and the geometric mean
respects that.

** Functional route
Comprehensions, and ~tz.valmap(sgm of the PAR seconds, tz.groupby("solver", runs))~ for the per-solver table.

* Step 4 — Performance profiles and the virtual best

** Rung 1 — the ratio table
Group runs by ~(instance, seed)~. For each group, the best solved time, then each solver's ratio to
it (infinity if unsolved or if nobody solved). ρ_s(τ) is the fraction of problems with ratio ≤ τ.
At τ = 1 it's the share of wins (ties count for everyone), and for large τ it's the share solved.

** Rung 2 — reading one
A curve that starts high wins often, and one that ends high solves a lot. They're different
questions, and a solver can lead on one and trail on the other. The virtual best solver is the
lower envelope: the most any portfolio of these solvers could achieve.

** Functional route
Group once into problems with ~tz.valmap(..., tz.groupby(lambda r: (r["instance"], r["seed"]), runs))~. A
profile is then a ratio function per (problem, solver) and a count per τ. The virtual best is ~tz.valmap~ of
"fastest solved run" over the same grouping.

* Step 5 — Bootstrap intervals

** Rung 1
~rng.choices(values, k=len(values))~ is a resample with replacement. Compute the statistic of each,
sort, and take the elements at the two quantile indices. The spec gives the exact index formula so the
tests can reproduce your interval with the same random stream.

** Rung 2 — paired ratios
Don't bootstrap each solver's geometric mean separately: the instances are shared, and that pairing
is most of the information. Bootstrap the per-instance log ratios, then exponentiate the interval.

** Functional route
The bootstrap is one sorted generator of statistics over resamples, plus an index function for the two
quantiles. The paired ratio maps pairs to log ratios and reuses it.

* Step 6 — Many comparisons

** Rung 1 — Holm
Sort p ascending. The k-th smallest (k = 1 … m) is multiplied by m − k + 1, and a running maximum keeps
adjusted values in the same order as the raw ones. Cap at 1, then put everything back in the
original order.

** Rung 2 — Wilcoxon signed-rank
Drop zero differences. Rank |d| with average ranks for ties, sum the ranks of positive and negative
differences, and T is the smaller sum. Under the null, mean n(n+1)/4 and variance
n(n+1)(2n+1)/24 − Σ(t³ − t)/48. With the continuity correction, p = erfc(z/√2).

** Functional route
Holm's step-down is an ~accumulate(..., max)~ over the multiplied p-values in sorted order, then ~sorted(zip(order,
stepped))~ to put them back. Wilcoxon groups the differences by absolute value with ~itertools.groupby~ after
sorting, takes group start offsets with ~accumulate(..., initial=0)~ for the average ranks, and sums the signed
ranks. The tie correction is one more sum over the groups.
