SAT and the CDCL engine

Unit propagation, conflict analysis, backjumping, activity, restarts — resolution with a very good heuristic, and a proof system with limits you can state precisely.

needs 17~1½ h slides + 3½ h lablab: uv run co lab 20

SAT solvers went from toy to industrial in about five years, around 1996–2001: GRASP (clause learning), Chaff (watched literals, VSIDS), then MiniSat's clean re-implementation. They now handle hardware verification instances with millions of variables, and they're the engine inside CP-SAT (unit 21), SMT solvers and many planners.

The unit has two halves that belong together. How CDCL works: every component the lab builds. What CDCL can't do: pigeonhole, and the resolution lower bound behind it. The second half is what turns "SAT solvers are magic" into a precise statement.

section 1

From DPLL to CDCL

Branch, propagate, and remember why you failed.

20 · DPLL

CNF, resolution, and unit propagation

CNF: a conjunction of clauses, each a disjunction of literals. Satisfiability is NP-complete (Cook 1971); 3-SAT already is.

resolution $$\frac{(C \lor x) \qquad (D \lor \lnot x)}{C \lor D}$$

Sound and refutation-complete: $F$ is unsatisfiable iff the empty clause can be derived.

DPLL (Davis, Logemann & Loveland 1962): branch on a variable, apply unit propagation (a clause with all literals false but one forces it), and backtrack chronologically on a conflict.

two watched literals (Chaff 2001)

Watch two non-false literals per clause. A clause needs attention only when a watched literal becomes false: find a replacement, or the clause is unit or conflicting.

Backtracking costs nothing: unassigning variables never invalidates a watch. No counters to restore.

Lab step 1's test: on 40 random formulas with random decisions, the watch loop reaches exactly the assignments and conflicts of naive propagation.

Propagation takes 80–90% of a modern solver's time, which is why its data structure matters so much. Before watched literals, solvers kept counters of false literals per clause and had to update them on every assignment and every backtrack. Watched literals made the backtrack free and most assignments cheap. That's the imperative core the functional reference keeps as it is.

Learning

A conflict is a lesson; write it down.

20 · learning

The implication graph and the first UIP

Lab step 2's textbook example. Clauses (¬x₁∨x₃), (¬x₂∨¬x₃∨x₄), (¬x₂∨¬x₄∨x₅), (¬x₄∨¬x₅). Decisions x₁, then x₂.

Implication graph: a node per assigned literal, with edges from the reason clause's other literals. A conflict is a clause with every literal false.

first unique implication point

A UIP is a current-level node on every path from the decision to the conflict. The first UIP is the one closest to the conflict.

Here x₄ is not a UIP: x₂ → x₅ bypasses it. The first UIP is x₂, and the learned clause is ¬x₂ ∨ ¬x₃: backjump to level 1 and assert ¬x₂.

Computing it is resolution: resolve the conflict clause with the reason of the latest current-level literal until one current-level literal remains.

Resolution trace for the example. Conflict (¬x₄∨¬x₅). Resolve with x₅'s reason (¬x₂∨¬x₄∨x₅) to get (¬x₂∨¬x₄): two current-level literals, x₂ and x₄. Resolve with x₄'s reason (¬x₂∨¬x₃∨x₄) to get (¬x₂∨¬x₃): one current-level literal (x₂), stop. The functional reference writes exactly this unfold. The imperative MiniSat loop computes the same thing with a counter and a seen-array.

Why first UIP and not the decision (all-decision learning) or a later UIP: Zhang, Madigan, Moskewicz & Malik (2001) compared schemes, and 1UIP gave the shortest clauses that assert the most. It has been the default ever since.

20 · learning

The rest of the engine

  • Non-chronological backjumping: jump to the second-highest level in the learned clause, where it becomes unit. It can skip many levels of irrelevant decisions.
  • VSIDS (Chaff): bump the activity of variables in each conflict, and grow the bump by 1/decay after every conflict, so recent conflicts weigh exponentially more. Branch on the most active.
  • Phase saving (Pipatsrisawat & Darwiche 2007): re-assign a variable its last value, so backjumps don't discard satisfied sub-problems.
  • Restarts (unit 19's Luby, or adaptive): cheap, because learned clauses and activities survive.
clause deletion by LBD (Glucose, 2009)

Literal block distance: the number of distinct decision levels in a learned clause. "Glue" clauses (LBD ≤ 2) link two blocks of propagation and are kept forever. Periodically delete the worse half of the rest, never a clause that is a current reason.

The same conflicts, 30–50× the time per conflict: the algorithm is right, the language is slow.

Why VSIDS works is still partly empirical. The best-supported explanation (Liang et al. 2015) is that it focuses search on the "bridge" variables of the formula's community structure, which is why it does well on industrial instances with modular structure and less well on random ones.

Proofs and limits

"Unsatisfiable" should come with evidence.

20 · proofs

DRAT: checkable unsatisfiability

A DRAT proof lists every learned clause (and every deletion) in order. A checker replays it: each lemma must be RUP, meaning that assigning its negation and unit-propagating over the clauses so far gives a conflict. It ends with the empty clause.

  • A first-UIP clause is always RUP: that's what analysis just showed.
  • RAT extends RUP to cover inprocessing techniques that add non-implied clauses (such as blocked clauses).
  • SAT competitions require verified proofs; drat-trim and the formally verified cake_lpr check them.

Lab then, proofs from your solver, checked by colib.drat (an independent RUP checker):

The tests also check 30 proofs, with aggressive deletions included. A solver bug that learns a wrong clause fails the check, even if the final answer happens to be right.

Proofs matter because solvers have bugs, and "UNSAT" is otherwise unfalsifiable. The Boolean Pythagorean triples problem (Heule, Kullmann & Marek 2016) was settled with a 200 TB DRAT proof, later compressed and verified. That's the course's certificate theme from unit 00, at scale.

20 · limits

CDCL is resolution, and pigeonhole defeats resolution

Pipatsrisawat & Darwiche 2011; Atserias, Fichte & Thurley 2011

CDCL with restarts polynomially simulates general resolution, and every CDCL run yields a resolution refutation of similar size. So a resolution lower bound is a CDCL lower bound.

Haken 1985

Every resolution refutation of the pigeonhole formula $\mathrm{PHP}^{n+1}_n$ has size $2^{\Omega(n)}$.

Counting ("$n+1$ objects don't fit in $n$ places") can't be expressed compactly in clause-by-clause reasoning. Unit 18's alldifferent does it in one matching.

Lab then, conflicts:

Better engineering shifts the curve; it doesn't change its shape. Stronger proof systems (cutting planes, extended resolution) have short pigeonhole proofs, and pseudo-Boolean solvers exploit that.

The intuition behind Haken's bound, via Ben-Sasson & Wigderson's width method (2001): any refutation must contain a clause of large width, about $n/3$ literals, and a wide clause forces exponential size. Pigeonhole's clauses mention one pigeon or one hole each, but refuting them requires reasoning about many pigeons at once, and resolution can only do that by building wide clauses one resolution at a time.

That's the checkpoint answer in one sentence: CDCL is a resolution proof search, pigeonhole's resolution refutations are exponentially long, so any CDCL run on it takes exponentially many conflicts, whatever the heuristic.

section 4

Encodings

The same constraint, very different CNFs.

20 · encodings

Tseitin, at-most-one, cardinality

  • Tseitin (1968): name every subformula with a fresh variable. Linear size, equisatisfiable. Arbitrary circuits become CNF.
  • At most one of $n$: pairwise ($\binom n2$ clauses), sequential counter (Sinz 2005, $3n - 4$ clauses, $n - 1$ variables), bitwise ($n \lceil\log n\rceil$ clauses), commander, product.
  • At most $k$: sequential counters, sorting and cardinality networks (Asín et al. 2011), totalizers.

Arc consistency is the property that matters: does unit propagation on the encoding prune as much as the constraint would? Pairwise and the sequential counter both have it.

Lab then, n-queens with your solver (clauses / seconds):

npairwisesequentialbitwise
40103 520 / 0.3318 292 / 3.8235 584 / 0.88
60352 880 / 0.9741 832 / 20.983 604 / 10.5

The largest CNF is the fastest. Pairwise propagates in one step. The counter needs chains of auxiliary variables, which also distract the branching heuristic. Fewer clauses is not the objective; propagation strength and a branching-friendly variable set are.

The bitwise encoding is not arc consistent in general: fixing one literal true propagates the bits, and the bits falsify the others, so it works in that direction. But fixing all but one literal false doesn't force the last one true through the bits. For at-most-one that's harmless. For exactly-one, the extra at-least-one clause does the work.

At industrial scale, 10⁵-literal cardinality constraints make pairwise impossible, and the choice between counters and networks becomes a real trade-off. It's also where pseudo-Boolean and CP-SAT's native linear constraints (unit 21) take over.
