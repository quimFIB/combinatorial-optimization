Variables, domains, constraints — and no relaxation. Remove what cannot be part of a solution, search over what remains, undo, repeat.

section 1

The formalism

Feasibility first; an objective, if any, is just another constraint.

17 · formalism

Constraint satisfaction problems

CSP

Variables $x_1, \dots, x_n$, finite domains $D_i$, and constraints $C_j \subseteq D_{i_1} \times \cdots \times D_{i_k}$ over subsets of the variables. A solution assigns every variable a value from its domain so that every constraint holds.

  • Constraints are arbitrary relations: "≠", "all different", "this table of allowed tuples", "count of 3s equals $x_3$". No linearity and no convexity.
  • Often no objective at all. With one, minimise by adding "objective < best" after each solution and continuing.
  • Deciding satisfiability is NP-complete: 3-colouring is a CSP.

Neither dominates, which is why unit 21's hybrid exists.

A concrete contrast. Scheduling jobs on machines with "no two overlap" is a disjunction: MIP needs a big-M constraint per pair (unit 05 showed how weak those are), while CP has a dedicated disjunctive propagator (unit 18) that reasons about intervals directly. The opposite case is a knapsack: its LP bound is excellent, while CP's bound-based propagation on a weighted sum sees very little until most variables are fixed.

section 2

Consistency

How much local reasoning, at what price.

17 · consistency

A ladder of consistency levels

  • Node: every value satisfies the unary constraints.
  • Arc (AC): for each binary constraint on $(x, y)$, every $a \in D_x$ has a support $b \in D_y$.
  • Path: every consistent pair extends to any third variable.
  • $k$-consistency: every consistent assignment to $k - 1$ variables extends to any $k$-th. Strong $n$-consistency means backtrack-free search.

Higher levels prune more and cost exponentially more: $k$-consistency takes $O(n^k d^k)$ time.

algorithmtimeidea
AC-3 (Mackworth 1977)$O(e d^3)$queue of arcs; revise
AC-4 (Mohr & Henderson 1986)$O(e d^2)$support counters
AC-2001 (Bessière & Régin)$O(e d^2)$AC-3 plus "last support" pointers
path consistency$O(n^3 d^3)$rarely used
Freuder 1982

If the constraint graph is a tree, arc consistency alone makes search backtrack-free. More generally, width $w$ plus strong $(w+1)$-consistency suffices, which is unit 16's treewidth again.

$e$ is the number of constraints and $d$ the largest domain. AC-3's $O(ed^3)$: each arc is re-queued at most $d$ times (once per value removed from its target), and each revise costs $O(d^2)$. AC-4 precomputes, for every value, the list of values it supports, and decrements counters. That's optimal in time but expensive in memory. AC-2001 keeps AC-3's simplicity and reaches AC-4's bound by remembering, for each value, where its last support was found.

In practice nobody enforces path consistency. Real engines stop at arc consistency (or the cheaper bounds consistency) for most constraints, and put the extra strength into global constraints (unit 18), which is where it pays.

reading the trace

Revise 1: $x = 3$ has no $y$ above it: gone. Revise 2: $y = 1$ has no $x$ below it: gone. Because $y$ shrank, the arc that reads $y$ to prune $x$ goes back on the queue.

Revise 5 uses the news from revise 3 ($y = 2$): $x = 2$ lost its support.

After 7 revise calls the queue is empty: $x = 1$, $y = 2$, $z = 3$, solved without search.

This constraint graph is a path, a tree, so Freuder's theorem already promised no search would be needed.

The push rule. When revise changes $D_x$, every arc $(w, x)$ that reads $x$ is rechecked, because $w$'s supports may have lived in the values just removed. In this run arc $(z, y)$ was still in the queue when revise 2 changed $y$, so only $(x, y)$ was pushed.

17 · consistency

Arc consistent, yet unsatisfiable

Every value has a support on every edge, so nothing is pruned. No 2-colouring exists.

Arc consistency is local. It checks one constraint at a time and never looks at a cycle. Each propagator is sound (it never removes a solution), and the fixpoint is sound, but it is not complete.

4×4 sudoku, from the lab

1 . . .
. . 3 .
. 4 . .
. . . 2

133 NotEqual propagations reach a fixpoint where every cell is a singleton: solved without search. Inkala's 9×9 "hardest sudoku" needs 3 598 nodes.

Search fills the gap between "consistent" and "solved". Stronger propagation shrinks the tree, at a price per node.

explore interactively

Tracing the 4×4 by hand, which is the checkpoint: start with the givens as singletons. Each NotEqual with a fixed side removes that value from the other cell. Cell (0, 1) loses 1 from its row and 4 from its column, leaving {2, 3}. Cell (0, 2) loses 1 (row) and 3 (column), leaving {2, 4}. Keep a queue of cells that became singletons and push their values into their 7 peers. The order doesn't change the fixpoint, only the number of propagations it takes to reach it. That confluence is what lab step 3's random test checks against naive sweeps.

The same confluence argument needs monotone propagators: a smaller input domain never yields a larger output. All the lab's propagators are monotone.

17 · consistency

Bounds consistency vs domain consistency

domain (generalised arc) consistency

Every value in every domain belongs to some satisfying tuple of the constraint.

bounds consistency

Only the min and max of each domain need supports, often with the others relaxed to intervals. Interior holes are ignored.

On a linear constraint $\sum a_i x_i \le b$, reasoning from bounds costs $O(n)$. Domain consistency on an equality $\sum a_i x_i = b$ is NP-hard: it contains subset sum.

Lab step 3's LinearLe removes every value $a$ of $x_i$ with

$$a_i\, a > b - \sum_{j \ne i} \min_{v \in D_j} a_j v$$

For a single inequality this is exactly domain consistency, since the others' best values are always available. The test checks it against brute-force supports. LinearEq, as two inequalities, is only bounds-like, and the test only asks it to be sound.

The general design question of Part IV: how strong a propagator, for what cost per call?

The magic series model shows why weak propagation plus redundant (implied) constraints is a common engineering pattern. The Count constraints alone already define the problem. Adding $\sum_i s_i = n$ and $\sum_i i\, s_i = n$ changes nothing semantically, but gives the bound reasoning something to bite on. Lab test: with them, $n = 10$ takes 14 nodes; without the second, 32.

17 · consistency · example

One equation with a hole

$x + y = 6$, with $D_x = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$ and $D_y = \{1, 2, 4, 5\}$: $y$ has a hole at 3.

bounds reasoning, as the lab's LinearEq

$x \le 6 - \min y = 5$ and $x \ge 6 - \max y = 1$. Same for $y$. Nothing is removed.

domain consistency
$x$12345
needs $y =$5 ✓4 ✓3 ✗2 ✓1 ✓

$x = 3$ has no support: domain consistency removes it. Bounds consistency can't see a hole in the middle.

an inequality is different

Same domains, $x + y \le 3$. Each variable's best partner is the other's minimum, 1, so $x \le 2$ and $y \le 2$: $D_x = \{1,2\}$, $D_y = \{1,2\}$.

Every remaining value has a support (its partner at 1), so this is already domain consistent: for one inequality, bounds reasoning is exact.

For the equation, checking every value means solving "is there $y$ with $a + y = 6$?"; with many variables that question is subset sum, hence NP-hard.

Both runs are the reference propagators: LinearEq([x, y], [1, 1], 6).prune returns no narrowing, and LinearLe([x, y], [1, 1], 3).prune returns $x, y \in \{1, 2\}$.

Why the hole matters in practice: holes appear as soon as search removes a value ($x \ne 3$ branches) or another constraint prunes an interior value. A bounds propagator then wakes up less often and infers less, which is the price of its $O(n)$ cost.

section 3

The engine

Propagators, a queue, and a trail.

17 · engine

Propagators and the fixpoint

A propagator for constraint $C$ is a function $p : D \to D$ with

  • contracting: $p(D) \subseteq D$;
  • monotone: $D \subseteq D'$ implies $p(D) \subseteq p(D')$;
  • sound: it never removes a solution of $C$, and it rejects any fully fixed assignment that violates $C$.

Run all propagators until none changes anything. The result is the greatest common fixpoint, independent of the order (Apt 1999).

the lab's engine
  • prune(dom) is pure: it reads domains and returns narrowings. Only the engine writes.
  • Watch lists: when a variable changes, wake only the propagators on it.
  • A propagator that narrows its own variables is re-queued: LinearEq and Count aren't idempotent.
  • Every call counts as one propagation.

Real engines add priorities (cheap propagators first), events (fixed / bounds changed / any change), and idempotence flags (Schulte & Stuckey 2008).

Why pure prune functions are a good teaching choice. They make each propagator testable in isolation against brute-force supports, which is how the lab tests them. They make the functional reference natural. And they separate the two design questions: what a propagator infers, and when the engine runs it.

The cost: a real propagator often wants incremental state between calls (unit 18's alldifferent keeps its matching). Unit 18 handles that by recomputing, and notes where incrementality would matter.

17 · engine

Trailing, copying, or recomputing

Search needs the domains of the parent node back when a branch fails. Three options (Schulte 1999):

  • Trailing: log each change on a trail and undo back to a mark. Cheap when few domains change per node. Used by most CP systems, and by the lab's imperative engine.
  • Copying: snapshot the whole store at each node. Simple, and it suits parallel search. Used by Gecode.
  • Recomputation: store only the decisions and replay them. It trades time for memory.
the functional reference

Immutable domain tuples passed down the recursion: copying, with structural sharing. No trail, because backtracking is returning. The same node and propagation counts as the trailing engine, 3–10× slower in Python.

Trailing is what in-place mutation costs you: every write must be undoable. Copying is what immutability costs you: every write allocates.

SAT solvers (unit 20) use the extreme form of trailing: the trail is the list of assigned literals in order, and backjumping pops it. The idea is the same, specialised to Boolean domains where a change is always "fixed".

Pop the remaining two: $y = 123$, $x = 123$. Empty trail, original domains.

Undo pops in reverse order, so $y$ is restored to 23 before being restored to 123. Search needs only the marks: "the branch $y = 2$ failed" is undo(m₁), then try $y \ne 2$.

The trail holds 4 entries for 2 decisions. A copying engine would instead keep 3 full snapshots of 3 domains. With thousands of variables and a handful of changes per node, trailing wins by a wide margin, which is why most CP systems use it.

section 4

Search

Propagate, branch, repeat.

17 · search

The CP loop, and its two numbers

The CP search loop:

  1. Propagate to a fixpoint. If a domain is empty: failure, backtrack.
  2. If every variable is fixed: a solution.
  3. Choose a variable (first-fail: smallest domain) and a value $a$. Branch $x = a$, then $x \ne a$.

"Fail first": pick the variable most likely to fail, so dead ends are found near the root, where they cut the most.

the diagnostic vocabulary

Nodes measure search effort; a better heuristic or stronger propagation reduces them. Propagations measure inference effort; stronger propagation increases them per node. Units 18–21 each move one against the other.

Why the lab uses binary branching ($x = a$ / $x \ne a$) rather than $d$-way branching (one child per value): after $x \ne a$ the engine propagates the removal, which can fix other variables or fail immediately. $d$-way branching never gets that inference. It's also the form SAT solvers use, which makes unit 21's merger natural.

CP-SAT's "branches" and "conflicts" are not the lab's nodes and failures. CP-SAT branches on Boolean literals of an encoding (unit 21) and counts a conflict each time clause learning runs. The comparison is of the shape of the effort, not a one-to-one count.
